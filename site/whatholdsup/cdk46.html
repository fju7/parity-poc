The Category Difference

whatholdsup.org

Three drugs of the same class treat the same disease. A guideline grades one of them above the other two, and says why. No trial has ever tested one against another. The two studies that compare them by other means find nothing to separate them — and on the endpoint the trials were actually designed to measure, the three are indistinguishable.

What the guideline says

And the reason it gives.

The NCCN breast cancer guidelines, version 6.2026, cover first-line treatment of hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer. For an aromatase inhibitor combined with a CDK4/6 inhibitor, the guideline assigns ribociclib category 1. The same combination with abemaciclib or palbociclib is category 2A.

Category 1 means high-level evidence and uniform panel consensus. Category 2A means lower-level evidence with uniform consensus. The guideline states the basis for the difference: the overall survival benefit seen with ribociclib.

The distinction is specific to that setting, and it is worth being precise about. In combination with fulvestrant rather than an aromatase inhibitor, both ribociclib and abemaciclib are category 1, and palbociclib is not. So the claim is narrow: first-line, with an aromatase inhibitor, ribociclib alone carries category 1.

The guideline also records that the three drugs have not been directly compared in clinical trials.

The four trials

Each tested one drug against endocrine therapy alone. None tested one against another. Every figure is the trial publication’s own and every cell names the analysis it comes from. These trials have each reported several times; a table that says only “the latest” invites the reader to compare one trial’s first readout against another’s fourth.

On the endpoint every one of these trials was powered to measure, the three drugs are indistinguishable. Progression-free survival hazard ratios of 0.54, 0.55, 0.57 and 0.58 — all four trials, every one of them significant. A spread of four hundredths across the class. Nor does the choice of readout rescue a difference: take any published analysis of any of these four trials and its progression-free hazard ratio falls between 0.52 and 0.58.

The grade turns instead on overall survival, which in these trials was a secondary endpoint.

The distance that separates a category 1 from a category 2A Abemaciclib’s hazard ratio is 0.80, on an interval running 0.64 to 1.02. Ribociclib’s is 0.76, on 0.63 to 0.93. The point estimates are four hundredths apart — the same spread the whole class shows on progression-free survival. The intervals overlap along almost their entire length: 97% of ribociclib’s interval lies inside abemaciclib’s. That figure is ours, not a published one, and it is one subtraction, on the same rounded bounds shown above: ribociclib’s interval runs 0.63 to 0.93, a width of 0.30; the part of it inside abemaciclib’s runs 0.64 to 0.93, a width of 0.29. 0.29 ÷ 0.30 = 96.7%. On the unrounded published bounds (0.637, 1.015) it is 97.7%. We print 97%.

One interval stopped at 0.93 and the other at 1.02, and that is the whole of it: ribociclib’s result reached statistical significance (two-sided p = 0.008) and abemaciclib’s did not (P = .0664). The threshold is the conventional one and the guideline applied it correctly. What separates a category 1 from a category 2A here is nine hundredths in where an upper bound landed, on point estimates four hundredths apart.

Whether that should carry a category difference is the question this piece puts. It is not a question about the arithmetic, which is right, or about the classification, which follows the rules. It is a question about what a category is for — whether it should record which trial cleared a threshold, or what the evidence says about the drug.

Palbociclib is a different case, and the difference is real. A hazard ratio of 0.96 on an interval from 0.78 to 1.18 is not a near miss; it is a null result. PALOMA-2 is also the trial whose investigators recorded a large and disproportionate imbalance in missing survival data between its arms — survival status was unknown for 13.3% of the palbociclib arm and 21.2% of the placebo arm — which they said limits interpretation. They then did something about it. A sensitivity analysis using recovered data cut the unknowns to 9.2% and 11.7%, and the result did not move: HR 0.92 (0.76–1.12). The defect is real, and the investigators’ own remedy points where their headline number already pointed. A different number, from a trial with a defect its own authors flagged, is still not a measurement of a different drug — but this piece is not claiming palbociclib’s result sits where abemaciclib’s does. It does not.

What this comparison cannot show Overlapping intervals do not prove two effects are equal, any more than one interval crossing the no-effect line proves two effects differ. What can honestly be said is narrower: these trials provide no evidence of a difference between the drugs, because they never compared them.

What happens when you do compare them

Twice, by different methods, on different data.

No randomised trial has tested one of these drugs against another. Two studies have compared them anyway.

A network meta-analysis (Scientific Reports, February 2024) pooled seven phase III randomised trials, 4,415 patients, at a median follow-up of 73.3 months. It found no statistically significant difference in overall survival between any pair of the three. It was published before MONARCH 3’s final overall-survival analysis and used the interim figure, HR 0.754, in place of the final HR 0.804 given above. The direction of its finding does not depend on that one input, but the input is out of date, and we would rather say so than have it found.

A real-world weighted comparison (Flatiron Health cohort, 2025) using a large oncology records cohort, with stabilised inverse-probability weighting, reported ribociclib versus palbociclib at an adjusted hazard ratio of 0.98 (P = 0.75), abemaciclib versus palbociclib at 0.95 (P = 0.43), and abemaciclib versus ribociclib at 0.97 (P = 0.70).

Neither is a randomised head-to-head trial, and neither should be read as one. What they are is the best comparative evidence in existence, arrived at by two different methods from two different bodies of data, and they agree with each other: no separation between the three.

They do not contradict the guideline’s own statement, and it is worth saying so plainly. In guideline language, “directly compared in clinical trials” means a randomised head-to-head trial, and none exists. The real-world study says as much itself: it opens by describing its own purpose as working in the absence of randomised trials that directly compare the three. The guideline is accurate as written. What these two comparisons add is not a correction to it, but the only evidence there is on the question it records as untested.

Where the three genuinely differ

One documented difference, and it does not point the way the grade does.

Ribociclib is the only one of the three whose labelling requires cardiac monitoring — electrocardiograms before treatment and during it. That is a real, documented difference between the drugs, and it is a burden rather than a benefit.

This piece is not arguing that the three drugs are identical. It is arguing that the evidence does not establish that one of them works better than another, while the one clear difference between them runs the other way from the grade.

What is established, and what is not

As of this draft.

Established

  • All three drugs substantially improve progression-free survival against endocrine therapy alone, at hazard ratios of 0.54, 0.55, 0.57 and 0.58, every one of them statistically significant.
  • Ribociclib plus letrozole improved overall survival against letrozole alone: 63.9 versus 51.4 months, HR 0.76 (0.63–0.93), two-sided p = 0.008, at 80 months of median follow-up. This is the one first-line overall-survival result in the class that reached significance.
  • Abemaciclib plus a non-steroidal aromatase inhibitor did not: 66.8 versus 53.7 months, HR 0.80 (0.64–1.02), P = .0664, at 8.1 years of median follow-up. The point estimate favours abemaciclib; the interval crosses the no-effect line.
  • Palbociclib plus letrozole did not: 53.9 versus 51.2 months, HR 0.96 (0.78–1.18). A sensitivity analysis recovering missing survival data did not change it, HR 0.92 (0.76–1.12).
  • The guideline assigns ribociclib category 1 first-line with an aromatase inhibitor, and abemaciclib and palbociclib category 2A, on the stated basis of that survival result.
  • No randomised trial has compared any of the three against another.
  • The two studies that compare them by other means find no significant difference between any pair.
  • Ribociclib alone carries a cardiac monitoring requirement.

Not established

  • That any one of these drugs is more effective than another. Nothing has tested it.
  • That the category difference predicts any difference in outcome for a patient.
  • That it reflects anything about the drugs beyond which trial’s secondary endpoint crossed a significance threshold.
  • That the divergent overall-survival results reflect a property of the molecules rather than of the trials — their populations, their follow-up, their power and, in one case, their missing data.
  • What a head-to-head trial would find. Nobody has run one, and on current incentives nobody will.

What a reader is owed

The part of this that is not academic.

If you are taking one of these drugs, or choosing between them, the question underneath all of this is whether a category 2A drug is a worse drug. On the evidence: the category reflects which trial produced a survival result, not which drug produces better survival.

That is a statement about evidence and not a recommendation. This assessment does not say what anyone should take. Prescribing decisions turn on toxicity, monitoring burden, comorbidities, interactions and cost, none of which a page like this can see, and all of which your oncologist can.
