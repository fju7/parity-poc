Fourteen deaths, and an interval too wide to read.
What Holds Up Issue one · 28 Aug 2026

On 19 August, Merck and Moderna announced that a large Phase 3 trial of a personalised mRNA cancer therapy met its primary endpoint in melanoma, and a key secondary endpoint as well. Several outlets called it a landmark. On the evidence so far it may well be.

The announcement establishes that the effect is real and statistically significant. It does not say how large. Both were met — the primary endpoint and the key secondary — and that is a finding in its own right, from 1,137 patients in a double-blind trial: it establishes the direction. What is missing is the size — no hazard ratio, no interval, no absolute rates. The companies say those will come at a medical meeting, which is normal. The figures in the headlines, a 49% reduction in the hazard of recurrence or death and 59% in the hazard of distant metastasis or death, come from a different study: a randomised but open-label trial of 157 patients, whose five-year results were presented at ASCO in June. Those are numbers with five years behind them. They are just not this trial’s, and 157 patients in an open-label trial is not 1,137 in a double-blind one.

Survival is the question that stays open. In that earlier trial, five-year overall survival — an exploratory endpoint the trial was never powered to test — rests on fourteen deaths in total — seven in each arm.
Fourteen deaths. For overall survival, the hazard ratio is 0.471 and the interval runs from 0.165 to 1.345 — compatible with the treated group’s hazard of death being about 35% higher than the comparison group’s, and with it being roughly 84% lower. The point estimate favours benefit, but the interval reaches into harm. On this exploratory analysis alone, neither the size of the effect nor the direction is established with any statistical confidence.

None of this means the treatment does not work. It probably does. It means the public has not been shown how large this trial’s effect is.

What the full assessment adds

Every figure above was verified against a company release or a peer-reviewed paper before publication, and the draft was checked twice. Before publishing we read the specialist coverage of the announcement, and it got the attribution right: the 49% and 59% belong to the earlier trial, and most said plainly that the Phase 3 numbers had not been released.

What the full assessment adds is the layer underneath. Why figures from a smaller trial cannot be read as the size of a blinded trial’s effect. Both trials leave the recurrence call to the investigator; the difference is that in the Phase 3 those investigators do not know who got what. In an open-label trial scan timing and ambiguous calls can tilt a result without anyone intending it, which is why the blinded trial’s own numbers are the ones that matter. What fourteen deaths can establish about direction, and what they cannot establish about magnitude. And what the absolute numbers look like beside the relative ones.

It also sets out a question with two defensible answers: the earlier trial enrolled stage IIIB–IV, while the Phase 3 reaches down into stage IIB. Whether that lowers the benefit those patients can expect is genuinely disputed, and the assessment gives both sides.
