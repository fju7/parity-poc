What Holds Up Issue two · 28 Aug 2026

Three drugs of the same class treat the same disease. A guideline lists all three as preferred, and grades the evidence behind one of them higher than the evidence behind the other two. That grade gets read as a ranking of the drugs. It is not one — and the first version of this assessment read it that way too.

The NCCN breast cancer guidelines, version 6.2026, cover first-line treatment of hormone-receptor-positive, HER2-negative advanced breast cancer. Combined with an aromatase inhibitor, ribociclib is category 1. The same combination with abemaciclib or palbociclib is category 2A. The guideline states the reason: the overall survival benefit seen with ribociclib.

That is one of two systems NCCN runs, and it is not the one that says which drug to prefer. The categories of evidence and consensus — the 1 and the 2A — record how strong the evidence is that a treatment is appropriate. A separate set, the categories of preference, ranks regimens against one another. On that second system all three aromatase-inhibitor-plus-CDK4/6-inhibitor combinations are preferred first-line options. The preference designation does not differ across the three. Only the category of evidence does.

The version of this assessment published on 28 August did not say that, and this email is going out because of it. The page reported the category difference, asked what a reader should make of it, and never mentioned that the guideline had already answered the question elsewhere in the same document. A reader would reasonably have concluded the guideline ranked one drug above the other two. It does not. The page was corrected on 29 August 2026 and the full account is in the correction history at its foot.

That leaves the more useful question, which is what the evidence behind the grade actually shows.

Three trials sit behind that grade — MONARCH 3 for abemaciclib, MONALEESA-2 for ribociclib, PALOMA-2 for palbociclib. Each tested one drug against endocrine therapy alone. None tested one against another.

On the endpoint every one of them was powered to measure, nothing separates them. Progression-free survival hazard ratios of 0.54, 0.57 and 0.58, each from the analysis named on the page. Across the eight investigator-assessed readouts these trials have published, every hazard ratio falls between 0.52 and 0.58. That is a claim about the eight we checked, not about every analysis ever run on these datasets.

The grade turns instead on overall survival, and the three trials did not design that analysis the same way.

Abemaciclib’s hazard ratio for death is 0.804, on an interval running 0.637 to 1.015. Ribociclib’s is 0.76, on 0.63 to 0.93. The point estimates are four hundredths apart.

One interval stopped at 0.93 and the other at 1.015. That is the difference between a significant result and a non-significant one, and it is what separates the two evidence grades.

Overlapping intervals do not prove two effects are equal, and this piece does not claim they do. Nor does the guideline claim the drugs differ. What the category difference rests on is where one interval stopped, and the assessment shows the arithmetic in full — including that abemaciclib’s interval is the wider one, which is our own observation and not a finding we can attribute to anyone else. What Tanguy and colleagues did establish, in npj Breast Cancer in 2018 and before any of these trials had mature survival data, is that MONARCH 3 had less statistical power than the other trials to reach significance on survival at all — and that significance appearing in some trials and not others might reflect chance rather than a real difference between the drugs.

Palbociclib is a different case, and the difference is real. A hazard ratio of 0.96 on an interval from 0.78 to 1.18 is not a near miss. What PALOMA-2 established is that it did not demonstrate a survival benefit — which is less than saying the drug does not work, and the assessment is careful about the difference. This piece does not claim palbociclib’s result sits where abemaciclib’s does. It does not.

The same drug, graded both ways

Change the partner drug and the grade changes. With fulvestrant instead of an aromatase inhibitor, ribociclib and abemaciclib are both category 1 — and the guideline gives the same reason it gives for the first-line grade: those combinations showed an overall survival benefit, in MONALEESA-3 and MONARCH 2.

So abemaciclib is a category 1 drug in one setting and a category 2A drug in another, on one standard, consistently applied.

The grade is not a verdict on the molecule. It is a record of which of that molecule’s trials produced a significant survival result. The guideline says so plainly, and the assessment takes it at its word.

What the full assessment adds

Several research programmes have compared all three drugs anyway. The assessment examines four — a network meta-analysis of seven randomised trials; P-VERIFY, a US real-world cohort of 9,146 patients; PALMARES-2, an Italian study of 1,982; and an indirect comparison that rebuilt patient-level data from three of the trials. Four studies are not the whole literature, and the assessment says so: an outside reviewer found two more that we had missed.

On palbociclib they contradict each other. P-VERIFY reported survival and progression in two separate papers from the same cohort, and neither found any separation. PALMARES-2 finds abemaciclib and ribociclib each ahead of palbociclib on progression-free survival, on intervals that exclude the no-effect line — abemaciclib’s by 0.08 and ribociclib’s by 0.05, which is significant but not comfortably so. The assessment reports that conflict rather than resolving it, and records one fact a reader weighing the two is entitled to have: P-VERIFY’s full name is the Palbociclib Verifying Evidence of Real-world Impact study, and its own poster states that it was funded by Pfizer, which makes palbociclib. That does not make its findings wrong, the assessment draws no inference from it, and it says in the same breath that PALMARES-2’s own declaration of interests is behind a wall we could not open — so neither study is offered as the disinterested one.

On the one comparison the category difference is actually about — abemaciclib against ribociclib — none of the four separates them. P-VERIFY puts it at 0.97 on 0.82 to 1.14 for survival. PALMARES-2, the study that did separate palbociclib, puts it at 0.91 on 0.73 to 1.14 for progression. The reconstructed patient-data comparison comes closest, at 0.722 on 0.520 to 1.002, p = 0.051 — and still does not reach significance. Eleven thousand patients, two endpoints, and not one of those four studies can tell abemaciclib and ribociclib apart. The guideline grades the evidence behind them apart, which is a different statement, and the whole point.

The assessment also sets out what each drug actually costs a patient, from the labels. Ribociclib requires cardiac monitoring. Abemaciclib carries a labelled thrombosis warning and causes diarrhoea in 81 to 90 percent of patients. Palbociclib causes grade 3 or worse neutropenia in 66% of PALOMA-2 patients, per the label. Three different burdens, and none of them is what the category measures.

And it records what we tested and could not stand up: three separate criticisms of the guideline, all three withdrawn before publication — and a fourth, ours, that a reader had to catch after publication.
