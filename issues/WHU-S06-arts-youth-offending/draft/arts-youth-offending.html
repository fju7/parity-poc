Arts Programmes and Youth Offending

whatholdsup.org

For children and young people who are at risk of offending, or who have already offended, do arts programmes reduce offending or improve their behaviour and wellbeing? A 2024 systematic review of the question found that none of its 43 included studies reported offending as an outcome. What the evidence holds is young people’s own accounts of feeling better, and a handful of small studies whose numbers could not be pooled.

The short answer

There is not yet evidence that arts programmes reduce offending by children and young people who are at risk of offending or who have offended.

A 2024 Campbell systematic review by Mansfield and colleagues found insufficient evidence from quantitative studies to support or refute the effectiveness of arts interventions for this group, for any outcome.

None of the studies it included reported offending as an outcome.

That is an absence of evidence, not evidence that the programmes fail.

What the review looked at

The review searched bibliographic databases, trial registries and grey literature up to January 2023, without language restrictions.

It included 43 studies of children and young people aged 8 to 25: 3 quantitative, 38 qualitative and 2 mixed methods.

Five of them, with 304 participants between them, went into the quantitative synthesis.

Three of those five were conducted in the UK and two in the USA, in settings ranging from a Young Offenders Institution to an urban classroom.

In the qualitative studies, music, on its own or with other art forms, was the most commonly reported intervention.

Behaviour and wellbeing

The authors could not run their planned meta-analysis, because there was not adequate quantitative data.

For every comparison and every outcome, it was not possible to pool data from more than one study, and every observed effect was rated very low certainty.

On behaviour, one study in a Young Offenders Institution reported incidents only for the 14 participants who completed it.

In the 3 months after that programme there were 3 incidents in the music group, 4 in the sculpture group and 5 in the control group, and the data were not complete enough to calculate an effect size.

A second study found no clear evidence of an effect on peer relations, with a mean difference of −3.53 (95% CI −8.72 to 1.66) among 11 participants.

Where wellbeing could be estimated, as self-esteem, locus of control or self-concept, the review found no clear evidence of an effect.

The review downgraded these estimates for serious study limitations and for imprecision.

The review’s authors asked four study teams for data they had not reported, and did not receive it.

What young people say

The qualitative studies suggested that arts interventions may lead to positive emotions, the development of a sense of self, engagement in creative practice and positive personal relationships.

The review judged its confidence in those findings very low, because of methodological limitations.

The Youth Endowment Fund’s Toolkit, summarising the same review, reports that some participants described feelings of hope, self-confidence, courage, pride and gratitude.

The Toolkit says there is insufficient evidence to calculate an impact rating for arts programmes on reducing violence.

The study with a comparison group

One of the review’s included studies, of a music programme run by Birmingham’s youth offending team, compared participants’ attendance with a matched comparison group.

Attendance data covered 42 participants and 145 children in the comparison group, and participants who completed the programme were statistically more likely to attend their youth offending team appointments.

Participants’ self-reported well-being rose significantly over the course of the project, but that was a before-and-after measure in participants alone; the comparison group was used only for attendance.

Improvements in attitudes and behaviour were positive but not statistically significant.

Children were referred by their caseworkers rather than at random, and the authors note that some selection bias may exist.

The study did not measure reoffending, and its authors name reoffending among the longer-term outcomes future research should look at.

The review could not use its well-being results, because they were not reported in a form that allowed an effect size to be calculated.

What is being tested now

A randomised controlled trial is testing SHIFT, an 18-week one-to-one music mentoring programme, with young people aged 11 to 17 in East Sussex, West Sussex and Brighton and Hove.

Its primary outcome is self-reported delinquent behaviour six months after randomisation, and wellbeing is among its secondary outcomes.

The registry record gives enrolment from 1 October 2024 to 26 June 2026 and a completion date of 27 November 2026, and posts no results.

Even when it reports, its main measure will be what young people say about their own behaviour, not recorded offending.

What holds up

Young people who take part in arts programmes often describe them as positive, and one comparison-group study found better attendance at youth offending team appointments.

Whether arts programmes reduce offending is unknown: no study in the review measured it.

The Toolkit’s own advice is to consider arts programmes as a way to engage children in support services, and to link them with interventions that are supported by evidence.
