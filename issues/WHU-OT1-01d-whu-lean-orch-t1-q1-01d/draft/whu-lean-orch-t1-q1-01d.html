Mobile tracking with health-worker prompts and patient reminders in primary care: what holds up

The question

The question is: “In primary health care, does using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improve the care people receive and their health?”

The short answer

A Cochrane review of digital tracking combined with decision support tools, targeted client communication, or both found that, compared with usual care, the results are mixed [1]. Its authors say these approaches may lead to benefits in some situations but may make little or no difference in others [1]. They add that the full impact is often unknown because there are not enough studies, or the studies do not provide clear answers [1].

What the reviews found

The combined approach: tracking plus prompts and/or reminders

The review examined whether primary care and community health workers using mobile devices to track patients through their electronic health records give better quality of care [1]. It included trials of digital tracking on mobile devices combined with electronic clinical decision support tools, with targeted client communication such as SMS messages, or with both [1]. Comparisons were with traditional methods, such as paper records or no tools at all [1]. The review included 18 studies, and 17 of them were from low- and middle-income settings where electronic medical record systems are rare or just being rolled out [1]. Most studies focused on health workers using mobile devices, particularly for maternal and child health or for managing chronic diseases [1]. The review states that these tools may have some benefits [1]. For tracking combined with both decision support and targeted client communication, compared with standard care, patient or client acceptability and satisfaction was assessed in one study with 3324 participants, rated as moderate-certainty evidence [1]. For that same comparison, no study assessed resource use or unintended consequences [1]. According to the source, digital tracking may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use [1]. According to the source, these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies [1]. According to the source, "We are uncertain of the effect of this intervention on providers' adherence to recommended practice due to the very low certainty evidence (2 studies, 185 participants)" [2]. According to the source, "We are uncertain about the effectiveness of mobile phone‐based decision‐support tools on several outcomes, including adherence to recommended practice" [2]. According to the source, none of the studies had a quality of care framework and focused only on specific health areas [2]. The source states: "We need well-designed research that takes a systems lens to assess these issues" [2]. According to the source, "The effect of TCCMD for most outcomes is uncertain" [3]. According to the source, there may be improvements for some outcomes using targeted communication, but these findings were of low certainty [3]. According to the source, high-quality, adequately powered trials and cost-effectiveness analyses are required to reliably ascertain the effects and relative benefits of TCCMD [3]. The source states that future studies should measure potential unintended consequences, such as partner violence or breaches of confidentiality [3]. According to the source, TCCMD may modestly increase in service attendance overall (RR: 1.12, 95% CI 0.92‐1.35, low certainty evidence), however the confidence interval encompasses benefit and harm [4]. According to the source, TCCMD (targeted client communication via mobile devices) may increase service utilisation overall (RR: 1.71, 95% CI 0.67‐4.38, low certainty evidence), however the confidence interval encompasses benefit and harm and there was considerable heterogeneity (I² = 72%), with mixed results according to type of service utilisation (STI/HIV testing, and VMMC) [4]. According to the source, TCCMD may improve some outcomes but the evidence is of low certainty [4]. According to the source, "The effect on most outcomes is uncertain/unknown due to very low certainty evidence or lack of evidence" [4]. According to the source, high quality, adequately powered trials and cost effectiveness analyses are required to reliably ascertain the effects and relative benefits of TCC delivered by mobile devices [4]. The source states that, "Given the sensitivity and stigma associated with sexual and reproductive health future studies should measure unintended consequences, such as partner violence or breaches of confidentiality" [4].

On-screen decision support on mobile devices, studied on its own

A separate Cochrane review of mobile decision-support tools in primary care found eight studies, three in the USA and five in India, China, Guatemala, Ghana and Kenya [2]. Its authors do not know whether these tools make health workers better at following recommended practice, because the evidence was of very low quality [2]. They found no clear pattern of a positive or negative effect on patients' behaviour or health, although the tools may slightly improve patients' satisfaction with medical information [2]. In one study of 2086 people at high cardiovascular risk, mobile decision support probably increased the number taking antihypertensive medication [2]. In that same study, it probably made little or no difference to smoking or systolic blood pressure, and it may have increased aspirin use [2]. No studies in this review measured time to appropriate management, health worker satisfaction, resource use or unintended consequences [2].

Reminder messages to patients, studied on their own

A review of targeted messages to pregnant women and parents found that some messages may improve some people's health and use of services, but others may make little or no difference [3]. That review describes the existing evidence as mostly of low or very low certainty [3]. Compared with no messages, more parents may take their children to services such as vaccination appointments [3]. Whether the messages improve children's health is unknown because the evidence is missing [3]. Pregnant women living with HIV who received messages may attend slightly more antenatal care appointments [3]. The messages may make little or no difference to whether these women and their babies follow ARV treatment according to plan [3].

Who gets left out, and how patients feel

In a review of text-message trials for HIV care, one trial in Mozambique excluded 345 of 1202 people assessed because they were illiterate, had no phone, or both [4]. Because only one trial stated that it provided phones, the review authors judge it likely that the other trials excluded people without a personal mobile phone [4]. A qualitative synthesis found that many clients like receiving messages from health services by mobile phone [5]. The same synthesis found that some clients have problems receiving messages because of lack of network access, internet or a phone, or because of language, reading or privacy issues [5]. Some participants felt reminders were not necessary and could be detrimental to patient independence [5]. Tracking combined with communication on mobile devices may increase antenatal appointment attendance (OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.80 to 2.96), but this is low-certainty evidence and the confidence interval includes both benefit and harm [3]. The intervention may increase skilled attendants at birth where lack of them is common (differing by urban/rural residence), but may make no difference where almost all women already have one (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.34 to 2.94; low-certainty evidence) [3]. The review found uncertain effects on maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity because the certainty of the evidence was very low [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices, compared with non-digital communication such as pamphlets, may have little or no effect on exclusive breastfeeding (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.07; low-certainty evidence) [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may reduce any maternal health problem (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.79) and any newborn health problem (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.06) up to 10 days postpartum, but the evidence is low-certainty and the newborn interval includes both benefit and harm [3]. The effect of digital tracking with decision support and/or client reminders on health service use is unknown because no studies were found [3]. For use of health services, effects on skilled attendance at birth are uncertain (very low-certainty evidence), and the intervention may make little or no difference to antenatal influenza vaccination attendance (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.71 to 1.58; low-certainty evidence), with the CI including both benefit and harm [3]. For pregnant and postpartum women living with HIV, mobile-device tracking combined with client communication (TCCMD) may make little or no difference to maternal and infant adherence to antiretroviral therapy compared with standard care (low-certainty evidence) [3]. Targeted client communication reminders sent by mobile phone may slightly increase antenatal care use (mean difference 1.5, 95% CI -0.36 to 3.36), but the evidence is low-certainty and the interval includes no effect [3]. The effect of digital tracking with decision support and/or client messaging on the proportion of births occurring in a health facility is uncertain, because the evidence is of very low certainty [3]. For health and well-being outcomes, the intervention's effect on neonatal death or stillbirth and on infant HIV is uncertain, because the evidence is of very low certainty [3]. No studies reported on maternal mortality or morbidity, so the effect of these mobile tracking and prompt or reminder interventions on those outcomes is unknown [3]. The effect of digital targeted client communication via mobile devices compared with non-digital targeted client communication is unknown, because no studies reported this comparison [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may increase infant ARV/prevention of mother-to-child transmission treatment adherence compared with digital non-targeted communication (RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.48; low-certainty evidence) [3]. For parents of children under five years, no studies reported on correct treatment, nutritional, or health outcomes [3]. Based on 10 trials, mobile tracking of clients' care with decision support or reminders (TCCMD) may modestly increase health service use such as vaccinations and HIV care (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.34; low-certainty evidence), though effects varied widely between studies [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may slightly increase attendance for vaccinations compared with non-digital targeted client communication (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.28; low-certainty evidence) and may make little or no difference to oral hygiene practices (low-certainty evidence) [3]. No trials in any population reported data on unintended consequences of these mobile tracking, decision-support and reminder interventions, so potential harms remain unmeasured [3]. All of the non-randomised studies included in the evidence were judged to have a high risk of bias [1]. In antenatal and postnatal care, tracking plus decision support may slightly increase home visits in the week after delivery, counselling on complementary feeding, skin-to-skin care and early breastfeeding, but effects on several other behaviours and on home visits within 24 hours are uncertain [1]. Digital tracking plus targeted client communication may reduce low birthweight babies (1 study) and, in one stroke study, may reduce deaths and stroke hospitalisations, while effects on stillbirths, neonatal and infant deaths, and antenatal HIV positivity are uncertain [1]. Tracking combined with decision support and targeted client communication probably increases provider guideline adherence for antenatal anaemia screening and management, diabetes and hypertension (each from one study), but probably leads to lower adherence for abnormal foetal growth (1 study, OR 0.59) [1]. Tracking with decision support and client communication may reduce deaths in patients with hypertension and diabetes (OR 0.61 [0.35, 1.06]) and new cardiovascular events in high-risk adults (OR 0.58 [0.42, 0.80]), but may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes (OR 0.99 [0.87, 1.12]) [1]. In one study of 3324 adults with hypertension or diabetes, tracking combined with decision support and client communication may make little or no difference to HbA1c, total cholesterol, 10-year cardiovascular risk, tobacco use, alcohol use, or PHQ-9 depression scores [1]. The evidence is uncertain for maternal or infant mortality, blood pressure control, body mass index, fasting glucose and quality of life, and digital tracking with prompts may have little or no effect on missed early infant diagnosis visits (1 study, 1183 participants; OR 0.92 [0.63, 1.35]) [1]. Based on one study of 3324 adults with hypertension or diabetes, the intervention probably increases slightly the number reporting a 'slightly/much better' change in quality of care (RR 1.02, 1.00 to 1.03), while effects on linkage to care are uncertain [1]. No studies evaluated time between presentation and appropriate management, timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences [1]. The certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, and it was often downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision [2]. The effect of the intervention on patients' and clients' health behaviours such as smoking and treatment adherence is mixed, with substantial variation across outcomes for similar types of behaviour (2 studies, 2262 participants) [2]. The intervention probably makes little or no difference to smoking rates among people at risk of cardiovascular disease, but probably increases other desired patient behaviours such as adherence to treatment [2]. The effect of the intervention on patients' and clients' health status and well-being is mixed, based on 5 studies with 69,767 participants [2]. Digital tracking with decision support or reminders probably makes little or no difference to some types of health outcomes, but its effect on other outcomes, including maternal and neonatal deaths, is uncertain because the evidence is very low-certainty [2]. The review found no studies that reported the time between illness presentation and appropriate management, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences [2]. TCCMD may modestly increase contraception use (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.35), but the evidence is low-certainty [4]. The effects on condom use, antiretroviral therapy adherence, and health service use are uncertain because the evidence is of very low certainty [4]. For targeted client communication via mobile devices compared with digital non-targeted communication, the effects on sexual health knowledge, condom use and contraceptive use are uncertain because the evidence is very low-certainty [4]. Interventions may increase health service use, such as attendance for STI/HIV testing (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.40), though the evidence is low-certainty [4]. Digital tracking with provider prompts or client messages may reduce sexually transmitted infection rates (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.33), but this is low-certainty evidence and the interval includes both benefit and harm [4]. We are uncertain whether digital tracking of clients combined with decision support or client messaging on mobile devices (TCCMD) results in unintended consequences, because there is a lack of evidence [4]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may modestly increase contraception use at 12 months (RR 1.17, 95% CI 0.92 to 1.48) and may reduce repeat abortion (RR 0.68, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.66), but the intervals include both benefit and harm (low-certainty evidence) [4]. The effect of mobile tracking with decision support and/or client messages on condom use is uncertain [4]. No study measured the impact of this intervention on sexually transmitted infection (STI) rates, so its effect on that health outcome is unknown [4]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may modestly increase adherence to antiretroviral therapy (RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.32; standardised mean difference 0.44, 95% CI -0.14 to 1.02), but the evidence is low-certainty and the intervals include no effect [4]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may modestly increase health service utilisation (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.31; low-certainty evidence), but heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 85%) and results varied by type of service [4]. For health outcomes, mobile tracking with prompts or reminders may have little or no effect on CD4 count (mean difference 13.99, 95% CI -8.65 to 36.63) and may slightly reduce virological failure (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.01), both low-certainty evidence [4]. For digital tracking with client communication on mobile devices versus non-digital tracking and communication, no studies reported STI rates, condom use, ART adherence, abortion rates, or contraceptive use, so effects on these outcomes are unknown [4]. Few of the included studies reported on unintended consequences, so evidence on possible harms of these mobile tracking and prompting systems is limited [4]. One study reported that a participant withdrew from the intervention because they felt it compromised their undisclosed HIV status [4]. The sources report that the unintended consequences include barriers to use that have equity implications [5].

What holds up

The most direct evidence on this question shows mixed results: possible benefits in some situations, and little or no difference in others. When the two add-ons are studied separately, much of the evidence is of low or very low certainty, and effects on people's health are often unknown. Resource use and unintended harms have largely not been studied, and the trials may have excluded people without phones or with low literacy. Firm claims that these tools improve care or health are not supported by the evidence summarised here.
