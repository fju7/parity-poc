Carbon dioxide removal and net zero: its role now and in the decades ahead

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

Reaching net zero CO2 or net zero GHG emissions requires deep and rapid reductions in gross emissions [2]. The IPCC also finds that deploying carbon dioxide removal (CDR) to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable (high confidence) [2]. CDR will also be necessary to achieve net negative CO2 emissions [2]. Across the IPCC categories of pathways, cuts in gross CO2 and non-CO2 emissions achieve over 80% of net GHG reductions between 2020 and global net zero CO2 [4]. One review states that CDR methods are not a substitute for emissions reductions [5]. It notes that scenarios suggest an 8:2 ratio of effort between reducing CO2 emissions and scaling CO2 removals by mid-century, and that a precautionary approach may suggest even more focus on reductions [5].

What the sources report

Why removals feature in net zero at all

From a physical science perspective, limiting human-caused global warming to a specific level requires three things: limiting cumulative CO2 emissions, reaching net zero or net negative CO2 emissions, and strongly reducing other GHG emissions [2]. Reaching net zero GHG emissions primarily requires deep reductions in CO2, methane and other GHG emissions, and implies net negative CO2 emissions [2]. Some hard-to-abate residual GHG emissions remain, for example some emissions from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes [2]. These would need to be counterbalanced by deployment of CDR methods to achieve net zero CO2 or GHG emissions (high confidence) [2]. As a result, net zero CO2 is reached earlier than net zero GHGs (high confidence) [2]. Global net zero CO2 means that remaining anthropogenic CO2 emissions are balanced by durably stored CO2 from anthropogenic removal [2]. Reaching it is a requirement to stabilise CO2-induced global surface temperature increase (high confidence) [2]. The IPCC glossary defines CDR as anthropogenic activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and durably store it in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in products [2]. Net zero GHG emissions, if sustained, are projected to result in a gradual decline in global surface temperatures after an earlier peak (high confidence) [2]. Global modelled pathways that reach net zero CO2 and GHG emissions include CDR alongside other measures [2]. These are a transition from fossil fuels without CCS to very low- or zero-carbon energy sources, demand-side measures, improved efficiency, and reduced non-CO2 GHG emissions [2]. For bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS), the glossary notes that CO2 can be removed from the atmosphere depending on the total emissions of the supply chain [2].

The role of removals now

Current CDR approximates 2 GtCO2 per year, or about 3% of current global net greenhouse gas emissions [5]. Conventional CDR on land is currently estimated to contribute around 2 Gt CO2 of removals per year [4]. Novel CDR, which includes BECCS, direct air carbon capture and storage and enhanced weathering, is currently estimated to contribute around 2 Mt CO2 per year [4]. In 1.5 °C pathways with limited overshoot, mitigation between 2020 and 2030 breaks down as follows: about 70% comes from reducing gross CO2 emissions and about 20% from cutting non-CO2 emissions [4]. About 10% comes from halting deforestation and expanding conventional CDR on land, which nearly doubles the volume of CDR this decade [4]. One analysis describes removals via afforestation and reforestation as playing a critical near-term role in mitigation [4]. Another study states that rapid emissions reductions, including reductions in deforestation-based land emissions, are the dominant source of global mitigation potential in the coming decades [3]. It adds that CDR will also have an important role to play [3]. One review describes three roles for CDR over time [5]. CDR methods can reduce emissions in the near term and compensate for hard-to-abate residual emissions in the medium term [5]. In the long term, they would potentially allow global temperature exceedance to be reversed, but would not compensate for the impacts of overshoot [5].

The role of removals by mid-century and beyond

Global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5 °C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot reach net zero CO2 emissions around 2050, with total GHGs reaching net zero later [2]. Across most 1.5 °C limited-overshoot scenarios, novel CDR methods scale up by mid-century to support net zero CO2, reaching around 4 Gt CO2 (range 2–6) by 2050 [4]. Between 2020 and global net zero CO2 in these pathways, gross CO2 reductions account for 71% of net GHG reductions, while CDR contributes a much smaller 15% [4]. Novel CDR technologies such as direct air carbon capture and storage scale to multi-gigatonne levels by 2050 and beyond, both to balance residual emissions and to draw down warming [4]. Even low estimates of CDR requirements by mid-century imply several gigatons of removals [5]. Across the assessed scenarios, gross greenhouse gas emissions at the year of global net zero CO2 are estimated at 18 (16–21) Gt CO2eq per year [4]. Removals play a larger role after 2050 in the OECD and EU region [4]. There they account for a median of around 43%, 48% and 35% of net GHG reductions across the C1, C2 and C3 pathways respectively [4].

Delay increases reliance on unproven methods

Delaying net zero CO2 increases the total CDR deployed up to that point [4]. It is 166 Gt CO2 for scenarios reaching net zero by mid-century, 251 Gt CO2 for net zero between 2050 and 2075, and 445 Gt CO2 for net zero later than 2075 [4]. Novel removals are still at a nascent stage of development [4]. They are expected to require both time and investment to ramp up and become a cost-effective part of a mitigation portfolio [4]. The amount of conventional CDR on land is largely consistent across different timings of delayed emissions reductions [4]. By contrast, novel CDR use rises strongly in scenarios with longer net emission halving times [4]. The authors say this shows these scenarios depend on future, unproven CDR technologies when net emissions reductions are delayed [4]. Less stringent climate objectives do not fundamentally change the scale of gross CO2 reductions needed, but merely shift them over time, which the authors say raises questions of intergenerational fairness [4]. Projected CO2 emissions from existing fossil fuel infrastructure without additional abatement would exceed the remaining carbon budget for 1.5 °C (50%) (high confidence) [2].

Plans, gaps and governance

One analysis identifies a 'CDR gap': CDR efforts proposed by countries fall short of those in integrated assessment model scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C [3]. The most ambitious national CDR proposals are close to levels in a low-energy demand scenario that combines the most-limited CDR scaling with aggressive near-term emissions reductions [3]. Many countries propose to expand land-based removals [3]. However, none yet commit to substantively scaling novel methods such as BECCS, biochar or direct air carbon capture and storage [3]. When net zero is reached for a given sector or region depends on several factors (high confidence) [2]. These are the potential to reduce emissions and to undertake CDR, the associated costs, and the availability of policy mechanisms to balance emissions and removals [2]. Scaling CDR will require advances in policymaking and governance, including strict monitoring to ensure removals are durable, additional and do not compete with emission reduction efforts [5]. Many have discussed separate policy tracks for reductions and removals [5]. The aim is to avoid the risk that longer-term ambitions for CDR dilute short-term efforts to reduce emissions [5]. Emerging compliance markets will need to deal with permanence and with how to govern liabilities for removals that expire after a certain period, such as afforestation and reforestation [5]. For BECCS, sustainability concerns include competition for land, impacts on food prices and impacts on biodiversity [4]. Without policy support, one review judges it seems unlikely that niche voluntary markets can sustain novel CDR in the long term [5].

What holds up

The firmest finding is that deep cuts in gross emissions do most of the work toward net zero [2, 4]. Even so, CDR to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable (high confidence) [2, 4]. Today, removals are almost entirely conventional land-based methods of around 2 Gt CO2 per year, while novel methods contribute around 2 Mt CO2 per year [4]. In the 1.5 °C pathways, the near-term contribution of removals comes mainly from land, at about 10% of net reductions this decade [4]. Novel CDR then scales to gigatonne levels by mid-century, and more is needed the longer emissions reductions are delayed [4]. Countries' current CDR proposals fall short of 1.5 °C scenario levels, and none yet commit to substantively scaling novel methods [3]. The sources treat removal as a complement to emission cuts, not a substitute [4, 5]. It depends on nascent technologies, sustainability safeguards and governance that ensures removals are durable and additional [4, 5].
