Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review of 28 studies concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review describes this as emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of such interventions [1]. It also describes the quality of the included studies as largely low [1]. Skill-building programmes showed the largest effects, on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but each of these effects rests on a low number of studies [1]. The authors say more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious and how best to deliver them [1].

What the review found

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers without disabilities in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. Evidence suggests that the gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is greatest in LMICs [1]. Before this review, the authors note, evidence on which interventions are actually effective had not been established [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, "a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom)" [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system‐level or school‐level changes [1]. The source states that, consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed [1].

What the evidence base looks like

The review searched for studies up to March 2022 and was published in February 2025 [1]. Most included studies were aimed at children with disabilities, and a few targeted family members or service providers [1]. People with intellectual or learning and developmental impairments were the group most frequently targeted [1]. Eight studies came from India, four from Iran, and two each from China, South Africa and Egypt; one study each came from Brazil, Jordan, Kenya, Lebanon, Romania, Turkey, Malaysia, Nigeria, Uganda and Zambia [1]. None of the studies were done in humanitarian settings, and few reported whether their setting was urban or rural [1]. Early childhood development interventions aimed at improving development and preventing disability in children generally were not eligible for the review [1].

Programmes that build children's skills for learning

Of the 18 studies analysed for effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but each was based on a low number of studies [1]. For numeracy, the pooled effect was d = 4.60 (0.69 to 8.50), from two studies with high heterogeneity [1]. For handwriting, the effect was 4.97 (CI 4.21 to 5.73), with low heterogeneity, but this is based on only two studies [1]. In the pooled analysis, effects on speech and communication (d = 0.70, five studies) and on school behaviour and adaptive functioning (d = 0.49, five studies) were described as insignificant [1]. The review's narrative summary nonetheless lists speech and communication, and school behaviour and adaptive functioning, among the areas where attainment-focused studies showed positive effects [1]. The interventions studied included computer-assisted instruction, parent education, alternative and augmentative communication for children with autism, auditory training for children with hearing impairments, and strategy instruction for students with dyslexia [1].

Programmes that make learning environments more inclusive

Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. That pooled effect was d = 0.35 (0.12 to 0.58) from four studies, with high heterogeneity and potentially moderate publication bias [1]. Studies aimed at making learning environments more accessible reported positive impacts on teacher knowledge and attitudes, parent knowledge and attitudes, and violence reduction [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. Examples include the Good School Toolkit, a randomised trial in Ugandan primary schools with 3,820 participants that aimed to reduce violence from peers and staff toward students with and without disabilities [1]. No studies represented the intervention categories 'Accessibility of built environment and learning materials' or 'Inclusive education policies' [1].

Programmes for attendance, enrolment and completion

Improving attendance, enrolment and completion by equipping mainstream settings for children with disabilities was achieved in one study, in which teacher knowledge and attitudes about inclusive education improved [1]. That study was a teacher training programme in Kenya, evaluated with an uncontrolled before-versus-after design [1].

Limits of the evidence

Most study designs could not account for many potential confounders, and masking, baseline balance and attribution were poorly reported [1]. The included studies all used different interventions and outcome measures, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. Funnel plots suggest some categories are likely to suffer from publication bias, and publication bias for numeracy and handwriting could not be assessed because only two studies were available for each [1]. Almost all studies included both male and female participants, but few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The review itself may be biased by its lack of grey literature and its absence of non-English literature [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies received an overall rating of low confidence in their findings [1]. The low confidence in the evidence across studies was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs, and did not always reflect multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. The source says a twin-track approach is needed, one that both addresses children with disabilities' specific needs and ensures their inclusion in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through school-level or system-level changes [1]. The sources say evaluation should focus on interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, which they describe as needed [1].

What holds up

What holds up is that a range of interventions, mostly targeted at individuals with disabilities, have been found to improve educational outcomes in LMICs [1]. The review calls this support emerging, and the underlying studies are largely of low quality [1]. The largest effects appear for skill-building programmes in literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy [1]. Because these effects rest on few studies, they do not establish which kind of programme works best. The review asked which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability, but its authors conclude that more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious [1]. Most interventions tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. The authors therefore call for interventions that target both the individual and their broader environment, such as teacher training and curriculum modifications [1]. They also call for programmes that address attendance, enrolment, completion and transition to higher levels of education [1]. Finally, they recommend larger studies with more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1].
