Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?

The short answer

The question is what part removing carbon dioxide from the air can play, alongside cutting emissions, in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead.

Read together, the IPCC and State of Carbon Dioxide Removal assessments give removal a necessary but bounded part: it is needed to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions, but across cost-effective Paris-compatible scenarios emission reductions contribute at least 80% of the effort to reach net zero CO2, and today's removals, almost all from forests and soils, fall short of the additional gigatonne-scale removal those scenarios deploy.

What counts as removal

The IPCC defines carbon dioxide removal (CDR) as human activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and store it durably in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in products.

The IPCC describes carbon capture and storage (CCS) at large-scale fossil-based energy and industry sources as an option to reduce emissions, provided geological storage is available; when CO2 is captured directly from the atmosphere (DACCS) or from biomass (BECCS), CCS provides the storage component of these removal methods.

The IPCC estimates technical geological CO2 storage capacity to be on the order of 1000 GtCO2, more than the CO2 storage requirements through 2100 to limit global warming to 1.5°C, although the regional availability of geological storage could be a limiting factor; if a storage site is appropriately selected and managed, it estimates that the CO2 can be permanently isolated from the atmosphere.

It also finds that implementation of CCS currently faces technological, economic, institutional, ecological-environmental and socio-cultural barriers, and that global rates of CCS deployment are far below those in modelled pathways limiting global warming to 1.5°C or 2°C.

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report divides removal into conventional CDR, well-established methods that largely involve forests and soils, and novel CDR, such as biochar, enhanced weathering and direct air carbon capture and storage.

Why net zero needs it

The IPCC's 2022 mitigation assessment states that deploying CDR to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or greenhouse gas emissions are to be achieved, and that the scale and timing of deployment will depend on how gross emissions fall in different sectors (high confidence).

Chapter 12 of that assessment calls CDR a necessary element of net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas emissions both globally and nationally, counterbalancing residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors.

It also calls CDR a key element in scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower by 2100 (robust evidence, high agreement).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report states that for as long as any emissions continue, CDR will be needed to halt the rise in global temperature, and that all scenarios that reach net zero and halt the rise in global temperature deploy additional CDR at gigatonne scale.

The IPCC's 2021 physical-science assessment reaffirms with high confidence that there is a near-linear relationship between cumulative anthropogenic CO2 emissions and the global warming they cause, with each 1000 GtCO2 of cumulative CO2 emissions assessed to likely cause a 0.27°C to 0.63°C increase in global surface temperature, with a best estimate of 0.45°C.

It states that achieving global net zero CO2 emissions, with anthropogenic CO2 emissions balanced by anthropogenic removals of CO2, is a requirement for stabilizing the CO2-induced rise in global surface temperature, and that pathways reaching and sustaining net zero greenhouse gas emissions defined by the 100-year global warming potential are projected to result in a decline in surface temperature after an earlier peak (high confidence).

In scenarios that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, global transport-related CO2 emissions fall by 59% (42–68% interquartile range) by 2050 relative to modelled 2020 emissions (high confidence), and in scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) they decrease by 29% [14–44% interquartile range]; in both, the transport sector likely does not reach zero CO2 emissions by 2100, so negative emissions are likely needed to counterbalance residual CO2 emissions from the sector (high confidence).

Alongside cuts, not instead of them

The IPCC assesses that, in addition to deep, rapid and sustained emission reductions, CDR can fulfil three complementary roles: lowering net emissions in the near term; counterbalancing hard-to-abate residual emissions, such as those from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes, to help reach net zero in the mid-term; and achieving net negative emissions in the long term if deployed at levels exceeding annual residual emissions (high confidence).

All the IPCC's modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and those that limit it to 2°C (>67%), involve rapid and deep and in most cases immediate emission reductions in all sectors, with CDR deployed to counterbalance residual emissions (high confidence).

Across cost-effective scenarios compatible with the Paris Agreement, the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that reductions in emissions contribute at least 80% of the effort to achieve net zero CO2 emissions, and CDR contributes the remainder.

The IPCC also assesses that deep emission reductions by 2030 and 2040, particularly of methane, lower peak warming and lead to less reliance on net negative CO2 emissions in the latter half of the century (high confidence).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that delaying emissions reductions would increase the need to deploy removals, especially novel CDR, if the Paris temperature goal is to be met, that dependence on such high levels of CDR, at the upper ends of potentials, risks crossing sustainability thresholds, and that reducing emissions more rapidly would allow for a more moderate scale-up of removals.

The IPCC finds that pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot involve more rapid and deeper near-term emission reductions through to 2030, and are projected to have less net negative CO2 emissions and less CDR in the longer term, than pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot (high confidence).

All mitigation strategies face implementation challenges, and challenges such as dependence on CDR and pressure on land and biodiversity are significantly reduced in modelled pathways that assume using resources more efficiently or that shift global development towards sustainability (high confidence).

The IPCC also notes that aggregate levels of CDR deployment in modelled pathways are higher than their total net negative CO2 emissions, because some of the deployed CDR counterbalances remaining gross emissions, that cumulative CDR from agriculture, forestry and other land use cannot be quantified precisely, and that total land-sector CDR equals or exceeds the net negative emissions reported.

The IPCC adds that cumulative CDR from agriculture, forestry and other land use cannot be quantified precisely because some pathways assess CDR deployment relative to a baseline and different models use different reporting methodologies, and that total net negative CO2 emissions in modelled pathways might not match the aggregated net negative CO2 emissions attributed to individual CDR methods.

Land-sector mitigation options, when sustainably implemented, can deliver large-scale emission reductions and enhanced removals, but cannot fully compensate for delayed action in other sectors (high confidence).

Strengthened near-term action beyond the pledges countries announced before COP26 can reduce or avoid long-term feasibility challenges of pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot (high confidence).

Most mitigation options face barriers when implemented rapidly at a large scale, and strengthened and coordinated near-term action in cost-effective pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower reduces the overall risks to the feasibility of system transitions, compared with delayed or uncoordinated action (high confidence).

Many mitigation options have environmental co-benefits but also adverse environmental impacts, such as reduced biodiversity, when applied at very large scale, for example very large scale bioenergy, which would have to be managed (medium confidence), and almost all mitigation options face institutional barriers that need to be addressed to enable their application at scale (medium confidence).

The IPCC uses feasibility to mean the potential for an option to be implemented, depending on geophysical, environmental-ecological, technological, economic, socio-cultural and institutional factors that are context-dependent and may change over time, and it names finance, technological innovation, stronger policy instruments, institutional capacity, multi-level governance and changes in behaviour and lifestyles as enabling conditions.

Now: how much is removed

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report puts total removal at 2.2 GtCO2 per year, equivalent to 5% of gross CO2 emissions, and conventional CDR at 99.9% of this total.

It reports novel CDR at 0.002 GtCO2 per year, growing at 40% per year, which it calls similar to successful technologies like solar energy but insufficient for the scale-up required to meet the Paris temperature goal.

Biochar and BECCS account for almost all novel removals, and the report states that these projects plus those in construction would reach 0.008 GtCO2 per year of capacity in 2030, although only 20% of planned capacity has been built in recent years.

For novel CDR, the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report puts company ambitions at approximately 42 MtCO2 per year of removals by 2030, but cautions that many of these ambitions do not necessarily provide concrete details of upcoming projects and are thus speculative and subject to change.

The IPCC's 2022 assessment likewise names afforestation, reforestation, improved forest management, agroforestry and soil carbon sequestration as currently the only widely practised CDR methods (high confidence).

The decades ahead: what the pathways use

In the IPCC's modelled pathways, global net zero CO2 emissions are reached in the early 2050s where warming is limited to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and around the early 2070s where it is limited to 2°C (>67%); many of these pathways continue to net negative CO2 emissions after the point of net zero, and the level of peak warming depends on cumulative CO2 emissions until net zero CO2 and on the change in non-CO2 climate forcers by the time of peaking (high confidence).

At the time of global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, the IPCC finds, net negative CO2 emissions counterbalance the metric-weighted emissions of the other greenhouse gases, and typical emissions pathways that reach and sustain net zero greenhouse gas emissions based on the 100-year global warming potential (GWP-100) are projected to result in a gradual decline in global warming (high confidence).

All of the IPCC's Illustrative Mitigation Pathways use land-based biological CDR, primarily afforestation and reforestation, and/or BECCS, and some include DACCS.

At the point modelled pathways reach global net zero CO2 emissions, 5–16 GtCO2 of emissions from some sectors are compensated for by net negative CO2 emissions in other sectors, and in most pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower the land sector, via reforestation and reduced deforestation, and the energy supply sector reach net zero CO2 earlier than buildings, industry and transport (high confidence).

Methods and levels of CDR in the IPCC's modelled pathways vary depending on assumptions about costs, availability and constraints.

In modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 is 30–780 GtCO2 from BECCS and 0–310 GtCO2 from DACCS, and the land sector contributes 20–400 GtCO2 of net negative emissions (high confidence).

For modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), the IPCC finds, with high confidence, that global cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 from BECCS and DACCS is 170–650 GtCO2 and 0–250 GtCO2 respectively, that the agriculture, forestry and other land use (AFOLU) sector contributes 10–250 GtCO2 net negative emissions, and that total cumulative net negative CO2 emissions are around 40 [0–290] GtCO2.

Chapter 12 reports that, across scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, median annual volumes in 2050 (5–95% range) are 2.75 (0.52–9.45) GtCO2 for BECCS, 2.98 (0.23–6.38) GtCO2 for removal from agriculture, forestry and other land use, mainly afforestation and reforestation, and 0.02 (0–1.74) GtCO2 for DACCS.

In the scenarios the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report assessed, conventional CDR plays the strongest near-term role, while novel CDR scales up by a factor of five between 2030 and 2035 and accelerates to over 3.5 GtCO2 per year by 2050.

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that all scenarios it assesses as consistent with the Paris Agreement grow both novel and conventional CDR by a gigatonne or more in 2050 to limit the amount and duration of 1.5°C overshoot.

The report finds that country pledges reach 2.5 GtCO2 per year of CDR in 2030, against a median of 2.9 GtCO2 per year in the highest-ambition Paris-compatible scenarios, a gap it puts at 0.3 GtCO2 per year that grows to 1.2 GtCO2 per year in 2035 and 5.2 GtCO2 per year in 2050.

Because emissions have continued to grow since the report's second edition, it says, so has the CDR gap, and it will continue to widen without significant near-term action to reduce emissions.

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that scenarios pursuing delayed ambition for ten years compared to immediate action show temperature peaking around 0.15°C higher than with highest ambition, and that they tend to have lower near-term CDR deployment through 2050 but then require higher CDR deployment after midcentury to return warming to 1.5°C.

Set side by side, the measured figure and the assessed need are far apart for novel methods: 0.002 GtCO2 per year removed now, against over 3.5 GtCO2 per year by 2050 in the scenarios the same report assessed.

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that Highest Ambition scenarios have median CDR levels of 2.9 GtCO2 in 2030, 3.9 GtCO2 in 2035 and 8.8 GtCO2 in 2050.

Going below zero

The IPCC's 2021 physical-science assessment states that CDR has the potential to remove CO2 from the atmosphere and durably store it in reservoirs (high confidence), and that it aims to compensate for residual emissions to reach net zero CO2 or net zero greenhouse gas emissions or, if implemented at a scale where removals exceed emissions, to lower surface temperature.

The assessment adds that potential negative and positive effects of CDR for biodiversity, water and food production are methods-specific and are often highly dependent on local context, management, prior land use, and scale.

It adds that CDR methods can have wide-ranging effects on biogeochemical cycles and climate, which can either weaken or strengthen their potential to remove CO2 and reduce warming, and can also influence water availability and quality, food production and biodiversity (high confidence).

It assesses that CDR leading to global net negative emissions would lower the atmospheric CO2 concentration and reverse surface ocean acidification (high confidence).

It projects that under an intermediate emissions scenario the rates of CO2 taken up by the land and ocean decrease in the second half of this century (high confidence), and that under very low and low emissions scenarios, in which CO2 concentrations peak and decline during this century, the land and ocean begin to take up less carbon in response to declining atmospheric CO2 (high confidence) and turn into a weak net source by 2100 under the very low scenario (medium confidence).

It assesses as very unlikely that the combined global land and ocean sink turns into a source by 2100 under scenarios without net negative emissions.

Removals and emissions of CO2 are partially compensated by CO2 release from, and uptake by, land and ocean carbon pools (very high confidence).

CDR would lower atmospheric CO2 by an amount approximately equal to the increase from an emission of the same size (high confidence), though the decrease could be up to 10% less than the increase from an equal amount of emissions, depending on the total amount of CDR (medium confidence).

If global net negative CO2 emissions were achieved and sustained, the CO2-induced rise in surface temperature would be gradually reversed, but other climate changes would continue in their current direction for decades to millennia; for instance, global mean sea level would take several centuries to millennia to reverse course even under large net negative CO2 emissions (high confidence).

The IPCC assesses that modelled pathways consistent with the pledges countries announced before COP26 will likely exceed 1.5°C during this century, and that those which then return warming to 1.5°C by 2100 imply global cumulative net-negative CO2 emissions of –380 [–860 to –200] GtCO2 in the second half of the century, with increased climate-related risk, increased feasibility concerns and greater social and environmental risks than pathways with no or limited overshoot (high confidence).

Limits and risks

CDR methods vary in maturity, removal process, time scale of carbon storage, storage medium, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, impacts and risks, and governance requirements (high confidence).

The IPCC categorises the processes by which CO2 is removed from the atmosphere as biological, geochemical or chemical.

The IPCC says CDR costs range from lower-cost methods (e.g., USD −45 to 100 per tonne of CO2 for soil carbon sequestration) to higher-cost methods (e.g., USD 100–300 per tonne for DACCS) (medium confidence), and storage times vary from decades to centuries for carbon in vegetation and soils to 10,000 years or more in geological formations (high confidence).

Removal and storage of CO2 through vegetation and soil management can be reversed by human or natural disturbances and is prone to climate change impacts, while CO2 stored in geological and ocean reservoirs, and carbon in biochar, is less prone to reversal (high confidence).

The impacts, risks and co-benefits of removal for ecosystems, biodiversity and people will be highly variable depending on the method, site, implementation and scale: reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon sequestration, peatland restoration and blue carbon management can enhance biodiversity, ecosystem functions, employment and local livelihoods, depending on context, while afforestation or biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, when poorly implemented, can have adverse socio-economic and environmental impacts, including on biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially at large scales and where land tenure is insecure (high confidence).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report adds that impacts beyond removing carbon can substantially shape the potential and viability of CDR methods, and that these side effects vary by deployment scenario, typically increase with scale, and include consequences for biodiversity, resource consumption, energy use and food production.

Chapter 12 finds that scenarios limiting warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower by 2100 commonly involve extensive mitigation in agriculture, forestry and other land use that at the same time provides biomass for mitigation in other sectors (robust evidence, high agreement), and that growing demands for food, feed, biomaterials and non-fossil fuels increase the competition for land and biomass.

It adds that appropriate integration of bioenergy and other bio-based systems with existing land and biomass uses can improve resource use efficiency, mitigate pressures on natural ecosystems and support adaptation (medium evidence, high agreement).

It notes that CDR options can have positive impacts on ecosystem services and the Sustainable Development Goals but also potential adverse side effects, and that implementation will depend in part on how societies weigh mitigation against food, nature conservation and other goals, and on society's future dependence on CDR (medium evidence, medium agreement).

Chapter 12 lists among the knowledge gaps it encountered the 'co-benefits and trade-offs with biodiversity and ecosystem services associated with the implementation of CDR methods' and 'constraining technical costs and potentials for CDR methods to define realistically achievable costs and potentials', research it says is 'useful for improving the representation of CDR methods in IAMs and country-level mitigation pathway modelling.'

It also finds that governance of CDR can support effective and equitable policy implementation (medium evidence, high agreement), and that incentivising CDR deployment requires a political commitment to formal integration into existing climate policy frameworks, including reliable monitoring, reporting and verification of carbon flows.

The governance of land-based mitigation, including land-based CDR, can draw on lessons from previous experience with regulating biofuels and forest carbon, but integrating them requires governance that goes beyond project-level approaches and emphasises integrated land-use planning and management.

The IPCC puts the projected economic mitigation potential of agriculture, forestry and other land use options between 2020 and 2050, at costs below USD100 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, at 8–14 GtCO2-eq per year (high confidence).

The largest share of this potential, 4.2–7.4 GtCO2-eq per year, comes from the conservation, improved management and restoration of forests and other ecosystems, while improved and sustainable crop and livestock management and carbon sequestration in agriculture, including soil carbon management, agroforestry and biochar, can contribute 1.8–4.1 GtCO2-eq per year.

Demand-side and material substitution measures, such as balanced, sustainable healthy diets, reducing food loss and waste and using bio-materials, can contribute 2.1 [1.1–3.6] GtCO2-eq per year, can with sustainable intensification of agriculture free up land for reforestation and restoration, and longer-lived wood products from sustainably managed forests also have potential.

The IPCC states that these land-sector measures cannot compensate for delayed emission reductions in other sectors, that persistent and region-specific barriers continue to hamper their economic and political feasibility, and that assisting countries to overcome barriers will help to achieve significant mitigation (medium confidence).

The 8–14 GtCO2-eq per year figure is the mean of top-down estimates, its lower bound, and global sectoral bottom-up estimates, its upper bound; the full range is 4.1–17.3 GtCO2-eq per year from top-down estimates using a no-policy baseline and 6.7–23.4 GtCO2-eq per year from global sectoral studies using a variety of baselines.

Land-sector carbon sequestration and emission reduction options have both co-benefits and risks for biodiversity and ecosystem conservation, food and water security, wood supply, livelihoods and the land tenure and land-use rights of Indigenous Peoples, local communities and small land owners, and options that compete for land and land-based resources can pose risks (high confidence).

Realising the land sector's mitigation potential entails overcoming institutional, economic and policy constraints and managing potential trade-offs (high confidence), with barriers that include uncertainty over long-term additionality, insecure land ownership, the risk of reversal and limited access to technology, data and know-how, and research and development are key for all measures.

Net costs of delivering 5–6 GtCO2 per year of forest-related carbon sequestration and emission reduction are estimated to reach about USD400 billion per year by 2050, and the costs of other land-sector measures are highly context specific.

Financing needs in the land sector, and in particular in forestry, include both the direct effects of any changes in activities and the opportunity costs associated with land-use change.

Enhanced monitoring, reporting and verification capacity, and the rule of law, are crucial for land-based mitigation (medium confidence).

Deploying land-based mitigation can draw on lessons from experience with regulations, policies, economic incentives and payments, including for biofuels and forest carbon; Indigenous Peoples, private forest owners, local farmers and communities manage a significant share of global forests and agricultural land and play a central role in land-based mitigation; and scaling successful policies relies on governance that emphasises integrated land-use planning and management (high confidence).

Land-based options such as reforestation and forest conservation, agroforestry and soil carbon management can have multiple synergies with the Sustainable Development Goals, and maximising synergies and managing trade-offs depend on specific practices, scale of implementation, governance and the involvement of local communities and Indigenous Peoples (high confidence).

Trade-offs in employment, water use, land-use competition and biodiversity can be avoided by well-implemented land-based mitigation options, and the sustainability of bioenergy and other bio-based products is influenced by feedstock, land management practice, climatic region and the timing, scale and speed of deployment (medium confidence).

Soil carbon sequestration and biochar can improve soil quality and food production capacity, while ecosystem restoration and reforestation can enhance biodiversity but can displace food production and livelihoods, and because some options are little applied today there are some uncertainties about potential benefits (high confidence).

The summary adds that mitigation options have synergies with many Sustainable Development Goals but some can also have trade-offs, and that the synergies and trade-offs vary with context and scale.

Some mitigation options, such as large-scale or poorly planned deployment of bioenergy, biochar and afforestation of naturally unforested land, can increase competition for land, water and biomass and reduce adaptive capacity (high confidence).

Chapter 12 estimates the potential of DACCS at 5–40 GtCO2 per year, limited mainly by its requirements for low-carbon energy and by cost, and puts DACCS at a medium technology readiness level and enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods at a low one (medium evidence, medium agreement).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report gives expected removal costs from less than $10 per tonne to over $1000 per tonne, with most methods having upper limits exceeding $200 per tonne, well above current carbon prices, and the more reliable lower estimates of potential for most methods around 1 GtCO2 per year.

The IPCC assesses that upscaling CDR depends on addressing sustainability and feasibility constraints, and names as enablers accelerated research, development and demonstration, improved tools for risk assessment and management, targeted incentives, and agreed methods for measurement, reporting and verification of carbon flows (high confidence).

What we read, and what we did not

We hold four documents in full and drew on all four: the Summary for Policymakers and Chapter 12 of the IPCC's 2022 Working Group III report, the Summary for Policymakers of the IPCC's 2021 Working Group I report, and the 2026 third edition of The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal.

We did not read the other chapters of the IPCC reports or the primary studies these assessments draw on, and we did not search for studies of individual removal methods.
