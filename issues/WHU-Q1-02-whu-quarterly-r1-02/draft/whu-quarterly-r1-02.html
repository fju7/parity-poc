Do education programmes for children with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is whether programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work, and which kinds work best.

The short answer

The most recent systematic review among our sources, by Hunt and colleagues, included twenty-eight studies. Its authors searched for studies up to March 2022. Many of the interventions it included were effective at improving children's functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on systemic or school-level changes. All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings. No study it included looked at outcomes related to attendance, enrolment, and completion. Because of the broad variety of interventions and outcomes and the limited number of high-quality randomised and quasi-experimental studies, the review says more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious. With evidence this limited, these reviews do not say which kind of programme works best.

What the 2025 systematic review found

Most of its studies (25) targeted children with disabilities. Two directly targeted family members, and the remaining three focused on service providers. Interventions most often targeted people with intellectual or learning and developmental impairments (17 studies).

The most common kind of intervention was educational attainment support, for instance a reading comprehension intervention for children with dyslexia. The second most common was accessible learning environments, for instance programmes which aimed to improve social skills or to reduce rates of victimisation of children with disabilities in schools.

Among the 18 studies included in the analysis of effects on skills for learning, 12 interventions had a significant effect. The effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting, and numeracy were significant. All these effects are large but are based on a low number of studies. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention. Studies concerned with conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education showed a moderately significant effect, and one study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size. The authors add that the studies on conditions for inclusion showed a large heterogeneity and a potential publication bias, and that the main limitation in this case was the low number of studies concerned with this topic.

Across studies, heterogeneity is high, and risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high. The authors say the lack of confidence was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study. Most of the included studies employed controlled before versus after designs, and a minority used a randomised controlled trial design. None of the studies were undertaken in humanitarian settings.

Thirteen interventions were conducted in inclusive mainstream settings, while 12 happened in segregated or specialised settings. The review did not reveal any systematic differences between the effectiveness of interventions delivered in mainstream or segregated or specialised settings, and its authors say this was likely due to the majority of interventions reporting positive findings.

The authors conclude that children with disabilities fall behind in educational outcomes as the current school systems are not set up to teach children with different impairment types. They conclude that there is no one magic bullet intervention for this group. They conclude that a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and ensures that they are included in mainstream activities, for example through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom. Most interventions included in the review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes. The authors call for evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment.

Earlier and narrower reviews

A 2018 rapid evidence assessment for the UK Department for International Development, by Kuper, Saran and White, found 24 eligible individual studies. For primary education, it found promising evidence that interventions are effective at improving the educational skills of primary-school-aged children with disabilities, but said better quality evidence is needed to make clearer judgements. The study quality of 13 of its 15 primary-education studies was deemed low, and the two remaining studies were deemed moderate. Two of those 15 studies made school-level changes. Among the evidence gaps it lists for primary education, the assessment notes that no studies assessed the impact of systems-level changes (e.g. policy). It found insufficient evidence on what works to improve secondary school outcomes, from only two studies. For early intervention, its text calls the evidence insufficient and its summary table calls it limited evidence. For early intervention, the assessment reports that 'the study outcomes were generally positive, with five studies showing improvements in the children's learning skills, two showing improvements in the skills of the parent, and only one study showing no impact,' although it still judged the evidence in this category insufficient. No studies were identified that assessed interventions to improve outcomes in lifelong learning or non-formal education. It concluded that more and better quality evidence is needed on what works for other stages of education, as well as for promoting the inclusive nature of schools, before specific approaches or programmes can be recommended.

A 2022 systematic review of educational technology for primary-school learners with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, by Lynch, Singal and Francis, comprised 51 published articles. It found examples of positive outcomes of educational technology interventions, particularly in special schools, but found the evidence of their efficacy weak. The authors also note that "the primary focus of many studies was on the use of EdTech to support factors of entry into learning, with little or no real exploration of the impact of these interventions on children's engagement, levels of independence and their participation within school settings."

A 2020 evidence and gap map by Saran, White and Kuper includes 166 studies of interventions for people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, 40 of them in education. The map's authors note, however, that "the studies classified under education very largely do not refer to participation of children with disabilities in formal education."

Across the interventions it maps, the evidence and gap map notes that there "appears to be a gap in the framing of the research, which is mostly within the medical model and does not use the social model of disability": the interventions "mostly try to change characteristics of the person with a disability (e.g., improve social skills) rather than to address structures (e.g., readiness of schools to include people with learning disabilities)."

Both the 2025 review and the 2018 assessment recommend that existing programmes run by governments, disabled people's organisations and non-governmental organisations evaluate whether they are effective in improving educational outcomes for people with disabilities.

What we held, read and did not read

We drew on four documents, each held in full text: the 2025 systematic review, the 2018 rapid evidence assessment, the 2022 educational technology review and the 2020 evidence and gap map.

We read the 2025 review's abstract, plain-language summary, discussion and conclusions, and the 2018 assessment's executive summary and conclusions; we did not read the individual studies the reviews include.

We did not obtain a 2020 correction to the evidence and gap map, because its publisher's terms do not allow our tools to read it.

We did not search databases systematically; these documents came from a web search on 29 October 2026.
