Do education programmes for people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe this evidence as providing "emerging support" for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions, rather than firm proof [1]. The clearest positive results come from programmes that build learners' skills, such as literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but those large effects rest on a low number of studies [1]. Separate evidence maps report that research in this field focuses mostly on changing the individual with a disability rather than on schools, institutions or infrastructure, so whether those broader approaches work remains largely untested [2, 3].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. Evidence suggests that the gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is greatest in LMICs [1]. Before the 2025 review, its authors noted that evidence on which interventions are actually effective for achieving international education goals for people with disabilities had not been established [1]. According to the source, "All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings." [1] According to the source, "A twin‐track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom)." [1] However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. According to the source, "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1] According to the source report, "This report illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs." [2] The source, titled "Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low‐ and middle‐income Asia‐Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map," is an evidence and gap map, and the supplied text is only that title, with no findings or certainty or confidence wording, so no conclusion about effectiveness can be attributed to it [2].

Skills-building programmes

Of the 18 studies in the review's analysis of intervention effects on "Skills for learning", 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but the review notes that they are based on a low number of studies [1].

Inclusion and teacher-focused programmes

In the same review, studies concerned with "Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education" showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. An evidence and gap map of teacher professional development for disability inclusion found 50 studies in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries, but only 16 of 41 countries reported such evidence [2]. Many of those teacher interventions focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability, because many of these countries are in the early stages of the inclusive education agenda [2]. Some of the included teacher studies were qualitative, with teachers reporting, for example, that training seminars and workshops in Vietnam improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2]. Only three studies in that map discuss interventions supporting mental health among students with a disability [2]. No studies were found that reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [2].

Which kinds work best

The interventions and outcome measures used by the studies in the 2025 review were all different, which the authors say makes direct comparison difficult [1]. Most interventions tried to improve children's skills but did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. A broader evidence map similarly found that studies focus on "fixing individuals", and that fewer studies address infrastructure or institutions and the social barriers to inclusion [3]. The review set out to ask which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability [1]. However, the evidence map calls for more studies on all types of impairment, including visual and hearing impairments [3].

Limits of the evidence

None of the studies in the 2025 review were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. None of the teacher development interventions in the Asia-Pacific map supported students with disabilities during emergency or crisis situations [2]. The broader evidence map reports a significant gap in study quality, especially for impact evaluations [3]. Over half of the impact evaluations in that map came from upper-middle income countries, with only nine from low-income countries [3]. The review's authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the studies received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. The review's low confidence in the evidence was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs, and did not always reflect multiple points of weakness within an individual study [1]. The sources say a twin-track approach is needed, one that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teacher skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them into school through system-level or school-level changes [1]. The finding says evaluation should focus on interventions that target both the individual with a disability and their broader environment, because such interventions are needed [1].

What holds up

What holds up is that programmes building learners' own skills, such as literacy and numeracy, show significant and large effects in LMICs, though from few studies. Programmes addressing conditions for inclusion show a moderately significant effect, and teacher training has some supporting evidence, much of it about attitudes or based on teachers' own reports. Which kinds work best cannot be settled from this evidence, because the studies used different interventions and measures and rarely tested school-level or system-level change. The review's authors themselves conclude that more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to, and successful engagement in, education [1].
