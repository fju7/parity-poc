Mobile tracking with health-worker prompts and patient reminders in primary care: mixed and often uncertain results

The question

The question is: “In primary health care, does using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improve the care people receive and their health?”

The short answer

A Cochrane review of health workers using digital tracking on mobile devices, combined with decision-support tools, targeted client communication, or both, found mixed results compared with usual care [1]. The review says these approaches may lead to benefits in some situations but may make little or no difference in others [1]. The review also says the full impact is often unknown because there are not enough studies, or the studies do not provide clear answers [1].

What the reviews found

What the interventions are

Digital tracking systems help healthcare workers collect and store patient information over time, and mobile devices like tablets and smartphones can make it easier to track patient information in settings that still rely on paper records [1]. Decision-support tools guide healthcare workers in analysing patient data and deciding on the best treatment [1]. Targeted client communication lets healthcare workers send patients messages such as appointment reminders, medication schedules, or health advice [1]. According to the source, digital tracking "may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use" [1]. The source states that "these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies" [1]. According to the source, "We are uncertain of the effect of this intervention on providers' adherence to recommended practice due to the very low certainty evidence (2 studies, 185 participants)" [2]. According to the source, "We are uncertain about the effectiveness of mobile phone‐based decision‐support tools on several outcomes, including adherence to recommended practice" [2]. None of the studies had a quality of care framework and focused only on specific health areas [2]. The source states: "We need well-designed research that takes a systems lens to assess these issues" [2]. According to the source, "The effect of TCCMD for most outcomes is uncertain" [3]. According to the source, there may be improvements for some outcomes using targeted communication, but these findings were of low certainty [3]. According to the source, high-quality, adequately powered trials and cost-effectiveness analyses are required to reliably ascertain the effects and relative benefits of TCCMD [3]. The source states that future studies should measure potential unintended consequences, such as partner violence or breaches of confidentiality [3]. According to the source, TCCMD may modestly increase service attendance overall (RR: 1.12, 95% CI 0.92‐1.35, low certainty evidence), however the confidence interval encompasses benefit and harm [4]. According to the source, tailored client communication via mobile devices (TCCMD) may increase service utilisation overall (RR: 1.71, 95% CI 0.67-4.38, low certainty evidence), however the confidence interval encompasses benefit and harm and there was considerable heterogeneity (I² = 72%), with mixed results according to type of service utilisation (STI/HIV testing, and VMMC) [4]. According to the source, TCCMD may improve some outcomes but the evidence is of low certainty [4]. The effect on most outcomes is uncertain/unknown due to very low certainty evidence or lack of evidence [4]. The source states that high quality, adequately powered trials and cost effectiveness analyses are required to reliably ascertain the effects and relative benefits of TCC delivered by mobile devices [4]. The source states that, given the sensitivity and stigma associated with sexual and reproductive health, future studies should measure unintended consequences, such as partner violence or breaches of confidentiality [4]. According to the source, the included studies were heterogeneous and the quality of the evidence therein is low to moderate [5]. The source states that there is a lack of information about health effects, adverse effects and harms, user evaluation of the intervention and user perceptions of its safety [5]. The source states that "the current evidence therefore still remains insufficient to conclusively inform policy decisions" [5]. According to the source, health outcomes, patients’ and healthcare providers’ evaluation and perceptions of the safety of the interventions, potential harms, and adverse effects of mobile phone messaging reminders should be assessed [5]. The source states that studies should report message content and timing in relation to the appointment [5]. According to the abstract of PMC5792697 (PMID 29343463), mobile phone usage has been rapidly increasing worldwide, and mHealth could efficiently deliver high-quality health care, but the evidence supporting its current effectiveness is still mixed [7]. Ten reviews were rated as low quality (AMSTAR score 0-4), seven were rated as moderate quality (AMSTAR score 5-8), and six were categorized as high quality (AMSTAR score 9-11) [7]. According to the source, although mHealth is growing in popularity, the evidence for efficacy is still limited [7]. According to the source, "In general, the methodological quality of the studies included in the systematic reviews is low" [7]. For some fields, its impact is not evident, the results are mixed, or no long-term studies exist [7]. According to the source, exceptions include the moderate quality evidence of improvement in asthma patients, attendance rates, and increased smoking abstinence rates [7]. According to the source, most studies were performed in high-income countries, implying that mHealth is still at an early stage of development in low-income countries [7].

The combined approach (tracking plus prompts and/or messages)

The review included 18 studies, 17 of them from low- and middle-income settings where electronic medical record systems are rare or just being rolled out [1]. Most of these studies focused on healthcare workers using mobile devices for maternal and child health or for managing chronic diseases [1]. One included trial, for example, gave community health workers (ASHAs) in India a mobile application to register and track pregnant women and newborns, with audio and visual prompts and algorithms to help identify danger signs and refer early [1]. Another, a cluster-randomised trial in rural China, evaluated an app on which village doctors collect, record and retrieve stroke patients' records [1]. For tracking combined with both decision support and targeted client communication, one study of 3324 participants gave moderate-certainty evidence on patient or client acceptability and satisfaction [1]. No study assessed resource use or unintended consequences [1].

Decision support on mobile devices alone

A separate Cochrane review of eight studies found that it is not known whether mobile decision-support tools make primary healthcare workers better at following recommended practice, because the evidence was of very low certainty [2]. That review found no clear pattern of a positive or negative effect on patients' behaviour or health, but the tools may slightly improve patients' satisfaction with medical information [2]. In one study of 2086 people with high cardiovascular risk, the tool probably increased the number taking antihypertensive medication but probably made little or no difference to smoking or systolic blood pressure (moderate certainty) [2]. In the same study, the tool may have increased the number taking prescribed aspirin (low certainty) [2]. The tools may make little or no difference to medication adherence or HbA1c among people with poorly controlled diabetes (one study, 185 participants) [2]. We are not sure whether the tools change the number of women giving birth in a hospital (one study, 799 participants); the review rates this evidence as moderate certainty in its summary table but low certainty in its detailed table [2]. That review found no studies on healthcare worker satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences such as harms [2].

Messages to patients alone

A review of targeted messages to pregnant women and parents found that some messages may improve some people's health and use of health services, while others may make little or no difference, and the evidence is mostly of low or very low certainty [3]. Compared with no messages, more parents may take their children to services such as vaccination appointments, but it is not known whether the messages improve children's health because the evidence is missing [3]. Pregnant women living with HIV who receive messages may go to slightly more antenatal care appointments, but the messages may make little or no difference to following antiretroviral treatment according to plan [3]. For appointment attendance, text message reminders improved attendance compared with no reminders (RR 1.10; 7 studies, 5841 participants; moderate certainty) and had a similar impact to phone call reminders [7].

Who may be left out

In a review of mobile phone messaging trials, two trials of text reminders for HIV antiretroviral care (in Mozambique and Brazil) excluded people who were illiterate, had no phone, or both; because only one trial stated that it provided phones, the authors judged it likely that all other trials excluded people without a personal mobile phone [4]. All the non-randomised studies included in the evidence were judged to have a high risk of bias [1]. Tracking combined with decision support may slightly increase postnatal home visits, complementary-feeding counselling, skin-to-skin care and early breastfeeding, while effects on several other practices (e.g. cord care, iron-folate intake, exclusive breastfeeding, Kangaroo Mother Care) are uncertain [1]. Tracking combined with targeted client communication may reduce low birthweight babies (RR 0.53) and, in stroke patients over 12 months, may reduce deaths (RR 0.52) and stroke hospitalisations (RR 0.45), while effects on stillbirths, neonatal and infant deaths, and HIV testing are uncertain [1]. Tracking combined with decision support and targeted client communication probably increases provider adherence to guidelines for antenatal anaemia, diabetes and hypertension (odds ratios about 1.45 to 1.88, one study each), but probably lowers adherence for abnormal foetal growth (OR 0.59, one study) [1]. Tracking with decision support plus targeted client communication (tracking + CDSS + TCC) may reduce deaths in patients with hypertension and diabetes (OR 0.61 [0.35, 1.06]) and new cardiovascular events in high-risk adults (OR 0.58 [0.42, 0.80]), but may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes in women receiving antenatal care (OR 0.99 [0.87, 1.12]) [1]. In one study of 3324 adults with hypertension or diabetes, tracking combined with decision support and targeted client communication may make little or no difference to HbA1c, total cholesterol, 10-year cardiovascular risk, tobacco use, alcohol use, or depression scores (PHQ-9) [1]. The effects on maternal or infant mortality, blood pressure control, body mass index, fasting glucose and quality of life are uncertain, and there may be little or no effect on missed early infant diagnosis visits (1 study, 1183 participants; OR 0.92 [0.63, 1.35]) [1]. One study of 3324 adults with hypertension or diabetes probably shows a slight increase in those reporting a slightly or much better change in quality of care (RR 1.02, 1.00 to 1.03), while effects on linkage to care are uncertain [1]. No studies evaluated time between presentation and appropriate management, timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences [1]. The certainty of evidence ranged from very low to moderate, and it was often downgraded for risk of bias and imprecision [2]. The effect of the intervention on patients' and clients' health behaviours such as smoking and treatment adherence is mixed, with substantial variation across outcomes for similar types of behaviour (2 studies, 2262 participants) [2]. The intervention probably makes little or no difference to smoking rates among people at risk of cardiovascular disease, but probably increases other desired patient behaviours, such as adherence to treatment [2]. The effect of the intervention on patients' or clients' health status and well-being is also mixed, based on 5 studies with 69,767 participants [2]. Digital clinical decision-support systems accessed by primary healthcare providers on mobile devices probably make little or no difference to some types of health outcomes, and we are uncertain about others, including maternal and neonatal deaths, because the evidence is very low-certainty [2]. The review found no studies that reported the time between presentation of an illness and appropriate management, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences [2]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices (TCCMD) may increase exclusive breastfeeding among pregnant and postpartum women in settings where it is less common (RR 1.30, 95% CI 1.06 to 1.59; low-certainty evidence), but has little or no effect where almost all women breastfeed (low-certainty evidence) [3]. In pregnant and postpartum women, targeted client communication via mobile devices (versus standard care) may increase antenatal appointment attendance (OR 1.54, 95% CI 0.80 to 2.96), but this is low-certainty evidence and the confidence interval includes both benefit and harm [3]. The intervention may increase skilled attendants at birth where lack of skilled attendance is common (though this differed by urban/rural residence), but may make no difference where almost all women already have one (OR 1.00, 95% CI 0.34 to 2.94; low-certainty evidence) [3]. The effects on maternal and neonatal mortality and morbidity are uncertain, because the certainty of the evidence was assessed as very low [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may have little or no effect on exclusive breastfeeding compared with non-digital communication such as pamphlets (RR 0.92, 95% CI 0.79 to 1.07; low-certainty evidence) [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices (TCCMD), compared with non-digital targeted client communication (e.g. pamphlets), may reduce any maternal health problem (RR 0.19, 95% CI 0.04 to 0.79) and any newborn health problem (RR 0.52, 95% CI 0.25 to 1.06) reported up to 10 days postpartum, based on low-certainty evidence, though the newborn interval includes both benefit and harm [3]. For pregnant and postpartum women, the effect of these interventions on health service use compared with non-digital communication (e.g. pamphlets) is unknown because there is a lack of studies [3]. For TCCMD versus digital non-targeted communication, effects on skilled attendance at birth are uncertain (very low-certainty evidence), and the intervention may make little or no difference to antenatal influenza vaccination attendance (RR 1.05, 95% CI 0.71 to 1.58; low-certainty evidence), though the CI includes both benefit and harm [3]. For pregnant and postpartum women living with HIV, TCCMD may make little or no difference to maternal and infant adherence to antiretroviral therapy compared with standard care (low-certainty evidence) [3]. Mobile phone reminders to patients (targeted client communication) may slightly increase antenatal care use (mean difference 1.5, 95% CI –0.36 to 3.36), but this is low-certainty evidence and the interval includes no effect [3]. The effect on the proportion of births occurring in a health facility is uncertain because the evidence is of very low certainty [3]. For health and well-being outcomes, the effect of the intervention on neonatal death or stillbirth and on infant HIV is uncertain, because the evidence is of very low certainty [3]. No studies reported on maternal mortality or morbidity, so the effect of these digital tracking and messaging interventions on those outcomes is unknown [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may increase infant ARV/prevention of mother-to-child transmission treatment adherence compared with digital non-targeted communication (RR 1.26, 95% CI 1.07 to 1.48; low-certainty evidence) [3]. The effect of targeted client communication via mobile devices on other outcomes is unknown, because of a lack of studies [3]. For parents of children under five years, no studies reported on correct treatment, nutritional, or health outcomes [3]. Based on 10 trials, TCCMD may modestly increase health service use such as vaccinations and HIV care compared with standard care (RR 1.21, 95% CI 1.08 to 1.34; low-certainty evidence), though effect estimates varied widely between studies [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may increase attendance for vaccinations compared with non-digital targeted client communication (RR 1.13, 95% CI 1.00 to 1.28; low-certainty evidence), and may make little or no difference to oral hygiene practices (low-certainty evidence) [3]. Compared with digital non-targeted communication, targeted communication via mobile devices (TCCMD) may reduce vaccination attendance, but the confidence interval includes both benefit and harm (RR 0.63, 95% CI 0.33 to 1.20; low-certainty evidence) [3]. No trials, in any population, reported data on unintended consequences, so possible harms of these targeted communication (TCCMD) interventions remain unassessed [3]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may modestly increase contraception use among adolescents compared with standard care (RR 1.19, 95% CI 1.05 to 1.35), based on low-certainty evidence [4]. The effects on condom use, antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence, and health service use are uncertain because the evidence is of very low certainty [4]. Interventions may increase health service use, such as attendance for STI/HIV testing (RR 1.61, 95% CI 1.08 to 2.40), but the evidence is low-certainty [4]. The intervention may reduce sexually transmitted infection rates (RR 0.61, 95% CI 0.28 to 1.33; low-certainty evidence), but the confidence interval includes both benefit and harm [4]. For targeted client communication via mobile devices (TCCMD) compared with digital non-targeted communication, the effects on abortion rates and on antiretroviral therapy (ART) adherence are unknown because of a lack of studies for this comparison [4]. We are uncertain whether tracking combined with decision support or messaging on mobile devices (TCCMD) results in unintended consequences, because there is a lack of evidence [4]. The effect of these mobile tracking and reminder interventions on condom use is uncertain [4]. Tracking combined with decision support or client messaging on mobile devices (TCCMD) may modestly increase adherence to antiretroviral therapy, but the evidence is low-certainty (RR 1.13, 95% CI 0.97 to 1.32; standardised mean difference 0.44, 95% CI -0.14 to 1.02) [4]. Targeted client communication via mobile devices may modestly increase health service utilisation (RR 1.17, 95% CI 1.04 to 1.31; low-certainty evidence), but heterogeneity was substantial (I² = 85%) and results varied by type of service [4]. For health outcomes, there may be little or no effect on CD4 count and a slight reduction in virological failure (RR 0.86, 95% CI 0.73 to 1.01), both based on low-certainty evidence [4]. Few studies reported on unintended consequences, so evidence about possible harms of these interventions is limited [4]. One study reported that a participant withdrew from the intervention because they felt it compromised their undisclosed HIV status [4]. Only one study reported generally that there were no adverse effects during the study period, and none of the studies reported in detail on specific adverse events such as loss of privacy, data misinterpretation, or message delivery failure [5].

What holds up

The most direct evidence comes from mostly low- and middle-income settings and shows mixed results: some benefits in some situations, and little or no difference in others [1]. Where the parts of the approach have been studied separately, mobile decision support for health workers has very uncertain effects on whether workers follow recommended practice, and patient-directed text reminders improve appointment attendance with moderate certainty [2, 7]. Effects on people's health are often unknown because evidence is missing or of low certainty [1, 3]. Costs and possible harms have not been assessed [1, 2]. More and better studies are needed before firm conclusions can be drawn [1, 2].
