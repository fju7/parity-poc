Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe this evidence as providing "emerging support" for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The strongest signals are for interventions that build learners' skills, with significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but these effects rest on a low number of studies [1]. The passages do not report which interventions work best for different types of disability, although the review set out to answer that question [1].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers without disabilities in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. The gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is described as largest in LMICs [1]. International directives place obligations on states to fulfil the right to education of people with disabilities, but one source states that evidence on which interventions are actually effective has not been established [1].

Interventions that build learners' skills

Of the 18 studies included in the review's analysis of intervention effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. By outcome, the effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant, and all of these effects were large [1]. The review cautions that these large effects are based on a low number of studies [1].

Interventions on the conditions for inclusion and on teachers

Included studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. An evidence and gap map of teacher professional development for disability inclusion in LMICs of the Asia-Pacific region found 50 studies [2]. That map found evidence from only 16 of 41 countries [2]. A significant number of the teacher interventions in that map focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability [2]. Some included studies were qualitative and reported positive perceptions [2]. In one study from Vietnam, for example, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2]. Another study described a one-day teacher workshop on an 'Equality and Non-discrimination Module' as having influenced students' perspectives of themselves and developed their social awareness skills [2]. No studies were found that reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [2]. Only three studies discussed interventions for supporting mental health among students with a disability [2].

Where the evidence is thin

Most interventions in the 2025 review tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. A broader evidence and gap map likewise found that interventions mostly try to change characteristics of the person with a disability rather than address structures such as the readiness of schools to include people with learning disabilities [3]. That map found fewer studies focused on improving infrastructure or institutions, and it calls the absence of evidence on what works to promote disability inclusion a very striking gap [3]. In the 2025 review, the interventions and outcome measures were all different, which made direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the review's studies was undertaken in humanitarian settings [1]. Few of the studies disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. Across disability interventions in LMICs more broadly, over half of the impact evaluations come from upper-middle income countries [3]. The same map notes a significant gap in study quality, especially for impact evaluations [3]. It also calls for more studies on all types of impairment, including visual and hearing impairments [3]. The 2025 review calls for larger samples, more rigorous study designs and standardised measures [1].

What holds up

It holds up that a range of interventions, mostly aimed at building the skills of individual learners with disabilities, improve educational outcomes in LMICs, with large and significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy [1]. This support is described as emerging, and it rests on few studies whose interventions and measures differ [1]. Interventions on the conditions for inclusion showed a moderately significant effect [1]. Evidence on teacher training comes largely from attitude-focused and qualitative studies [2]. These sources therefore cannot say whether systemic or school-level approaches work as well as or better than individual-level ones, because such approaches have been little studied [1, 3]. Nor do the passages answer which interventions work best for which type of disability.
