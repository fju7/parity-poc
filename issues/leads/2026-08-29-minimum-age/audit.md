# Source audit — Australia's Impact Analysis, OIA24-08210

Does each cited source support what it was cited for? Verbatim citing sentences
from the Impact Analysis, checked against the sources themselves.

**Status: three of eight audited.** All three findings are internal to public
documents and rest on quotations, not characterisation.

---

## FINDING 1 — one study, cited twice, as two

**What the Impact Analysis says**, in consecutive sentences:

> "Additionally, the US Surgeon General's Advisory on Social Media and Youth
> Mental Health states that 'adolescent social media use is predictive of a
> subsequent decrease in life satisfaction for certain developmental stages
> including for girls 11–13 years old and boys 14–15 years old.'"

> "Similarly, a UK Study published in 2022 of over 17,000 young people found
> that the most detrimental effects of high levels of social media use occur at
> ages 14-15 for boys and 11-13 for girls."

**What we checked.** The quoted Surgeon General sentence carries endnote 17. It
cites Orben, A., Przybylski, A. K., Blakemore, S. J., & Kievit, R. A. (2022),
"Windows of developmental sensitivity to social media", *Nature Communications*
13(1), 1649.

That is the UK study of 17,409 young people. **The two citations are the same
finding.**

**What this is and is not.** It is not dishonesty, and it is not a wrong figure.
Citing a primary study and an authoritative body that summarises it is ordinary
practice. What it is: the connective language — "Additionally", "Similarly" —
signals accumulating support from separate lines of evidence. A reader counting
evidence counts two. There is one study, cited once directly and once through a
body that is quoting it.

This is the class the source ledger calls `AGREEMENT_MISTAKEN_FOR_CORROBORATION`,
which we recorded against ourselves on 2026-08-29 after citing P-VERIFY as
confirming a claim P-VERIFY had inherited.

## FINDING 2 — a conditional finding stated as an established one

The Impact Analysis: the UK study "found that the most detrimental effects of
high levels of social media use occur at ages 14-15 for boys and 11-13 for
girls."

The paper's own account of what it can support:

> "Only if these assumptions are met can this observational study be said to
> capture the causal effects between social media and life satisfaction."

Five assumptions are listed, including that "there are no time-varying
unobserved confounders". And on magnitude:

> "The effect sizes of both paths were small in magnitude."

The coefficients are b = −0.11, −0.14 and −0.08 for girls at 11, 12 and 13, and
−0.10 and −0.18 for boys at 14 and 15.

**"Found that the most detrimental effects occur" states as established what the
paper frames as conditional on assumptions it does not claim to have met.** The
age windows are reported accurately. The epistemic status is not, and the
"small in magnitude" caveat does not travel.

The paper also contains no recommendation for an age-based restriction.

## FINDING 3 — a figure about the internet, used for social media

The Impact Analysis:

> "Research by eSafety found almost two-thirds of 14 to 17-year-olds have viewed
> extremely harmful content online including drug abuse, suicide or self-harm,
> as well as violent and gory material."

The scope word is **"online"**, and it is the Impact Analysis's own word. The
figure is not stated to be about social media, and it is cited in support of a
restriction that applies to social media specifically and to nothing else.

Whether the underlying eSafety research measured social media separately is not
established here and must be checked against eSafety's own report before this
finding is published.

## What the Surgeon General's Advisory says that the Impact Analysis does not quote

> "At this time, we do not yet have enough evidence to determine if social media
> is sufficiently safe for children and adolescents."

The Advisory recommends strengthening and enforcing age minimums as one element
of a package that also includes age-appropriate design standards, data privacy
requirements, digital literacy and research funding. **It does not recommend a
ban.**

Whether the Impact Analysis reports this qualifier anywhere is NOT YET CHECKED
and is the next thing to establish. The document does concede an evidence gap in
its own voice — "while there is a gap in the correlative evidence of the harms
of social media on young people, this may be in part due to major social media
services choosing not to share their own market and user impact research" — so
it is not silent on the point, and a fair audit has to say so.

# Audit completed — the remaining five

## FINDING 4 — the echo runs three deep, not two

Finding 1 established that the Surgeon General's Advisory and the "UK Study of
over 17,000 young people" are the same paper. The **Queensland Chief Health
Officer's Position Statement** is the third citation in the same chain: it
lists among its own primary sources "the US Surgeon General's 2023 advisory".

So of the sources the Impact Analysis assembles for its harm claim, three
trace back to Orben, Przybylski, Blakemore & Kievit (2022):

    Impact Analysis ─┬─> Orben et al. 2022                 (direct)
                     ├─> Surgeon General's Advisory ──────> Orben et al. 2022
                     └─> Queensland CHO statement ────────> Surgeon General ─> Orben

Three citations. One study. The document's connectives — "Additionally",
"Similarly", "also points to" — read as accumulation throughout.

## FINDING 5 — every source that recommends anything recommends DESIGN; the remedy was ACCESS

This is the finding the others were building toward, and it is the one that
matters. Four of the cited sources make recommendations. **Not one of them
recommends what Australia enacted.**

**Orben et al. (2022)** — the study underneath three of the citations — points
to *"targeted interventions"* grounded in the developmental changes that
underlie the sensitivity windows. No age-based restriction is proposed.

**The US Surgeon General's Advisory** recommends strengthening and enforcing
age minimums *as one element* of a package including age-appropriate design
standards, data privacy requirements, digital literacy and research funding. It
does not recommend a ban. And it says, in terms the Impact Analysis does not
quote:

> "At this time, we do not yet have enough evidence to determine if social media
> is sufficiently safe for children and adolescents."

**Common Sense Media** (Nesi, Mann & Robb 2023) is explicit that the remedy is
design rather than exclusion:

> "putting kids at the center of how platforms are designed may allow them to
> continue to use social media for all of its benefits"

**The Queensland Chief Health Officer** recommends *"parents limit access to
social media for children under the age of 14 years"* — a parental measure, at
14, not a legislated platform obligation at 16.

The Bills Digest named the mechanism precisely: *"algorithmic content
recommendation, endless scroll and other psychological manipulation techniques
which encourage near-endless engagement."* The Impact Analysis describes the
same mechanism — *"content recommendation systems that send users down 'rabbit
holes'"* — and assesses no regulatory option aimed at it.

**Every source points at how the products are built. The remedy chosen governs
who may open them.**

## FINDING 6 — a source's own evidence caveat, not carried

The Queensland CHO statement, cited for the harm claim, says of its own
findings:

> "These findings have not yet reached a consensus fully supported by
> peer-reviewed research. More work needs to be done."

> "The preliminary data, while not conclusive, suggests that there could be
> significant reasons for concern."

## FINDING 7 — a source cited for harm that documents benefit

Common Sense Media appears in the Impact Analysis only in a footnote, attached
to a harm claim. The report itself finds that girls regularly encounter helpful
mental health information; that *"nearly four in 10 girls who are struggling
socially offline"* are more likely to reap the benefits of the digital world;
that more than half of LGBTQ+ respondents connect with people who share their
identities; and that girls with depressive symptoms encounter *both* harmful and
helpful content at higher rates.

Those are the young people an access restriction removes from the platform.

## The two scope claims

**National Online Safety Survey 2022**, in the Impact Analysis's own words:
children are *"becoming exposed to **the internet** at an increasingly younger
age"*. **eSafety**: two-thirds of 14–17s have viewed extremely harmful content
*"**online**"*. eSafety's published research asks *"Have you ever seen or heard
any of the following things online?"* and its snapshot carries no breakdown by
platform type.

Both figures are about the internet. The remedy applies to social media and to
nothing else. The scope word is the government's own in both sentences.

## UNRESOLVED — and it must stay that way until read

**Wiyi Yani U Thangani (Women's Voices).** Cited for the observation that social
media is used as a tool for bullying, and that racism manifests as online hate.
We could not reach the report's treatment of social media or its recommendations
on digital access. It is a human rights report on Aboriginal and Torres Strait
Islander women and girls, and there is an obvious question — whether a document
concerned with the rights and inclusion of First Nations young people supports
restricting their access — **but we have not read it, and the question is not
the answer.** Nothing about this source goes on a page until somebody opens it.

Also unidentified: the specific eSafety report the Impact Analysis cited. The
survey we read ran December 2024 to February 2025, *after* the Impact Analysis
was published in October 2024, so it cannot be the cited work. Finding 3 rests
on the Impact Analysis's own scope word, not on that survey.
