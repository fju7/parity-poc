### CE-01 — role says SURVIVED (LOW confidence)

**We say.** Nobody has published a source-by-source audit of the evidence cited in Australia's Impact Analysis for the social media minimum age, checking whether each cited source supports what it was cited for.

**Does it change the conclusion.** The claim survives in the sense that no source-by-source audit was found. However, several published critiques come close: Fatt & Fardouly (2025, Child and Adolescent Mental Health), Archer (2025), UNSW (2025), and the Deakin University/Tandfonline paper (Fatt et al., 2026) all characterise the Impact Analysis as relying on only one academic source (Orben et al., 2022) and the US Surgeon General's remarks. These are characterisations of the evidence base's thinness, not systematic source-by-source audits checking whether each citation supports the specific proposition for which it was cited. If the piece's conclusion is that no such audit exists, that conclusion is not broken by these critiques — but the gap they identify is narrower than the claim implies.

### CE-02 — role says SURVIVED (LOW confidence)

**We say.** No commentator has noted that the US Surgeon General's Advisory and the UK study of 17,000 young people, both cited in Australia's Impact Analysis, are the same underlying study by Orben and colleagues.

**Does it change the conclusion.** The specific logical point — that the Surgeon General's Advisory and the UK 17,000-person study are the same underlying Orben data, meaning the Impact Analysis double-counts a single study — was not found in any source. The closest found is the Deakin University/Tandfonline paper (Fatt et al., 2026, doi:10.1080/10447318.2026.2695938), which notes the Impact Analysis cited 'only one source of academic evidence (Orben et al., Citation2022) and remarks by the US Surgeon General (Murthy, Citation2023) as the justification for the ban.' This identifies the thinness and names both citations, but does not make the further step of arguing that the Surgeon General's Advisory itself relies on Orben's work, making the two citations the same underlying data. If the piece's conclusion is that this specific double-counting observation has never been published, that conclusion is not broken — but the near-miss in the Deakin paper is close enough that a careful reader of that paper might infer the point. The claim would be most at risk in grey literature: parliamentary submissions, Substack posts, or expert blog commentary not indexed by standard search engines.
