### CE-01 — role says NARROWED (MEDIUM confidence)

**We say.** Nobody has published an analysis asking what standard of evidence has historically been required before restricting minors' access to a product — alcohol, tobacco, gambling, driving, firearms — and whether the evidence on social media harm meets that historical standard, as opposed to whether it matches the strength of the smoking-and-lung-cancer evidence.

**What breaks it.** A peer-reviewed PMC article (Frontiers in Psychiatry, 2020) explicitly compares the evidence and enforcement standards for age restrictions across alcohol, tobacco, gambling, and driving, and a 2025 MDPI narrative review of Australia's social media ban synthesises 26 academic studies and 15 policy documents to assess whether age-threshold evidence is sufficient — both papers perform the cross-domain evidentiary comparison the claim says nobody has done, though neither frames it as a formal 'historical standard' test against which social media evidence is measured.

**Citation.** https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychiatry/articles/10.3389/fpsyt.2020.601712/full ; https://www.mdpi.com/2673-4060/7/5/75

**Their words.** “As with tobacco and alcohol, age restrictions are only effective when rigorously enforced”

**We inherited this.** The original says: The claim appears to be the authors' own assertion, not inherited from a guideline or review. No narrower source wording was found.

**Does it change the conclusion.** The counterexamples narrow but do not fully break the claim. The Frontiers/PMC piece compares enforcement effectiveness across domains but does not reconstruct the historical evidentiary threshold that was actually required before each restriction was enacted and then ask whether social media evidence clears that bar. The MDPI review finds the empirical findings 'remain inconclusive and context-dependent' but does not frame this as a historical-standard comparison. The specific analytical move the claim describes — 'what quantum of evidence did regulators historically demand, and does social media evidence meet it?' — has not been published as a standalone analysis. The claim is therefore true in its strongest form (no paper does exactly that framing) but false in its weakest form (papers do compare evidence across domains). The piece's broader conclusion — that this gap in the literature exists — survives, but the sentence as written ('nobody has published an analysis asking…') is too absolute; the question has been partially asked, just not in the precise framing described.

### CE-02 — role says BROKEN (HIGH confidence)

**We say.** No jurisdiction that has enacted a minors' social-media access restriction has published the evidentiary basis it relied on.

**What breaks it.** Australia's Online Safety Amendment (Social Media Minimum Age) Act 2024 is accompanied by a publicly available Explanatory Memorandum (tabled in Parliament, accessible at parlinfo.aph.gov.au) that sets out the evidentiary basis relied on, and a separately published Impact Analysis Equivalent prepared with the Office of Impact Analysis (OIA24-08210), accessible at oia.pmc.gov.au — both are public documents specifying the evidence the government relied on.

**Citation.** https://parlinfo.aph.gov.au/parlInfo/search/display/display.w3p;query=Id:%22legislation/ems/r7284_ems_b9c134ac-a19a-47b2-9879-b03dda6e3c1a%22 ; https://www.loc.gov/item/global-legal-monitor/2024-12-08/australia-social-media-banned-for-children-under-16/

**Their words.** “there is clear evidence about the harms young people experience on these platforms, particularly as a result of harmful features such as algorithms”

**We inherited this.** The original says: The claim appears to be the authors' own assertion. No narrower source wording was found. It may have been inherited from commentary on US state laws (where regulatory impact statements are less common), but it was stated universally.

**Does it change the conclusion.** This breaks the sentence outright. Australia — the first and most prominent jurisdiction to enact such a restriction — published both an Explanatory Memorandum and a formal Impact Analysis Equivalent, both of which are public and set out the evidentiary basis relied on. Critics (including a peer-reviewed PMC paper) argue that evidentiary basis is inadequate or inconclusive, but the claim is that no jurisdiction has *published* its basis — and Australia plainly has. The piece's broader argument (that the evidence base is weak or unexamined) may still hold, but this specific sentence — 'no jurisdiction has published the evidentiary basis' — is false. A counterexample that undermines the sentence but not necessarily the conclusion still breaks the sentence.
