# Reading notes — Australia's minimum age law

**These are reading notes, not findings.** Every passage below reached us
through a fetch-and-summarise of the source, not through reading the document
end to end. Nothing here is quotable until the passage is confirmed against the
document text. The standing rule that produced "Jacot and colleagues" applies
with full force: a summary of a document is not the document.

---

## 1. Bills Digest No. 39, 2024–25

Nell Fraser and Owen Griffiths, Parliamentary Library, 25 November 2024.
Read via a mirrored PDF; the official aph.gov.au copy returned 403 and must be
obtained before anything is quoted.

Prepared **for legislators, before the vote**, and it says:

> "There is limited evidence to suggest that a blanket ban to prohibit children
> from using social media is the most advantageous solution to addressing
> online harms."

It records that the relevant Committee **declined to recommend a minimum age**,
recommending other measures instead; that over 140 researchers and academics
signed a letter arguing a ban is "too blunt an instrument to address risks
effectively"; and it quotes the American Psychological Association that using
social media "is not inherently beneficial or harmful to young people".

On the harm mechanism it is specific, and this is the part that matters:

> "algorithmic content recommendation, endless scroll and other psychological
> manipulation techniques which encourage near-endless engagement"

## 2. Impact Analysis — Social Media Age Limit (OIA24-08210)

October 2024, Office of Impact Analysis.

**What it cites.** An enumerable list, which is what makes the audit possible:
the National Online Safety Survey 2022; eSafety research (almost two-thirds of
14–17s have viewed extremely harmful content); the US Surgeon General's
Advisory; a UK study of 17,000+ young people on age windows of sensitivity;
Common Sense Media; studies on body dissatisfaction and eating disorders; the
Queensland Chief Health Officer's position statement; and the Wiyi Yani U
Thangani report.

**What it concedes.** That there is a gap in the evidence — and attributes it:

> "while there is a gap in the correlative evidence of the harms of social media
> on young people, this may be in part due to major social media services
> choosing not to share their own market and user impact research"

That sentence is doing a great deal of work and connects directly to what the
US states allege about concealed internal research.

**What it does not do — three things, all checkable.**

1. It describes algorithmic harm — "content recommendation systems that send
   users down 'rabbit holes'" — and **assesses no regulatory alternative
   aimed at it.** The diagnosis names design; the remedy is an age gate.
2. It offers **no empirical estimate of whether an age limit would work.**
3. It **names the failure modes** — circumvention by VPN, migration to
   unregulated platforms — without testing their likelihood.

## 3. What happened next

The BMJ, 25 June 2026: over 85% of under-16s still using restricted platforms
three months in; 15–19% on fake accounts; 6–11% via private browsers. The
impact analysis named circumvention as a risk and did not estimate it. The
first measurement found circumvention.

And the study is itself being carried further than it goes. Dr Victoria Baines
states that the press release "slightly overstates the findings" and omits the
pre-ban baseline; Prof Matt Williams says it "should not be read as proof that
age-based restrictions cannot work."

## The shape this makes

Not "does social media harm teenagers", which is unbounded and which Rutar has
already addressed on the evidence-comparison question. Instead:

**A government named engagement-optimising design as the harm, chose an age
gate as the remedy, assessed no alternative aimed at the mechanism it had
named, estimated no effectiveness, listed the failure modes, and got them.
Then the first study of what happened was reported as more conclusive than it
is.**

Every element is a public document. The evidence base is enumerable — eight or
so cited sources, each checkable against what it actually says.
