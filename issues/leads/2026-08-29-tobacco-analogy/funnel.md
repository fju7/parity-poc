# Candidate — the tobacco analogy

Run through the funnel on 29 August 2026. **Not an issue.** A candidate that has
passed the entry test, with the kill condition written down and one prior-art
check unresolved.

---

## Stage 1 — is a claim in circulation, and is it carried?

Yes, and it is unusually well documented.

Meta settled the state attorneys general's child-safety claims on 26 August
2026. Coverage is heavy and immediate: CNN, NBC, Axios, France 24, TechPolicy
Press, Deseret. The trial itself ran through August, with the states alleging
that Meta hid internal research on teen addiction.

The claim we would examine is **not** the settlement figure. Outlets report it
as $16bn, $17bn and "up to $18bn" — a real discrepancy, but one already widely
noted, and a piece about it would be reporting a thing that is already being
reported.

The claim in circulation is the argument the case is being used to carry:

> **Social media companies should be treated like tobacco and alcohol companies
> with respect to the availability of their products to children.**

That argument is explicit, named, and has a named opposition already in print:
the American Enterprise Institute's "Social Media Addiction Lawsuits: The
Deceptively Flawed Tobacco Analogy", TechFreedom's "Regulating Big Tech Like
Big Tobacco: Same Playbook, Same Mistakes", and a Notre Dame Law Review article
on regulating social media like big tobacco.

## Stage 2 — the asymmetry

**An analogy is an empirical claim wearing rhetorical clothes.** "Treat X like
tobacco" asserts that the two evidentiary situations are alike. The tobacco
case rested on three legs:

1. **Internal documents** showing the industry knew of harm and concealed it.
2. **Epidemiology** with very large effect sizes and clear dose-response.
3. **A biological mechanism** that made causation intelligible.

The social media case is being argued as though all three transfer. On the
first, the states allege exactly this and the trial has produced unsealed
material — the parallel may well hold. On the second, the effect sizes are
small and the literature is actively contested, with a live 2026 methodological
exchange in print. On the third, the mechanism is disputed and behavioural
addiction has no settled psychiatric status.

**So the analogy has three legs and they are in three different conditions, and
nobody is separating them.** That is the gap: not that the analogy is wrong,
but that it travels as a single claim when it is three claims of very different
evidentiary strength.

Crucially, the most prominent published critique does **not** do this. AEI's
argument is conceptual and categorical — First Amendment status, physical
versus mental harm, offsetting benefits, whether behavioural addiction is a
real category. Those are arguments about whether the products are alike. None
of them is an argument about whether the **evidence** is alike, which is the
question a reader actually has.

## Stage 3 — the kill condition, written before drafting

This candidate dies if any of the following is true:

- **The evidentiary comparison has already been done carefully and is in
  circulation.** One unresolved hit: a Substack post, "You can't analogize the
  evidence on social media harms to that on smoking!", which from its title
  appears to do exactly this. **We could not open it — 429 rate limit — and it
  has not been read.** It must be read before a word is drafted. If it does the
  job well, this candidate is either dead or becomes a piece that cites it
  rather than one that claims the ground.
- **The three legs turn out to be in the same condition** — if the unsealed
  documents show knowledge and concealment comparable to the tobacco record
  *and* the epidemiology is comparable in strength, the analogy holds and there
  is no story.
- **The alcohol leg collapses the framing.** Age restriction is the actual
  policy question, and the minimum-legal-drinking-age literature is large and
  reasonably settled. If age restriction turns out to be well supported on its
  own terms, the tobacco parallel is doing no work and the piece is about
  something else.

## Stage 4 — source accessibility

**Strong, and this is the reason to prefer this candidate over another
oncology subject.** Issue two was decided by a document no automated check was
permitted to read, and six adverse claims about it published unchecked.

Here: state complaints and court filings are public; the tobacco evidentiary
record is exceptionally well preserved and open, including the industry
documents archive and the Surgeon General reports; the contested psychology is
in journals with open commentary in *Nature* and *Science*; the alcohol
age-restriction literature is open. The AEI and TechFreedom critiques are free
to read.

## Stage 5 — is the evidence base bounded?

**Partly, and this is the live risk.** The comparison is bounded: the tobacco
evidentiary record is a specific historical thing. The social media literature
is not bounded, and unbounded literatures are what produced "no study finds a
difference" and "no randomised trial has ever compared them".

**Mitigation, and it is a scoping decision made now rather than discovered
later:** the piece is about whether the *analogy's evidentiary legs* transfer.
It is not about whether social media harms teenagers. Any sentence that answers
the second question is out of scope, and the counterexample hunter runs on
every universal claim before drafting rather than after.

## Stage 6 — what this piece must not do

This is a contested political question and What Holds Up assesses evidence, not
policy. The piece can say whether the evidentiary parallel holds. It cannot say
whether social media should be age-restricted — that turns on values, on
rights, and on trade-offs no evidence assessment settles, and the publication's
own standard already says so in every issue: *this is a statement about
evidence and not a recommendation*.

A version of this piece that arrives at a policy conclusion has failed, whatever
the conclusion is.

## Verdict

**Passes the entry test, conditional on the prior-art check.** Read the Substack
post first. If it has done this, cite it and find another candidate — that is
the Tanguy lesson applied before publication rather than after.
