Planning for Climate Change: What the Evidence Measures

The question is whether taking climate change into account when designing and planning towns, rural areas and infrastructure makes them better able to cope and improves people’s wellbeing. It is widely taken for granted that it does. In our reading, the reviews and assessment we read do not show that it fails; they show that the benefit is mostly assumed rather than measured, that measured results are few and narrow, and that evidence about people’s wellbeing is thinner still.

How much of the evidence measures the outcome

Very little, in the largest stocktake of adaptation that has actually been carried out.

In 2021 Berrang-Ford and colleagues screened more than 48,000 articles and synthesised 1,682 that documented adaptation to climate change actually being carried out.

They found the documented adaptations largely fragmented, local and incremental, with negligible evidence of risk reduction outcomes.

In their coding, 62% of papers (1,044) provided implicit or explicit evidence that adaptation activities were reducing risk or vulnerability, but only 58 papers (3.4%) indicated that risk reduction outcomes had been formally assessed after implementation.

Of those, 30 papers, 1.8% of all the academic studies in their database, presented primary evidence of risk reduction.

The authors say the rest mostly assumed risk reduction from secondary evidence or theories of change.

They also caution that an absence of evidence in the academic literature does not necessarily mean no risk reduction is taking place, and that grey literature may hold more.

This stocktake covers adaptation of every kind, not planning alone; we read it for what it says about how outcomes are measured, not as a count of planning studies.

What the IPCC assessed about planning itself

Rarely implemented; limited evidence that it works where it is; evaluation slow to arrive.

The IPCC’s 2022 chapter on cities, settlements and key infrastructure finds limited implementation of zoning and land use measures for climate adaptation from cities across diverse contexts, which it rates as robust evidence with high agreement.

It cites limited evidence, from Stuttgart and Berlin, Greater Manchester and Colombo, that risk reduction through zoning and land use can protect and expand green infrastructure and soft land cover to alleviate pluvial flooding and decrease the urban heat island effect.

It finds, with high confidence, that adaptation through zoning and land use is more effective when combined with other planning measures, such as ecosystem-based adaptation, community-based adaptation, and built form regulations and codes.

It also records that property values have risen in areas protected from risks such as flooding, which may trigger spatial inequity leading to climate gentrification.

And it finds, with medium confidence, that slow uptake of monitoring and evaluation frameworks constrains the potential for climate resilient urban development, with a lack of agreement on metrics to measure urban adaptation investment, impacts and outcomes.

One measured result, and what it covers

Flood claims fell, but only where communities had gone furthest.

Frimpong and colleagues studied all 675 communities in Alabama and Mississippi taking part in the US National Flood Insurance Program with at least one policy in force between 1998 and 2014, comparing those in the Community Rating System, which credits floodplain management beyond the program’s minimum standards, with those outside it.

The system grades communities from Class 9, entry level, to Class 1, the highest.

They found taking part raised flood insurance uptake, but reduced flood damage claims were limited to Class 5 communities.

There, the annual average sum of flood damage claims was $125,000 lower than in communities outside the system, a 5.8 percent reduction from the mean flood claim of $2.1 million.

This scheme manages flood risk as it stands, not climate change specifically, and it covers two states; it shows that this kind of planning can be measured against outcomes, not that climate-informed planning in general improves them.

People’s wellbeing

Some reported benefits, a paucity of evidence, and a likely bias toward reporting good news.

Scheelbeek and colleagues reviewed what is reported about the effects on health of climate change adaptation responses in low- and middle-income countries, drawing on the same database of 1,682 publications.

They identified 99 studies reporting 1,117 outcomes from 66 countries, of which only two were ex ante formal evaluations of adaptation responses.

Reported health outcomes included reduction in infectious disease incidence, improved access to water and sanitation and improved food security, while all-cause mortality was rarely reported.

Only 3.5% of the 1,117 reported health outcomes were negative, and the authors judge reporting and publication bias likely.

They conclude that there is some evidence adaptation responses may benefit health, but that the overall paucity of evidence is concerning.

Because this review drew its studies from the same database as the stocktake, the two are not independent confirmations of each other, and it covers adaptation of every kind in low- and middle-income countries rather than planning in particular.

What this adds up to

Not proven, not disproven: mostly unmeasured.

On what we read, the belief that planning for climate change makes places cope better and leaves people better off is a reasonable expectation rather than a demonstrated result.

The sources show plausible mechanisms and a few measured effects on hazards, such as cooler and less flood-prone ground where green cover is protected and lower flood claims where floodplain management goes furthest, but we found no controlled evaluation showing that climate-informed planning improves both how a place copes and how its people fare.

For someone deciding what to require in a local plan, the assessment we read supports combining zoning with other measures and watching who gains and who is displaced, and the stocktake supports building in a way to measure whether a measure has worked.

What we did not read

We hold four documents: two systematic reviews, one assessment chapter and one evaluation.

Of the IPCC chapter, we drew on its executive summary and its section on land use planning, and did not read the rest.

We did not search the grey literature, which the stocktake’s authors say may hold more evidence of risk reduction, and we did not read the primary studies of heat and green infrastructure that the IPCC chapter cites.

We hold the stocktake as the authors’ accepted manuscript, which carries the repository’s notice that it may differ from the published version, and the flood study as the 2019 working paper rather than its journal version.
