Do education programmes for children with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

Many programmes aimed at individual children with disabilities improved the skills they were measured on, such as reading, writing and arithmetic, but the studies are small and mostly low in rigour, none of them measured whether children got into school or stayed there, and the evidence cannot yet say which kinds of programme work best.

The main review

The main source for this answer is a Campbell systematic review published in February 2025 [1].

Its authors searched for studies up to March 2022 [1].

Twenty-eight studies were included in the review [1].

Of the 28 studies, four were randomised trials, 16 were controlled before-and-after studies and eight were uncontrolled before-and-after studies [1].

Most of them (25) targeted children with disabilities, two targeted family members and three targeted service providers [1].

People with intellectual or learning and developmental impairments were the group most often targeted: in 17 studies by the abstract's count, and in 16 by the count in its results section [1].

The most common kind of programme was support for educational attainment, such as a reading comprehension programme for children with dyslexia [1].

The second most common kind made learning environments more accessible, for instance programmes to improve social skills or to reduce the victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1].

Thirteen interventions were run in inclusive mainstream settings, 12 in segregated or specialised settings, and 3 did not report their setting [1].

The review's discussion says most interventions were delivered by specialists, therapists or intervention coaches, and a minority by community members or existing staff, but its results section counts 11 delivered by specialists, therapists or intervention coaches, 10 by community members or existing staff and 7 where it was unclear who delivered them [1].

What the measured effects show

Across 22 studies, the pooled effect of the programmes on skills for learning was large (d = 2.10, from 0.91 to 3.29), with high heterogeneity (I² = 99%) and marginally significant publication bias [1].

The abstract counts the analysis differently: among the 18 studies included in its analysis of effects on skills for learning, 12 interventions had a significant effect [1].

By outcome, the review's table reports large effects on numeracy (d = 4.60, two studies), literacy (d = 2.46, four studies), handwriting (d = 4.86, two studies) and cognitive skills including attention and memory (d = 3.38, four studies) [1].

The review says all these effects are large but are based on a low number of studies [1].

The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1].

The review is not consistent on two of these outcomes: its abstract says the effects on cognitive skills and numeracy are significant, while a passage in its results says the effect on cognitive skills and numeracy is insignificant.

Programmes concerned with the conditions for including people with disabilities in education had a moderate pooled effect (d = 0.35, from 0.12 to 0.58, four studies), based on a low number of studies with high heterogeneity [1].

One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1].

All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings, largely because of low-rigour study designs [1].

No study in the review looked at attendance, enrolment or completion of education [1].

No study reported qualifications gained, transition to higher levels of education, or education-related quality of life [1].

Which kinds work best

The review says that more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious, and how best to deliver them [1].

It gives two reasons: the broad variety of interventions and outcomes assessed, and the limited number of high-quality randomised and quasi-experimental studies [1].

The interventions and outcome measures used by the included studies were all different, which the review says makes direct comparison difficult [1].

The review did not find any systematic differences between the effectiveness of interventions delivered in mainstream settings and in segregated or specialised settings, which it says was likely because most interventions reported positive findings [1].

The authors conclude that children with disabilities fall behind in educational outcomes because current school systems are not set up to teach children with different impairment types [1].

They conclude that there is no single intervention that solves the problem for this group, and that a twin-track approach is needed, which addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also ensures they are included in mainstream activities, for example through improving the skills of teachers and the accessibility of the classroom [1].

They add that most interventions in the review targeted individual children's functioning, skills and competencies, and did not focus on bringing these children into mainstream school through system-level or school-level changes [1].

They call for evaluations of interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment [1].

For teacher training, a separate evidence and gap map of in-service teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries found 50 studies, from 16 of the region's 41 low- and middle-income countries [2].

A majority of those interventions focused on changing teachers' attitudes towards including students with disabilities and their understanding of different forms of disability [2].

Most of the studies in that map were observational studies and programme reports, and only a few included effectiveness data [2].

Two of its studies used an experimental design with random allocation, and four reported improvements in student achievement, including test scores [2].

The map's authors identified two main gaps in research about professional learning: only three studies reported interventions aimed at supporting mental health among students with a disability, and no studies were found that reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour, although the map's results section separately states that fourteen studies report outcomes for enabling positive student behaviour [2].

Its authors say that more rigorous studies with larger samples that report effectiveness data, particularly on outcomes for children with disabilities, are still required [2].

They say the map illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability-inclusive teacher professional development in these countries, including programmes that are ongoing or are components of large education sector programmes [2].

What we read, and its limits

We hold the full text of Hunt 2025 systematic review [1].

Of Hunt 2025 systematic review [1], we read: the abstract; the plain language summary; section 5, the results, with Tables 4 to 14; section 6, the discussion; section 7, the authors' conclusions.

We hold the full text of Ahmed 2022 evidence and gap map [2].

Of Ahmed 2022 evidence and gap map [2], we read: the abstract; the plain language summary; section 5, the results; section 6, the discussion; section 7, the authors' conclusions.

We hold the full text of Ahmed 2022 map corrigendum [3].

We searched: Europe PMC, for systematic reviews and evidence and gap maps of programmes for the education of children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries.

We did not search: trial registries; impact evaluations published after the main review's search ended in March 2022; studies not in English; reports from governments, UN agencies and non-governmental organisations that are not in Europe PMC.
