What Holds Up — reliability-study issue WHU-S02, not for publication

Does it matter how CBT is given to a depressed teenager?

Study draft, 22 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

Most of the formats that have been tested did better than no treatment by the end of treatment, and the trials have not found any format clearly better than another. But not finding a difference is not the same as showing that the formats are equal: a network meta-analysis of 62 trials says none was shown to be equivalent to another, several comparisons rest on few or no head-to-head trials, and the benefits had shrunk by six to twelve months. England's national guideline still treats the formats differently: for 12- to 18-year-olds it offers digital or group options first for mild depression and individual CBT for moderate to severe depression.

What people have been told

A University of East Anglia news release said that CBT delivered through apps, online software, video games, phone calls and video conferencing "is just as effective as face-to-face CBT" for treating anxiety and depression in children and young people. In 2019 pharmaphorum reported that NICE, in a draft guideline, had recommended digital CBT as a first-line treatment for children and young people with mild depression.

What the largest review found

A Campbell systematic review published in 2024 sorted CBT for adolescents into five formats: group CBT, individual CBT, remote CBT, guided self-help, and unguided self-help. It included randomised trials of young people aged 10 to 19 with clinically relevant symptoms or a diagnosis of depression. Its main analysis of symptoms at the end of treatment included 62 of those trials, with 6,435 participants. Every format except remote CBT reduced depressive symptoms more than no intervention did. Fewer formats did better than treatment as usual, and none did better than a placebo condition. The review found no format clearly better than another, and none shown to be equivalent to another. The certainty of those comparisons ranged from low to very low. Guided self-help ranked highest and unguided self-help lowest, but the authors rated their certainty in that ranking as very low.

Expressed on the Beck Depression Inventory, the estimated differences between formats ranged from 0.2 to 6.8 points, against a minimum clinically important difference that the authors say is likely to fall between 3 and 6 points. Every one of those between-format estimates had a confidence interval that included no difference, and each was rated low or very low certainty.

What the trials did not test

No trial in the review compared group CBT with individual CBT directly: the network's estimate for that comparison, a standardised mean difference of 0.02 (95% CI −0.65 to 0.68), is indirect and was rated very low certainty. Among the included trials, guided self-help was never compared directly with another active treatment. It was also tested only in trials with the oldest adolescents, aged 16 to 19. Remote CBT was evaluated in only one included study, with the youngest adolescents, aged 10 to 13. That study's comparison of remote with individual CBT involved 28 participants and was rated very low certainty. When the review left out interactive interventions, its estimate for guided self-help against no intervention shrank from −1.29 to −0.77.

Six to twelve months on

Nineteen trials, with 3,260 participants, measured symptoms 6 to 12 months later, and none of them tested guided self-help or remote CBT. At that point effects were generally smaller, no format did better than no intervention, and no format was clearly better than another, although unguided self-help and group CBT both did better than treatment as usual. Unguided self-help, the lowest-ranked format at the end of treatment, ranked highest at follow-up. On drop-out, which the review used as a stand-in for acceptability, there was no clear evidence that any format was more acceptable than another.

Technology against face-to-face

The University of East Anglia review behind the news release found seven trials that compared technology-assisted CBT with face-to-face CBT for depression in children and young people. For depression, the pooled standardised mean difference was 0.12 (95% CI −0.42 to 0.46). Its authors concluded that their study suggests technology-assisted CBT is non-inferior to face-to-face CBT for depression in this age group, while acknowledging that only two of the ten included articles met all criteria to be considered 'high quality' and that the number of studies in the meta-analyses was relatively small. They also noted that the number of studies was relatively small. And they grouped a diverse set of interventions, from a therapist on the phone to an online programme, under the one heading of technology-assisted CBT. A confidence interval that spans differences in both directions shows that no difference was detected, not that the two formats were shown to match.

A 2022 review of computerised CBT for adolescents found a small effect on depression against control groups (standardised mean difference −0.23, 95% CI −0.39 to −0.07). The effect was substantially smaller and not significant when the control groups were limited to those with an active treatment component. In that comparison, two trials gave a standardised mean difference of −0.17 (95% CI −0.49 to 0.14), which the authors interpreted as suggesting cCBT may be comparable with alternative, active interventions.

In New Zealand's SPARX trial, 187 adolescents aged 12 to 19 who were seeking help for depressive symptoms, had no major risk of self-harm and were judged by their clinicians to need treatment were randomised to a computerised CBT programme or to treatment as usual. Treatment as usual comprised primarily face-to-face counselling delivered by trained counsellors and clinical psychologists. The technology-assisted review noted that this trial's comparison arm seemed to have provided counselling and not face-to-face CBT. In the per-protocol analysis, SPARX was not inferior to treatment as usual. In the per-protocol analysis (n=143 of 187 randomised), scores on the Children's Depression Rating Scale–Revised fell by 10.32 points with SPARX and 7.59 with treatment as usual (difference 2.73, 95% CI −0.31 to 5.77; P=0.079), and remission was significantly more common with SPARX (43.7% against 26.4%; difference 17.3%, 95% CI 1.6% to 31.8%; P=0.030).

How much therapy helps at all

A 2023 meta-analysis of psychotherapies for depression in children and adolescents, covering CBT and other therapies, found response rates of 37% in individual formats, 41% in group formats and 41% in guided self-help and other formats. None of its subgroup analyses found significant differences in the response rates of psychotherapies, though for control groups the diagnosis (p=0.04) and risk-of-bias (p=0.01) subgroups differed significantly, and for therapy-versus-control relative risks the type of control condition was significant (p=0.02). Overall, 39% of young people responded to therapy, against 24% in control conditions. Its authors noted that more than 60% of young people receiving therapy did not respond.

What the guideline says

For 12- to 18-year-olds with mild depression continuing after 2 weeks of watchful waiting, and without significant comorbid problems or active suicidal ideas or plans, England's NICE guideline says to offer a choice of digital CBT, group CBT, group non-directive supportive therapy or group interpersonal psychotherapy for a limited period of approximately 2 to 3 months. If those options would not meet the young person's clinical needs or are unsuitable for their circumstances, it says to consider attachment-based family therapy or individual CBT. For 12- to 18-year-olds with moderate to severe depression, it says to offer individual CBT for at least 3 months.

How the coverage did

The news release's claim that the technology is just as effective rests on two sets of trials: seven anxiety trials, whose pooled result had a confidence interval from −0.10 to 0.23, and seven depression trials, whose pooled result had a confidence interval from −0.42 to 0.46. The release also quoted its lead author as saying that technology-assisted CBT can be a viable alternative to traditional face-to-face CBT for some children and young people. pharmaphorum was reporting a draft; the published guideline lists digital and group options first only for mild depression, and offers individual CBT to 12- to 18-year-olds with moderate to severe depression (for 5- to 11-year-olds it is one of several options to consider).

What a reasonable person can conclude

For a teenager with mild depression, the trials give no clear reason to prefer one-to-one CBT over a group or a self-help programme, and the guideline offers digital and group options first. What the evidence does not support is the stronger claim that the formats are interchangeable: most were shown to do better than no treatment at the end of treatment (none did at 6 to 12 months), not shown to match one another, and the remote and guided options rest on the fewest trials and the narrowest age ranges. Many of the 68 trials in the review were conducted in schools (28) or universities (6), whereas for 12- to 18-year-olds with moderate to severe depression the guideline recommends offering individual CBT for at least 3 months.
