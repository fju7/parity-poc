Carbon dioxide removal and net zero: a necessary complement to emissions cuts, not a substitute

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

Rapid emissions reductions, including cuts in deforestation-based land emissions, are described as the dominant source of global climate mitigation potential in the coming decades, but carbon dioxide removal (CDR) will also have an important role to play [3]. The IPCC assessment finds that CDR is a necessary element to achieve net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions, both globally and nationally, by counterbalancing residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors, and that it is a key element in scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower by 2100 (robust evidence, high agreement) [1]. Its role is described as shifting over time: further reducing net emissions in the near term, counterbalancing residual emissions to help reach net zero in the mid term, and achieving and sustaining net-negative emissions in the long term [1]. The sources are equally clear that CDR is not a substitute for emissions reductions [4].

What the sources report

What CDR is

CDR refers to a cluster of technologies, practices and approaches that remove carbon dioxide from the atmosphere and durably store the carbon in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in products [1]. Some methods, such as afforestation, improved forest management, wetland restoration and soil carbon sequestration, have been practised for decades to millennia, although not necessarily with the intention of removing carbon [1]. Methods such as direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS), bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and enhanced weathering are novel, and their demonstration and deployment are limited in scale [1]. CDR methods differ in removal process, timescale of carbon storage, technological maturity, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, adverse side effects and governance requirements [1].

Roles across the decades

One review states that CDR would potentially allow a global temperature exceedance to be reversed in the long term, but would not compensate for the impacts of overshoot [4]. Net zero CO2 emissions will be achieved earlier than net zero GHG emissions, and because residual non-CO2 emissions are expected to be significant, this time-lag could reach one to several decades [1]. Counterbalancing residual non-CO2 emissions with CO2 removals will lead to net-negative CO2 emissions at the time of net zero GHG emissions [1]. Globally, net zero might involve some countries already reaching net-negative levels while others are given more time [1].

Scale today and what scenarios call for

Taken together, current CDR approximates 2 GtCO2 per year, or about 3% of current global net greenhouse gas emissions [4]. Even low estimates of CDR requirements by mid-century imply several gigatons of removals [4]. A 2018 review of then-available scenarios found that pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C reach net zero CO2 globally between 2046 and 2056, with NETs deployment already between 5 and 15 GtCO2 per year by 2050 in most scenarios [6]. The same review gives best estimates for sustainable global potentials in 2050 of 0.5–3.6 GtCO2 per year for afforestation and reforestation, 0.5–5 for BECCS, 0.5–2 for biochar, 2–4 for enhanced weathering, 0.5–5 for DACCS, and up to 5 for soil carbon sequestration [6]. The review concludes that it is unlikely that any single one of the carbon-removal approaches it assesses (afforestation and reforestation, BECCS, biochar, enhanced weathering, DACCS and soil carbon sequestration) 'will be able to sustainably meet the rates of carbon uptake described in integrated assessment pathways consistent with 1.5 °C of global warming' [6]. Scenarios suggest an 8:2 ratio of effort between reducing CO2 emissions and scaling CO2 removals by mid-century, and a precautionary approach may suggest even more focus on reductions [4].

National plans fall short of scenario levels

A 2024 analysis identifies a 'CDR gap': CDR efforts proposed by countries fall short of those in integrated assessment model scenarios that limit warming to 1.5°C [3]. The most ambitious national proposals are close to levels in a low-energy-demand scenario with the most limited CDR scaling and aggressive near-term emissions reductions [3]. Many countries propose to expand land-based removals, but none yet commit to substantively scaling novel methods such as BECCS, biochar or DACCS [3]. Countries with net zero targets have begun integrating CDR into national pathways and research efforts, but this has not yet extended to net-negative targets and policies [1].

Limits and risks

Many CDR processes have medium to low technology readiness levels and are therefore subject to significant uncertainty [1]. The efficiency of DACCS depends on the carbon intensity of its energy input, although studies found that its life-cycle net emissions can be negative even for some current energy mixes [1]. Soil carbon sinks saturate after 10–100 years and are reversible if the practices that create them cease [6]. The same review estimates that soil carbon sequestration costs are low, "in the range of US$0–100/tCO2", and that its side effects are likely to be less of an issue than for many of the other technologies the review assesses, "though sink saturation and reversibility (non-permanence) are significant drawbacks" [6]. Compensating excess emissions with later removals can come with a penalty, because the cooling from net negative emissions may only offset part of the warming from earlier emissions [6]. Many authors have discussed organising separate policy tracks for reductions and removals, to avoid the risk that longer-term CDR ambitions dilute short-term efforts to reduce emissions [4].

What holds up

Well supported: CDR is a necessary element for reaching net zero CO2 and GHG emissions, chiefly to counterbalance residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors, and it is a key element in scenarios limiting warming to 2°C or lower (robust evidence, high agreement) [1]. The IPCC assessment itself states that, within ambitious mitigation strategies at global or national levels, CDR cannot serve as a substitute for deep emissions reductions but can fulfil multiple complementary roles [1]. Both [3] and [4] state that emissions reductions remain the dominant lever and that CDR complements rather than replaces them, though [4], a systematic map of governance literature, takes this framing from scenario work (Prütz et al., 2023) rather than assessing it itself. One review concludes that, given these uncertainties, strategies should aim to limit warming with the least possible assumed dependence on NETs, and that there should be no delay in a global peak and decline of CO2 emissions until NETs are demonstrably available at scale [6]. The choice of methods, and the scale and timing of their deployment, will depend on the ambition of gross emissions cuts, how sustainability and feasibility constraints are managed, and how political preferences and social acceptability evolve [1].
