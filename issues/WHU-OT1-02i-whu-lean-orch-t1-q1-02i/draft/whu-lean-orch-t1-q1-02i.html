Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe the evidence as providing "emerging support" for these interventions rather than settled proof [1]. The clearest effects reported are for interventions that build learners' skills, such as literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but these large effects are based on a low number of studies [1]. Interventions that change schools, systems or environments have been studied much less, so the passages cannot say whether they work better or worse [1, 2, 3].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers in enrolment, retention and completion of education, and the gap in educational attainment is largest in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Before the 2025 review, its authors noted that evidence on which interventions are actually effective had not been established [1].

Skills-building interventions

Of the 18 studies included in the review's analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. By outcome, effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant, and the review says all these effects are large but based on a low number of studies [1].

Inclusion conditions and teachers

Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. An evidence and gap map of teacher professional development for disability inclusion in Asia-Pacific low- and middle-income countries found 50 studies, with only 16 of 41 countries reporting evidence [2]. In that map, a significant number of interventions focused on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability [2]. Only three studies in the map addressed student mental health [2]. No studies in the map reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [2]. Some included studies were qualitative [2]. In one such study from Vietnam, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2].

A lopsided evidence base

Most interventions in the 2025 review tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. A broader evidence and gap map on disability in low- and middle-income countries likewise found that studies mostly try to change characteristics of the person with a disability rather than address structures such as schools' readiness to include them [3]. That map also found that fewer studies focus on improving infrastructure or institutions [3]. Over half of the impact evaluations in that map came from upper-middle income countries, with only 9 from low-income countries [3]. The same map reports a significant gap in study quality, especially for impact evaluations [3]. In the 2025 review, the interventions and outcome measures were all different, making direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the studies in the 2025 review were in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1].

Which kinds work best for which disabilities

The 2025 review set out to ask which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability [1]. None of the passages provided here reports its answer to that question.

What holds up

It holds up that a range of individual-level, skills-focused interventions improve educational outcomes for learners with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, though the reviewers call this support "emerging" [1]. Skills-building interventions show the most consistent evidence, but the large effects on literacy, numeracy and related skills rest on a low number of studies [1]. Whether school-level or systemic approaches work best cannot be judged from this evidence, because such approaches have been little studied [1, 3]. The 2025 review's authors say more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to, and successful engagement in, education [1]. The authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1].
