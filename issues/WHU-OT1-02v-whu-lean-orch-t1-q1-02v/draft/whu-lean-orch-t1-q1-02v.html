Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe their evidence as providing "emerging support" for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. Interventions aimed at skills for learning showed significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, and the review calls these effects large but based on a low number of studies [1]. For literacy specifically, the review reports d = 2.46 (0.01 to 4.90) from 4 studies with 389 participants and I² = 93%, which it describes as a "Large effect based on a low number of studies with high heterogeneity and potentially high publication bias" [1].

What the review found

Before the review

The review's authors note that international directives oblige states to fulfil the right to education of people with disabilities, but that evidence on which interventions are actually effective for these goals had not been established [1, 4]. The review asked what works to improve educational outcomes for people with disabilities in these countries, and which interventions appear to be most effective for different types of disability [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. The source states that a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source concludes that, consequently, a focus on evaluating interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment is needed [1].

What kinds of programmes were studied

Most of the interventions studied tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. The second most common category was 'Accessible learning environments', which included programmes that aimed to improve social skills or to reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1]. No studies represented the intervention categories 'Accessibility of built environment and learning materials' or 'Inclusive education policies' [1]. The included studies range from a randomised trial of a language and literacy intervention with 65 participants in South Africa, to a randomised trial of the Good School Toolkit, a school-wide anti-violence intervention, with 3820 participants in Uganda, to an uncontrolled before-versus-after study of an inclusive education training programme for teachers with 130 participants in Kenya [1].

Effects

Of the 18 studies included in the analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. The effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant, and the review calls all of them large but based on a low number of studies [1]. Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed what the review calls a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1].

Limits of the evidence

The interventions and outcome measures in the included studies were all different, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the studies were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. They also call for more research in underrepresented regions and settings, such as Latin America and the Caribbean, rural areas and humanitarian contexts [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies were rated as having low confidence in their findings overall [1]. The low confidence across studies was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs, and did not always reflect multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and ensures their inclusion in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1].

What holds up

The review's authors conclude that more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to education and the likelihood of successful engagement in it [1]. They also recommend a focus on interventions that address attendance, enrolment, completion and transition to higher levels of education, not only individual skills [1]. The review reports that "no study looked at outcomes related to attendance, enrolment, and completion of people with disabilities in education" [1].
