Have the world's greenhouse gas emissions grown faster in recent decades than in earlier ones?

Study draft, 21 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

In tonnes, yes; in percentage growth, not since the 2000s. Average yearly emissions have been higher in each decade than in the one before, and the rise from the 2000s to the 2010s was the largest on record. But the yearly rate of growth was higher in the 2000s than in the decades just before or since.

The claim, as it is usually made

In April 2014 the IPCC announced its mitigation report under the headline that greenhouse gas emissions accelerate despite reduction efforts. Its release said emissions grew more quickly between 2000 and 2010 than in each of the three previous decades. A November 2025 summary of the UN Environment Programme's Emissions Gap Report for exam candidates said the report highlights accelerating global greenhouse gas emissions.

What the 2014 assessment found

The IPCC's 2014 assessment found that annual greenhouse gas emissions grew on average by 2.2% a year from 2000 to 2010, compared with 1.3% a year from 1970 to 2000. On those figures, the 2000s were indeed a decade of faster growth than the three before them.

What happened in the 2010s

The IPCC's next assessment, in 2022, found that average annual emissions during 2010–2019 were higher than in any previous decade, but that the rate of growth between 2010 and 2019 was lower than between 2000 and 2009. The average annual rate of growth slowed from 2.1% a year between 2000 and 2009 to 1.3% a year between 2010 and 2019. Yet the 2010s averaged 56 ± 6.0 GtCO2-eq a year, 9.1 GtCO2-eq a year more than the 2000s, which the IPCC calls the highest increase in average decadal emissions on record. The dataset behind that assessment, by Jan Minx and colleagues, found the decadal growth rate fell from 1.8% a year in the 1970s to 0.9% a year in the 1990s, rose to 2.4% a year in the 2000s, and fell to 1.2% a year in 2009–2018.

The most recent decade

The Global Carbon Budget 2025 found that total carbon dioxide emissions from fossil fuels and land use grew by 0.3% a year over 2015–2024, against 1.9% a year over 2005–2014. For fossil carbon dioxide alone, it puts growth at 4.3% a year in the 1960s, 3.1% in the 1970s, 1.5% in the 1980s and 1.0% in the 1990s, then 2.8% in the 2000s, 1.2% in the 2010s and 0.8% in 2015–2024. The Indicators of Global Climate Change 2025 update by Forster et al., published in Earth System Science Data in 2026, put greenhouse gas emissions at an all-time high of 54.6 ± 5.5 GtCO2e a year on average over 2015–2024, and said there is evidence that carbon dioxide emission growth is slowing.

One year is not a decade

The UN Environment Programme reported that total greenhouse gas emissions rose 2.3% in 2024, more than four times the 2010s' average growth of 0.6% a year and comparable to the 2000s' average of 2.2% a year.

How sure are these numbers?

The IPCC gives its 2019 global total an uncertainty of ±11% at a 90% confidence interval, and ±70% for carbon dioxide from land use. For land-use carbon dioxide, it found no statistically significant trend because of high uncertainties in the estimates. Different datasets and editions give somewhat different rates for the same decade, 2.1% or 2.4% a year for the 2000s, but they agree on the order: faster growth in the 2000s, slower before and after.

How the coverage did

The Jamaica Gleaner's December 2025 report on the Emissions Gap Report quoted the UN comparison of 2024 with both the 2010s and the 2000s, so a reader there could see the decades side by side.

What a reasonable person can conclude

Emissions are at a record high and have been higher in each decade than the last, so the problem has grown in size every decade. But the claim that emissions are rising faster and faster fits the 2000s, not the years since: the growth rate fell in the 2010s and fell further in 2015–2024. Whether that slowdown lasts is not settled by these sources.
