Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review describes its evidence as providing 'emerging support' for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The clearest effects are for programmes that build learners' skills: the review reports significant, large effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, though these rest on a low number of studies [1]. The review covers people with disabilities generally rather than only children and young people, and the passages here do not say which approaches work best for which types of disability [1].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities consistently fall behind their peers without disabilities in school enrolment, completion, mean years of schooling and literacy levels [1]. Evidence suggests this gap in educational attainment is greatest in LMICs [1]. Before this review, its authors wrote, evidence on which interventions are actually effective had not been established [1]. All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. The source states that "A twin‐track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom)." [1] According to the source, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source states that, "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1] The source states that the EGM it summarizes "provides a starting point for researchers, decision makers and programme managers to access the available research evidence on the effectiveness of interventions for people with disabilities in LMICs in order to guide policy and programme activity, and encourage a more strategic, policy-oriented approach to setting the future research agenda." [2]

Skills-for-learning programmes

Of the 18 studies included in the analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. The included studies tested approaches such as a phonological awareness and early literacy programme in South Africa (an RCT with 65 participants), computer-assisted working-memory training in Iran, and self-regulated strategy development for writing in Egypt [1].

Programmes that change the learning environment

Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. A single study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. 'Accessible learning environments' was the second most common intervention category, including programmes to improve social skills or reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in school [1]. Examples include the Good School Toolkit, a school-wide violence-reduction programme tested in an RCT with 3,820 participants in Ugandan primary schools, and a teacher inclusive-education training programme in Kenya evaluated with an uncontrolled before-versus-after design with 130 participants [1]. No studies represented the categories 'Accessibility of built environment and learning materials' or 'Inclusive education policies' [1].

Limits of the evidence

The interventions and outcome measures in the included studies were all different, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the studies were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated findings by gender [1]. The review's authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs, and standardised measures [1]. An earlier evidence and gap map on interventions for people with disabilities in LMICs, not limited to education, found a significant gap in study quality, especially among impact evaluations [2]. That map found most studies came from upper-middle-income countries, with limited evidence from low-income countries [2]. It also found that studies mostly try to change characteristics of the person with a disability rather than address structures such as schools' readiness to include them [2]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies were rated as low confidence in their findings overall, so the evidence on these programmes is weak [1]. The lack of confidence across studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs, and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1]. The sources conclude that evaluation should focus on interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, which are needed [1].

What holds up

What holds up is that individually targeted programmes, especially those building literacy, numeracy, cognitive and handwriting skills, show significant and often large effects, but these rest on few, varied and often small studies. Whether school-wide, policy or infrastructure changes work is largely unknown, because these have rarely or never been evaluated. The review's authors say more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to, and successful engagement in, education [1]. They also call for interventions that address attendance, enrolment, completion and transition to higher levels of education, not only individual skills [1].
