Do mobile tracking tools with prompts and reminders improve primary care?

The question

The question is whether, in primary health care, using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improves the care people receive and their health.

We hold two documents in full and drew on both: a Cochrane systematic review updated in 2025 and a World Health Organization guideline from 2019.

The short answer

The review's authors conclude that digital tracking may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use, but that these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies.

They also say they have little confidence in the findings because there were not many studies, some studies were small, and others had design problems. The authors add that evidence for some outcomes was only based on a small number of studies from a restricted range of settings or few events, and they call for better-designed and larger studies from a broader range of settings.

What the review found

The review searched for studies from 2000 to November 2022 and included 18 studies (11 randomised, seven non-randomised), conducted in Bangladesh, China, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Palestine, Tanzania, Uganda and the USA. The review notes that all of the studies (aside from 1) "were conducted in low‐ and middle‐income settings where electronic medical records are still being rolled out."

All the non-randomised studies had a high risk of bias, and the results summarised in the review's abstract come from the randomised studies.

Tracking with prompts for health workers

Tracking combined with decision support may slightly increase home visits in the week following delivery and counselling for starting complementary feeding, both from two studies.

It may slightly increase skin-to-skin care and early breastfeeding, may reduce low birthweight babies (one study of 3023 participants; risk ratio 0.53, confidence interval 0.38 to 0.73), and may increase the number of infants with pneumonia or fever who seek care.

Its effects on stillbirths, neonatal and infant deaths were uncertain, and it may result in little or no difference to how many children under five were fully immunised.

Tracking with messages to patients

The evidence for tracking combined with targeted client communication came from one study of 1226 stroke patients over 12 months, in which the approach may reduce deaths (risk ratio 0.52, confidence interval 0.28 to 0.96) and stroke hospitalisations (risk ratio 0.45, confidence interval 0.32 to 0.64), may slightly increase blood pressure medication adherence, and may slightly reduce systolic blood pressure.

Tracking with both

Tracking combined with both decision support and targeted client communication probably increases guideline adherence for antenatal screening and management of anaemia, diabetes and hypertension, but probably leads to lower adherence for abnormal foetal growth.

It may reduce new cardiovascular events in adults at high cardiovascular risk (one study of 8642 participants; odds ratio 0.58, confidence interval 0.42 to 0.80).

In women receiving antenatal care it may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes, and in adults with hypertension or diabetes it may make little or no difference to HbA1c, total cholesterol or 10-year cardiovascular risk.

Its effects were uncertain on maternal or infant mortality in HIV-positive mothers and on blood pressure control, fasting glucose and quality of life in adults with hypertension or diabetes, and it may have little or no effect on missed early infant diagnosis visits.

It probably slightly increases the number of adults with hypertension or diabetes reporting a slightly or much better change in the quality of care (one study of 3324 participants; risk ratio 1.02, confidence interval 1.00 to 1.03).

None of the review's included studies evaluated time between presentation and appropriate management, timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences. For tracking combined with decision support compared with usual care, the review reports that for client acceptability of or satisfaction with the intervention 'No studies assessed these outcomes.'

What the WHO guideline recommends

WHO recommends digital tracking combined with decision support, and digital tracking combined with both decision support and targeted client communication, only in specific contexts or conditions.

The conditions are that the health system can support the components in an integrated manner and that the tasks are already within the health worker's scope of practice; for the three-part package, concerns about data privacy and transmitting sensitive content to clients must also be addressable.

For digital tracking with targeted client communication, the guideline states that no evidence was identified, and it assessed the evidence for combining tracking with both decision support and targeted client communication as very low certainty.

What we did not read

We did not read the 18 included studies themselves, and we did not search for studies published after November 2022.
