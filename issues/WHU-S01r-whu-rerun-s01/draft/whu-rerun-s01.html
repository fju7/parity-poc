Does DNA-based TB testing change what happens to patients?

The question

The question is whether, for people being checked for TB, using a DNA-based test instead of the older microscope or lab-culture methods changes what happens to them: whether they survive, are cured, or start treatment sooner.

When WHO endorsed the first such test, Xpert MTB/RIF, on 8 December 2010, it said the test could revolutionize TB care and control by providing an accurate diagnosis for many patients in about 100 minutes.

WHO said then that evidence to date indicated the test could result in a three-fold increase in the diagnosis of patients with drug-resistant TB and a doubling in the number of HIV-associated TB cases diagnosed in areas with high rates of TB and HIV.

The short answer

Compared with smear microscopy, testing with Xpert MTB/RIF probably means fewer people with bacteriological confirmation are lost to follow-up before treatment, and more of those treated have bacteriological confirmation (moderate-certainty evidence).

Whether it changes survival overall is not settled: the Cochrane review was unable to confidently rule in or rule out an effect on all-cause mortality.

Among people known to be infected with HIV, Xpert MTB/RIF probably reduces mortality (moderate-certainty evidence).

The review is uncertain whether Xpert MTB/RIF has a modest effect or none on the proportion treated or, among those treated, on the proportion with a successful outcome, and judges that it probably does not have a substantial effect on these outcomes.

For the newer Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra, a 2025 Cochrane review of two trials concludes, based on limited evidence, that it makes little to no difference to all-cause mortality at six and 18 months, probably decreases the time taken to diagnose TB and time to treatment initiation, and increases the risk of loss to follow-up compared with smear microscopy.

WHO's guideline strongly recommends these tests in place of smear microscopy or culture with high certainty of evidence, but that certainty is graded on how accurately the tests detect TB, and the guideline lists their effect on cure, mortality and time to treatment as a research priority.

Survival

The Cochrane review by Haraka and colleagues (2021) included 12 studies of Xpert MTB/RIF used rather than smear microscopy: eight randomized controlled trials and four before-and-after studies, with the randomized and non-randomized studies analysed separately.

Most of those trials had a low risk of bias in most domains of the Cochrane risk-of-bias tool.

There was inconclusive evidence of an effect on all-cause mortality overall: risk ratio 0.89 (95% CI 0.75 to 1.05) across 5 trials and 9932 participants, moderate-certainty evidence.

Restricted to the 3 trials with six-month follow-up, the risk ratio was 0.98 (95% CI 0.78 to 1.22; 8143 participants; moderate-certainty evidence).

In participants known to be infected with HIV, the odds ratio for death was 0.80 (95% CI 0.67 to 0.96; 5 trials, 5855 participants), which the review reads as probably a reduction in mortality (moderate-certainty evidence).

An individual patient data meta-analysis of five trials by Di Tanna and colleagues (2019) gave a pooled adjusted odds ratio for all-cause death by six months of 0·88 (95% CI 0·68–1·14), based on the three trials that reported this outcome.

Its authors were unable to confidently rule in nor rule out an Xpert-associated reduction in mortality among outpatients tested for tuberculosis.

For HIV-positive patients they reported a lower rate of death with Xpert (hazard ratio 0·76, 95% CI 0·60–0·97), and wrote that this reduction, in a secondary analysis, suggests the possibility of population-level impact. For the primary outcome of all-cause death by six months, the authors' stratified analysis showed a pooled odds ratio of 0·83 (95% CI 0·65–1·05; p=0·12) for HIV-positive individuals, an interval that does not rule out no effect. In a time-to-event analysis of all outpatients tested, the authors reported 9·69 deaths per 100 person-years in the Xpert group and 11·63 deaths per 100 person-years in the smear microscopy group (HR 0·83, 95% CI 0·65–1·06; p=0·13), and these results were similar in sensitivity analyses adjusting for age, gender, weight, and tuberculosis symptoms.

In a cluster randomized trial among adults newly diagnosed with HIV in rural Malawi, by Ngwira and colleagues (2019), all-cause mortality was 22% lower in the Xpert arm than in the fluorescence microscopy arm, with a rate ratio of 0.78 (95% CI .58–1.06). The authors state that, in this cluster randomized trial across 12 primary healthcare clinics in rural Malawi, screening for TB symptoms at the time of HIV diagnosis followed by point-of-care Xpert (vs point-of-care LED fluorescence microscopy) did not significantly reduce all-cause mortality over 12 months.

Its authors concluded that point-of-care Xpert reduced all-cause 12-month mortality among individuals with advanced HIV, a planned subgroup in which the rate ratio was 0.43 (95% CI .22–.87).

In Uganda, a cluster-randomized trial, XPEL-TB, compared on-site Xpert testing plus implementation supports with routine care using on-site smear microscopy and referral-based Xpert testing, and its mortality analysis by Katamba and colleagues (2024) reported an adjusted rate ratio of 0.77 (95% CI: 0.47–1.28).

The test used on site in XPEL-TB was Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra.

Its authors read the consistent point estimates favoring the intervention in their trial and previous ones as suggesting that Xpert testing may have an impact on mortality at community health centers.

They also judged that the magnitude of effect is small, and that statistically significant results are unlikely to be attained within a single trial.

They wrote that future trials of novel TB diagnostics at community health centers should focus on more proximal outcomes, including TB detection and treatment initiation.

Treatment and cure

Among participants starting TB treatment, the Cochrane review's odds ratio for a successful treatment outcome was 1.10 (95% CI 0.96 to 1.26; 3 trials, 4802 participants; moderate-certainty evidence), and it is uncertain whether Xpert MTB/RIF has no or a modest effect on it.

There was also inconclusive evidence of an effect on the proportion of participants treated for TB (risk ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.98 to 1.23; 5 trials, 8793 participants; moderate-certainty evidence).

Among participants with bacteriological confirmation, the proportion lost to follow-up before treatment was probably reduced (risk ratio 0.59, 95% CI 0.41 to 0.85; 3 trials, 1217 participants; moderate-certainty evidence).

Of participants treated for TB, the proportion with bacteriological confirmation was probably higher with Xpert MTB/RIF (risk ratio 1.44, 95% CI 1.29 to 1.61; 6 trials, 2068 participants; moderate-certainty evidence).

The review concludes that Xpert MTB/RIF probably increases both the proportion of treated participants who had bacteriological confirmation and the proportion with a laboratory-confirmed diagnosis who were treated.

Its authors add that these findings may inform decisions about uptake alongside evidence on cost-effectiveness and implementation.

In the Brazil trial's report of treatment outcomes, by Trajman and colleagues (2015), overall unfavourable outcomes were not reduced in the Xpert arm (29.6% versus 31.7%), the proportion of patients successfully treated did not increase, with high loss to follow-up rates in both arms, and its authors concluded that Xpert MTB/RIF introduction did not improve TB treatment outcomes in Brazil.

They also reported a 35% reduction in TB-related mortality, which they hypothesized may be explained by less advanced disease among the smear-negative patients diagnosed by Xpert; a 2016 correction revised the adjusted odds ratio for TB-attributed death from 0.65 (0.44–0.97) to 0.69 (0.46–1.04), so that its interval now includes 1, while stating that the conclusions remain unchanged.

Starting treatment sooner

The Cochrane review did not analyse time to treatment: of the studies it included, only one randomized study reported a hazard ratio for it, and the medians across studies appeared to vary greatly.

That study, a pragmatic randomised trial in a large TB/HIV primary care clinic in South Africa by Cox and colleagues (2014), found that 13% of people with bacteriologically confirmed TB had not started treatment by 3 months after presentation in the Xpert arm, compared to 25% in the routine arm. The trial's pre-specified primary outcome was the proportion of bacteriologically confirmed TB cases that had not initiated appropriate treatment by 2 months after enrolment; the authors report that, as there were a number of TB cases diagnosed late in this period, the time period to assess treatment initiation was extended (at the time of data analysis) to 3 months after enrolment.

Time to treatment initiation was improved overall (hazard ratio 0.76, 95% CI 0.63–0.92), and there was no difference in 6-month mortality with Xpert versus routine diagnosis. Because more participants in the Xpert arm started treatment, the trial found that the proportion of participants with a successful treatment outcome overall was significantly greater in the Xpert arm than in the routine arm: 21.9% (215/982) compared to 17.5% (176/1,003), respectively (risk ratio 1.25, 95% CI 1.04–1.49, p = 0.0319), although among those who did start treatment, outcomes were similar.

Its authors concluded that these data suggest that, in this routine primary care setting, Xpert increased the number of people with bacteriologically confirmed TB who were treated by 3 months and reduced time to treatment initiation, particularly among HIV-infected participants.

In a stepped-wedge cluster-randomized trial in 14 primary care laboratories in two Brazilian cities, by Durovni and colleagues (2014), median time to treatment decreased from 11.4 to 8.1 days, although not among confirmed cases.

Replacing smear microscopy with Xpert MTB/RIF in Brazil increased confirmation of pulmonary TB, but no increase in overall notification rates was observed, possibly because of high rates of empirical TB treatment.

In the Ugandan trial, reported by Cattamanchi and colleagues (2021), more patients were treated for confirmed TB within 14 days after presentation with the on-site testing strategy (342 patients across 10 intervention health centers, against 220 across 10 control health centers; adjusted rate ratio, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.21 to 2.01). In the same trial, more patients at intervention centers than at control centers completed tuberculosis testing (adjusted rate ratio, 1.85; 95% CI, 1.21 to 2.82), received a same-day diagnosis (adjusted rate ratio, 1.89; 95% CI, 1.39 to 2.56), and received same-day treatment for confirmed tuberculosis (adjusted rate ratio, 2.38; 95% CI, 1.57 to 3.61).

Among 706 patients with confirmed TB in that trial, a higher proportion in the intervention group were treated on the same day (adjusted rate ratio, 2.29; 95% CI, 1.23 to 4.25).

The newer test, Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra

A 2025 Cochrane review by Otieno and colleagues included two trials, in South Africa and Uganda, that compared Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra with smear microscopy in 11,228 adults, and excluded studies of the original Xpert MTB/RIF, which Ultra has superseded.

It reports that the choice of test made little to no difference to all-cause mortality at six months (risk ratio 0.79, 95% CI 0.61 to 1.01; 1 trial, 8116 participants; high-certainty evidence) and at 18 months (adjusted rate ratio 0.77, 95% CI 0.46 to 1.29; 1 trial, 8413 participants; high-certainty evidence).

Neither trial assessed how the test used affects the likelihood of curing TB.

In XPEL-TB the review reports a slightly higher risk of loss to follow-up with Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra (13.6%) than with smear microscopy (10.1%), a risk ratio of 1.35 (95% CI 1.20 to 1.51; 1 trial, 9563 participants; high-certainty evidence).

A testing strategy that includes Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra probably reduces the time to diagnosis (adjusted geometric mean ratio 0.49, 95% CI 0.39 to 0.62) and the time to treatment (adjusted geometric mean ratio 0.35, 95% CI 0.21 to 0.58), both on moderate-certainty evidence.

The authors write that the evidence is limited by sparse data, a lack of some key outcomes (specifically, cure), varied effect measures and the exclusion of children, and that, while they graded the evidence as moderate certainty for time to diagnosis and treatment initiation, serious concerns about the risk of bias and the narrow scope restrict its applicability to other NAATs and patient outcomes.

They conclude that there were no data on the effects of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra compared with smear microscopy or culture on tuberculosis cure rates.

The review grades the XPEL-TB mortality result as little to no difference with high certainty, while the trial's own authors read the same point estimate, with earlier ones, as suggesting that Xpert testing may have an impact on mortality; we state both.

Why the effect on survival is hard to see

A systematic review of trial design by Ochodo and colleagues (2019) concluded that most studies of Xpert MTB/RIF were designed and conducted in a manner that resulted in more patients being diagnosed and treated for TB, minimising the potential difference in mortality Xpert MTB/RIF testing could have achieved compared to usual care.

The study features it lists as ones that could explain the lack of observed effect on mortality include the higher quality of care at study sites and the involvement of study staff in ensuring adherence with care and follow-up. Across the seven randomised and one non-randomised study it included, the review reports that all demonstrated relative reductions in overall mortality in the Xpert MTB/RIF arm ranging from 6% to 40%, although the mortality reduction was reported to be statistically significant in only two studies. The other study features the review lists as ones that could explain the lack of observed effect on mortality are the inclusion of patients with a higher pre-test probability of TB leading to higher than expected empirical rates, the performance of additional diagnostic testing not done in usual care leading to increased TB diagnosis or empiric treatment initiation, and the recruitment of participants likely to return for follow-up.

What WHO assesses

WHO's current guideline, the fourth edition issued in 2025, recommends that for adults and adolescents with signs or symptoms of TB, low-complexity automated NAATs be used on respiratory samples as initial diagnostic tests for TB rather than smear microscopy or culture (strong recommendation, high certainty of evidence).

That class includes the Xpert MTB/RIF and Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra assays and the Truenat MTB Plus and MTB-RIF Dx assays.

The evidence the guideline sets out for that recommendation is diagnostic accuracy: a summary sensitivity of 90.4% (95% CI 88.0–92.4) and a summary specificity of 94.9% (95% CI 93.0–96.3), with the certainty of evidence for sensitivity and specificity graded as high.

Among its research priorities, the guideline lists evaluating the impact of LC-aNAAT testing on patient-important outcomes: cure, mortality, time to diagnosis and time to start of treatment.

So the two kinds of evidence differ, and we state both: the expert assessment is confident about how well the tests detect TB, while the measured patient outcomes probably favour Xpert MTB/RIF for bacteriological confirmation, for fewer people lost before treatment and for death among people with HIV, and the 2021 review is uncertain about death overall and about successful treatment, while the 2025 review judges that Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra makes little to no difference to death.

What we read, and its limits

We drew on fifteen documents: three systematic reviews or pooled analyses of trials, a systematic review of trial design, six trial reports, three corrections, WHO's 2025 diagnostic guideline and WHO's 2010 announcement.

We did not read the reports of the South African XTEND and TB-NEAT trials themselves; what this page says of other trials comes from the two pooled analyses.

We did not search separately for trials of the newer DNA-based tests on patient outcomes; a recency check on our first draft pointed us to the 2025 Cochrane review of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra and to the Brazil trial's report of treatment outcomes, which we then read.

The Ugandan trial tested on-site molecular testing together with guided restructuring of clinic workflows and monthly feedback of quality metrics, so its results are the effect of that strategy and not of the test alone.
