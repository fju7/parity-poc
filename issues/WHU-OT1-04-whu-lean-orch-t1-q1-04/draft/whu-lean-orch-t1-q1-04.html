Carbon dioxide removal and net zero: a supporting role to deep emission cuts, growing over the coming decades

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

Rapid emissions reductions, including reductions in deforestation-based land emissions, are described as the dominant source of global climate mitigation potential in the coming decades, but carbon dioxide removal (CDR) 'will also have an important role to play' [2]. The IPCC Working Group III contribution to the Sixth Assessment Report states that deploying CDR to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is 'unavoidable' if net zero CO2 or GHG emissions are to be achieved [5]. In the assessed mitigation pathways, reducing fossil fuel and deforestation emissions accounts for over 80% of net greenhouse gas reductions until global net zero CO2, independent of how stringent the climate objective is [3]. In the near term, removals via afforestation and reforestation play a critical role, accounting for around 10% (median) of net greenhouse gas reductions between 2020 and 2030 in scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C with limited overshoot [3]. In the decades ahead, novel removal technologies such as direct air carbon capture and storage scale to multi-gigatonne levels by 2050 and beyond in these pathways, to balance residual emissions and draw down warming [3].

What the evidence shows

Emission cuts do most of the work

In 1.5 °C pathways with limited overshoot (C1), most mitigation between 2020 and 2030 comes from reducing gross CO2 emissions (70%) and cutting non-CO2 emissions (20%), with halting deforestation and expanding conventional CDR on land accounting for 10% of net GHG reductions [3]. Between 2020 and global net zero CO2 in C1 pathways, gross CO2 reductions account for 71% of net GHG reductions, with CDR contributing a much smaller amount (15%) [3]. The authors interpret this as showing that less stringent climate objectives do not fundamentally change the scale of gross CO2 reductions needed but merely shift them over time, raising questions of intergenerational fairness [3].

How much removal happens today

Conventional CDR on land, which captures and stores carbon in the land biosphere, is currently estimated to contribute around 2 Gt CO2 of removals per year [3]. Novel CDR methods, including BECCS, DACCS and enhanced weathering, are not used at scale today and are currently estimated to contribute around 2 Mt CO2 of removals per year [3]. Taken together, current CDR approximates 2 GtCO2 per year, or about 3% of current global net greenhouse gas emissions [4]. Afforestation/reforestation, soil carbon sequestration and harvested wood products are relatively well integrated into national policy and planning, whereas emerging methods such as BECCS, DACCS, biochar, enhanced weathering and blue carbon have so far had little exposure to governance and policy regimes [4].

The role of removals grows over time

C1 pathways nearly double the volume of CDR this decade, and across most C1 scenarios novel CDR methods scale up by mid-century to support net zero CO2, reaching around 4 Gt CO2 (interquartile range 2 to 6) by 2050 [3]. At the year of global net zero CO2, the authors estimate a gross greenhouse gas emission level of 18 Gt CO2eq/yr across the scenarios, largely consistent with another estimate of a residual emission level of 16 Gt CO2eq/yr [3]. In the OECD and EU region, removals play a much larger role after 2050, accounting for a median of around 43%, 48% and 35% of net GHG reductions across the C1, C2 and C3 pathways respectively [3]. In the Asia region, CDR accounts for over 20% (median) of regional net GHG reductions beyond 2050 in C2 and C3 pathways, where global near-term action is slower [3].

Delay increases reliance on unproven removals

Delaying net zero CO2 increases total CDR deployed up to that year: from 166 Gt CO2 for net zero by mid-century, to 251 Gt CO2 for net zero between 2050 and 2075, and to 445 Gt CO2 for net zero later than 2075 [3]. The amount of conventional land-based CDR is largely consistent across different timings of delayed emission reductions [3]. By contrast, the scenarios show a strong signal of more novel CDR where net emission halving takes longer, which the authors describe as dependence on future, unproven CDR technologies when net emission reductions are delayed [3]. Novel removals are described as still at a nascent stage of development, and are expected to require both time and investment to ramp up deployment and become cost-effective [3].

National plans fall short of scenario levels

One analysis identifies a 'CDR gap': CDR efforts proposed by countries fall short of those in integrated assessment model scenarios that limit warming to 1.5 °C [2]. The most ambitious national proposals are close to the CDR levels of a low-energy-demand scenario that has the most limited CDR scaling and aggressive near-term emission reductions [2]. Many countries propose to expand land-based removals, but none yet commit to substantively scaling novel methods such as BECCS, biochar or direct air carbon capture and storage [2].

Safeguards, governance and accounting

Scaling CDR will require advances in policymaking and governance, including strict monitoring to ensure durable, additional removals that do not compete with emission reduction efforts [4]. Many have discussed the need for separate policy tracks for reductions and removals, to avoid the risk that longer-term ambitions for CDR dilute short-term efforts to reduce emissions [4]. Emerging compliance markets must deal with permanence and with liabilities for removals that expire after a certain period, such as afforestation/reforestation [4]. Voluntary carbon markets could support business decarbonisation but could also offer new avenues for greenwashing [4]. Without policy support, the authors judge that it seems unlikely that niche voluntary markets can sustain novel CDR in the long term [4]. For BECCS, the sustainability concerns noted include competition for land, impacts on food prices and impacts on biodiversity [3]. Direct air capture currently lacks explicitly specified IPCC inventory methodologies, and in January 2024 the IPCC tasked its inventory task force with developing a Methodology Report on CDR technologies [5].

What holds up

The sources agree that deep cuts in gross emissions dominate the path to net zero, while removing CO2 plays a supporting but necessary role, chiefly to counterbalance residual emissions [2, 3, 5]. For now, almost all removal comes from land-based methods such as forests, which deliver around 2 Gt CO2 per year against roughly 2 Mt CO2 per year from novel methods [3, 4]. In the decades ahead, the modelled pathways rely on novel methods reaching gigatonne scale by mid-century, and that reliance grows as emission cuts are delayed [3]. That future role is less certain: countries are not yet committing to scale novel methods, the technologies are nascent, and the governance needed to keep removals durable and separate from emission cuts is still being built [2, 3, 4].
