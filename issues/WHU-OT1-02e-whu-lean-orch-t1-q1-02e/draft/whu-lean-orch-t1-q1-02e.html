Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities work in low- and middle-income countries?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review describes its evidence as providing emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The review reports large effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but these effects are based on a low number of studies [1].

What the evidence shows

Interventions that build learners' skills

Of the 18 studies the review included in its analysis of intervention effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. The review finds significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, and says all these effects are large but are based on a low number of studies [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source states: "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1] According to the source report, "This report illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs." [2] The source, titled 'Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map,' is presented as an evidence and gap map, and the text provided contains no findings or certainty or confidence wording to quote [2]. According to the source, the EGM summarized there "provides a starting point for researchers, decision makers and programme managers to access the available research evidence on the effectiveness of interventions for people with disabilities in LMICs in order to guide policy and programme activity, and encourage a more strategic, policy-oriented approach to setting the future research agenda." [3]

Interventions on inclusion, learning environments and teachers

Included studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. The second most common category of intervention was 'Accessible learning environments', for instance programmes which aimed to improve social skills or to reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1]. A 2022 evidence and gap map found 50 studies on teacher training for the inclusion of students with disabilities in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries, and only 16 of 41 countries reported evidence on in-service teacher professional development for disability inclusion [2]. That map reports that a significant number of the interventions focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability, while only three studies discuss interventions for supporting mental health among students with a disability [2]. In one qualitative study from Vietnam, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2].

Gaps and limits in the evidence

The 2025 review found that most interventions tried to improve children's skills but did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. The included studies all used different interventions and outcome measures, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the studies in that review were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The teacher-training map likewise found no interventions that support students with disabilities during emergency or crisis situations [2]. A broader evidence and gap map of disability interventions in LMICs found that interventions mostly try to change characteristics of the person with a disability rather than address structures such as the readiness of schools to include them [3]. That map also reports that over half of the impact evaluations come from upper-middle income countries, with only 9 from low income countries [3]. It calls for more studies on all types of impairment, including visual and hearing impairments [3]. The 2025 review calls for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. Studies of interventions targeting speech and school behaviour found no significant effect of the intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies were rated low confidence in their findings, so the evidence on these programmes is weak [1]. The finding says the lack of confidence in the studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1]. Only three studies reported interventions aimed at supporting mental health among students with a disability [2]. The review found no studies reporting on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [2].

What holds up

The claim best supported by the sources is that individual-level interventions, especially those building skills for learning, can improve educational outcomes, though the review calls this support emerging [1]. Any ranking of which kinds work best rests on thin ground, because the largest reported effects come from a low number of studies and the studies are hard to compare. Systemic and school-level approaches remain largely unevaluated, and the review states that more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to, and successful engagement in, education [1]. The evidence on teacher training comes from a map that charts which studies exist rather than measuring effects, and much of it concerns teacher attitudes and understanding [2].
