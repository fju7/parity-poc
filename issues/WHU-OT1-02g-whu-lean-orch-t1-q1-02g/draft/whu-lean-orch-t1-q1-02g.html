Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A Campbell systematic review of 28 studies concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The authors describe this as 'emerging support' for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions, not settled proof [1]. They also judge the quality of the included studies to be 'largely low' [1]. On which kinds work best, the review says more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious and how best to deliver them [1].

What the review found

What was studied

The review covers 28 studies, most of them aimed at children with disabilities, with a few targeting family members or service providers [1]. People with intellectual, learning or developmental impairments were the groups most often targeted [1]. Eight of the studies were from India and four from Iran [1]. Brazil, Jordan, Kenya, Lebanon, Romania, Turkey, Malaysia, Nigeria, Uganda and Zambia each contributed one study [1]. Most interventions tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. No included study addressed accessibility of the built environment and learning materials, or inclusive education policies [1]. Structural interventions and anti-bullying programmes were each represented by a single study [1]. The authors searched for studies up to March 2022 [1]. Early childhood development interventions were excluded from the review [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system‐level or school‐level changes [1]. The source states: "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1]

Skills for learning

Of the 18 studies analysed for effects on 'skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but the review notes these are based on a low number of studies [1]. Handwriting showed a large effect of 4.97 (CI 4.21 to 5.73) with low heterogeneity, but this rests on only two studies [1]. Numeracy showed a high effect of d = 4.60 (0.69 to 8.50), based on two studies with high heterogeneity [1]. Publication bias for numeracy and handwriting could not be assessed because only two studies were available for each [1]. In the pooled analysis, the effect on speech and communication (d = 0.70, −1.70 to 3.09, five studies) was insignificant, with high heterogeneity [1]. The effect on school behaviour and adaptive functioning (d = 0.49, −1.76 to 2.74, five studies) was also insignificant [1]. The review's narrative summary nonetheless reports positive effects of individual studies on speech and communication and on school behaviour and adaptive functioning, alongside literacy, numeracy, handwriting and cognitive skills [1].

Learning environments and inclusion

Studies on 'conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderate effect (d = 0.35, 0.12 to 0.58) [1]. This effect is based on four studies with high heterogeneity (I² = 86%) and potentially moderate publication bias [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. Studies aimed at making learning environments more accessible reported positive impacts on teacher knowledge and attitudes, parent knowledge and attitudes, and violence reduction [1]. Only one study addressed attendance, enrolment and completion by equipping mainstream settings for children with disabilities [1]. In that study, teacher knowledge and attitudes about inclusive education improved [1].

Limits of the evidence

Most included studies used controlled or uncontrolled before-versus-after designs, and only a few were randomised controlled trials [1]. Many studies had small samples, some with as few as 8 participants [1]. Most study designs could not account for many potential confounders, and masking, baseline balance and attribution were poorly reported [1]. Interventions and outcome measures all differed, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the studies took place in humanitarian settings [1]. Few studies disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The review may be biased by its lack of grey literature and its absence of non-English literature [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of the intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the studies reviewed received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. Low confidence in the evidence across studies was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs, and it did not always reflect multiple points of weakness within any single study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that both meets the specific needs of children with disabilities and includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teacher skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1].

What holds up

What holds up is that individual-level programmes, especially those building literacy, numeracy, handwriting and cognitive skills, show significant and often large effects. Those effects rest on few, mostly low-quality studies. Programmes that improve conditions for inclusion show a moderate effect, but the evidence is thin and heterogeneous. The evidence does not establish which kinds of programme work best. System-level and policy approaches are largely untested. The authors call for interventions that target both the individual with a disability and their broader environment, such as teacher training and curriculum modifications [1]. They also call for a focus on attendance, enrolment, completion and transition to higher levels of education [1]. For research, they call for larger samples and more rigorous study designs [1].
