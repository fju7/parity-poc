Removing carbon dioxide from the air: what part it can play in reaching net zero

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report states that the deployment of carbon dioxide removal (CDR) to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or greenhouse gas emissions are to be achieved (high confidence) [4].

It describes removal as working in addition to deep, rapid and sustained emission reductions, where it can fulfil three complementary roles (high confidence) [4].

In the most ambitious new scenarios assessed by the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report, CDR accounts for around 16% of mitigation effort cumulatively when net-zero CO2 is achieved, compared to 84% from reducing sources of emissions [3].

Removal today comes mostly from forests: the same report puts current annual removal at around 2,200 MtCO2 per year, the vast majority from afforestation and reforestation [2].

At the same time, another 330 MtCO2 per year, withdrawn from the atmosphere in previous years by trees, is transferred to other forms of durable storage in wood products [2].

Novel CDR activity, at 2.0 MtCO2 per year, is around 1,000 times smaller than conventional CDR, but it is growing [2].

What removal is, and what it is for

The IPCC defines CDR as human activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and store it durably in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in products [4].

The processes by which CO2 is removed from the atmosphere are categorised as biological, geochemical or chemical [4].

CDR methods vary in their maturity, removal process, time scale of carbon storage, storage medium, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, impacts and risks, and governance requirements (high confidence), and Chapter 12 says implementation strategies need to reflect those differences [4] [1].

The three roles it assesses are lowering net emissions in the near term, counterbalancing hard-to-abate residual emissions (for example from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes) to help reach net zero in the mid-term, and achieving net negative emissions in the long term if removal exceeds annual residual emissions (high confidence) [4].

Chapter 12 of the same assessment calls CDR a necessary element to achieve net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas emissions both globally and nationally, counterbalancing residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors (robust evidence, high agreement) [1].

Chapter 12 concludes that, within ambitious mitigation strategies at global or national levels, CDR cannot serve as a substitute for deep emissions reductions but can fulfil multiple complementary roles [1].

The scale and timing of removal will depend on the trajectories of gross emission reductions in different sectors (high confidence) [4].

In modelled pathways that reach global net zero CO2, 5–16 GtCO2 of emissions from some sectors are compensated for by net negative CO2 emissions in other sectors at the point of net zero (high confidence) [4].

In most of these pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, the land sector, via reforestation and reduced deforestation, and the energy supply sector reach net zero CO2 earlier than the buildings, industry and transport sectors (high confidence) [4].

At the point pathways reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions, around 74% [54 to 90%] of global emission reductions come from CO2 reductions in energy supply and demand, 13% [4 to 20%] from CO2 mitigation in agriculture, forestry and other land use, and 13% [10 to 18%] from cutting non-CO2 emissions (medium confidence) [4].

How much removal, and when

Global net zero CO2 is reached in the early 2050s in modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and around the early 2070s in pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) (high confidence) [4].

Many of these pathways continue to net negative CO2 emissions after the point of net zero, and they also include deep reductions in other greenhouse gas emissions (high confidence) [4].

The level of peak warming depends on cumulative CO2 emissions until the time of net zero CO2 and on the change in non-CO2 climate forcers by the time of peaking (high confidence) [4].

In modelled pathways that report CDR and limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, global cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 is 30–780 GtCO2 from bioenergy with carbon dioxide capture and storage (BECCS) and 0–310 GtCO2 from direct air carbon dioxide capture and storage (DACCS) (high confidence) [4].

In those pathways the land sector contributes 20–400 GtCO2 of net negative emissions, and total cumulative net negative CO2 emissions across all removal options are 20–660 GtCO2 (high confidence) [4].

In pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 is 170–650 GtCO2 from BECCS and 0–250 GtCO2 from DACCS, the land sector contributes 10–250 GtCO2 of net negative emissions, and total cumulative net negative CO2 emissions are around 40 [0–290] GtCO2 (high confidence) [4].

All of the IPCC's Illustrative Mitigation Pathways use land-based biological CDR (primarily afforestation and reforestation) and/or BECCS, and some include DACCS [1].

Across scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, Chapter 12 gives median annual volumes in 2050 of 2.75 (0.52–9.45) GtCO2 for BECCS, 2.98 (0.23–6.38) GtCO2 for removal from agriculture, forestry and other land use (mainly afforestation and reforestation) and 0.02 (0–1.74) GtCO2 for DACCS [1].

Chapter 12 adds that while national mitigation portfolios aiming at net zero or net-negative emissions will need to include some level of CDR, the choice of methods and the scale and timing of their deployment will depend on the ambition for gross emissions reductions, how sustainability and feasibility constraints are managed, and how political preferences and social acceptability evolve [1].

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report's scenarios of highest ambition pursued with immediate action deploy 3.9 (full range 2.7–4.1) GtCO2 of CDR by 2035 and 8.8 (full range 6.5–13.3) GtCO2 by 2050, with temperatures peaking between 1.7°C and 1.8°C [3].

CDR is deployed at scale even in scenarios modelling current climate targets and pledges, primarily through expanded use of conventional CDR on land and with novel CDR reaching gigatonne levels by 2050 [3].

Scenarios that delay ambition by ten years tend to have lower near-term CDR deployment through 2050, but then require higher CDR deployment after midcentury to return warming to 1.5°C [3].

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that, in its scenarios that delay ambition, a decade of delay in mitigation ambition means an additional 0.15 °C of increased temperature and associated impacts (median across scenarios), even though these scenarios scale up CDR later [3].

Deep greenhouse gas emission reductions by 2030 and 2040 lead to less reliance on net negative CO2 emissions that reverse warming in the latter half of the century (high confidence) [4].

Pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot involve more rapid and deeper near-term emission reductions and have less net negative CO2 emissions and less CDR in the longer term than pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot (high confidence) [4].

Pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) have on average lower net negative CO2 emissions than pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot and pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot (high confidence) [4].

Pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot show near-term emission reductions similar to pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), and for a given peak warming level, greater and more rapid near-term reductions are associated with later net zero CO2 dates (high confidence) [4].

Early and sustained emissions reduction actions ease CDR scaling and feasibility concerns, the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds, whereas delay creates higher future reliance on CDR [3].

How much removal is done now

Afforestation, reforestation, improved forest management, agroforestry and soil carbon sequestration are currently the only widely practised CDR methods (high confidence) [4].

Conventional CDR from afforestation and reforestation averaged 2,200 MtCO2 per year over the decade from 2014 to 2023, with a model range of between 1,800 MtCO2 and 2,600 MtCO2 per year [2].

The transfer of carbon from managed forests to durable wood products averaged 780 MtCO2 per year from 2014 to 2023; after the CO2 re-emissions from the decay of existing wood products, the net increase in carbon stored is estimated at 330 MtCO2 per year [2].

Activity from novel CDR methods is estimated at 2.04 MtCO2 globally in 2025, up from 1.4 MtCO2 in 2023, with average annual growth of 36% per year from 2020 to 2025, led by biochar, although the volume from some novel methods declined from 2023 to 2025 [2].

Biochar (1.46 MtCO2, of which 0.97 MtCO2 is a net transfer of woody carbon) and BECCS (0.51 MtCO2) were the largest contributors to novel CDR activity in 2025, followed by biomass direct storage (0.05 MtCO2) [2].

Projects operating and under construction suggest novel CDR capacity of approximately 8.4 MtCO2 per year by 2030, but real-world increases in removal in recent years have achieved only 20% of projected capacity [2].

Chapter 12 assesses DACCS as currently at a medium technology readiness level, and enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods as currently at a low technology readiness level [1].

Global rates of carbon capture and storage deployment are currently far below those in modelled pathways limiting global warming to 1.5°C or 2°C (high confidence) [4].

The limits and risks of removal

Costs range from USD -45 to 100 per tonne of CO2 for soil carbon sequestration to USD 100–300 per tonne for DACCS (medium confidence) [4].

Estimated storage time scales vary from decades to centuries for methods that store carbon in vegetation and through soil carbon management, to 10,000 years or more for methods that store carbon in geological formations (high confidence) [4].

Removal through vegetation and soil management can be reversed by human or natural disturbances and is prone to climate change impacts, whereas CO2 stored in geological and ocean reservoirs and as carbon in biochar is less prone to reversal (high confidence) [4].

Afforestation or production of biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, when poorly implemented, can have adverse socio-economic and environmental impacts, including on biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially at large scales and where land tenure is insecure (high confidence) [4].

Reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon sequestration, peatland restoration and blue carbon management can enhance biodiversity and ecosystem functions, employment and local livelihoods, depending on context (high confidence) [4].

Despite limited current deployment, Chapter 12 estimates moderate to large future mitigation potentials for DACCS, enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods (medium evidence, medium agreement) [1].

For direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS) and enhanced weathering, Chapter 12 finds that there is potential in these categories, but given the current technology readiness levels, for 2030 the potential is limited [1].

The potential for DACCS, 5–40 GtCO2 per year, is limited mainly by requirements for low-carbon energy and by cost [1].

Enhanced weathering has the potential to remove 2–4 (full range: <1 to about 100) GtCO2 per year, at costs ranging from USD 50 to 200 (full range: USD 24–578) per tonne of CO2 [1].

Ocean-based methods have a combined potential to remove 1–100 GtCO2 per year at costs of USD 40–500 per tonne of CO2, but their feasibility is uncertain due to possible side effects on the marine environment [1].

Carbon capture and storage is also an option to reduce emissions from large-scale fossil-based energy and industry sources, provided geological storage is available; capture and subsurface injection is a mature technology for gas processing and enhanced oil recovery, but less mature in the power sector and in cement and chemicals production [4].

When CO2 is captured directly from the atmosphere (DACCS) or from biomass (BECCS), carbon capture and storage provides the storage component of these removal methods (high confidence) [4].

The technical geological CO2 storage capacity is estimated to be on the order of 1000 GtCO2, more than the CO2 storage requirements through 2100 to limit global warming to 1.5°C, although the regional availability of geological storage could be a limiting factor (high confidence) [4].

If the geological storage site is appropriately selected and managed, the CO2 is estimated to be permanently isolated from the atmosphere, but implementation of carbon capture and storage currently faces technological, economic, institutional, ecological-environmental and socio-cultural barriers (high confidence) [4].

Land-based mitigation options, when sustainably implemented, can deliver large-scale emission reductions and enhanced removals but cannot fully compensate for delayed action in other sectors (high confidence) [4].

Sustainably sourced agricultural and forest products can also be used instead of more greenhouse-gas-intensive products in other sectors (high confidence) [4].

Barriers to these land-based options and trade-offs may result from the impacts of climate change, competing demands on land, conflicts with food security and livelihoods, the complexity of land ownership and management systems, and cultural aspects, though there are many country-specific opportunities to provide co-benefits and avoid risks (high confidence) [4].

Upscaling the deployment of CDR depends on developing effective approaches to address feasibility and sustainability constraints, especially at large scales (high confidence) [4].

Enablers of CDR include accelerated research, development and demonstration, improved tools for risk assessment and management, targeted incentives and agreed methods for measurement, reporting and verification of carbon flows (high confidence) [4].

Increasing required volumes of CDR intensifies social and economic constraints and raises sustainability concerns related to land, water, ecosystems and resource demands, the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds [3].

Removal in the wider mitigation picture

Global greenhouse gas emissions are projected to peak between 2020 and at the latest before 2025 in pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and in 2°C (>67%) pathways that assume immediate action, with rapid and deep reductions following through 2030, 2040 and 2050 (high confidence), while without stronger policies emissions are projected to rise beyond 2025, leading to a median warming of 3.2 [2.2 to 3.5] °C by 2100 (medium confidence) [4].

All pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and those that limit it to 2°C (>67%), involve rapid and deep and in most cases immediate emission reductions in all sectors, through strategies that include deploying CDR to counterbalance residual greenhouse gas emissions (high confidence) [4].

The IPCC's summary figure of these pathways is headed: modelled mitigation pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C, and 2°C, involve deep, rapid and sustained emissions reductions [4].

Pathways consistent with national pledges announced before COP26 will likely exceed 1.5°C during the 21st century, and those that then return warming to 1.5°C by 2100 imply an overshoot of 0.15°C–0.3°C, with global cumulative net-negative CO2 emissions of –380 [–860 to –200] GtCO2 in the second half of the century and a rapid acceleration of other mitigation efforts across all sectors after 2030 (high confidence) [4].

Such overshoot pathways imply increased climate-related risk, increased feasibility concerns and greater social and environmental risks than pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot (high confidence) [4].

Future non-CO2 warming depends on reductions in non-CO2 greenhouse gases, aerosols and ozone precursors, and the projected reduction of aerosol emissions, mostly from cutting fossil fuel combustion that lacked effective air pollution controls, leads to net warming in the near to mid term (high confidence) [4].

Non-CO2 greenhouse gas emissions at the time of net zero CO2 are about 8 [5–11] GtCO2-eq per year, mostly methane, and deep cuts in methane up to that time effectively reduce peak warming (high confidence) [4].

At the time of global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, net negative CO2 emissions counterbalance metric-weighted non-CO2 greenhouse gas emissions, and pathways that reach and sustain global net zero greenhouse gas emissions are projected to result in a gradual decline of global warming (high confidence) [4].

About half of the pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions in the second half of the century, and they show warming falling after the peak to 1.2 [1.1–1.4] °C by 2100, against 1.4 [1.3–1.5] °C in those that do not (high confidence) [4].

In modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) (C3 category), there is no significant difference in warming by 2100 between those pathways that reach net zero GHGs (around 30%) and those that do not (high confidence), and where net zero greenhouse gas is reached it comes around 10–40 years later than net zero CO2 (medium confidence) [4].

All mitigation strategies face implementation challenges, and challenges such as dependence on CDR and pressure on land and biodiversity are significantly reduced in pathways that use resources more efficiently or shift global development towards sustainability (high confidence) [4].

Aggregate CDR deployment in modelled pathways is higher than total net negative CO2 emissions, because some of the deployed CDR counterbalances remaining gross emissions [4].

Cumulative CDR from the land sector cannot be quantified precisely, because some pathways assess it against a baseline and models report gross emissions and removals differently, but it equals or exceeds the net negative emissions given; the IPCC's ranges are the 5–95th percentile across pathways that include the specific method [4].

Transport-related CO2 emissions fall by 59% (42–68% interquartile range) by 2050 in 1.5°C pathways and by 29% [14–44% interquartile range] in 2°C pathways, but the transport sector likely does not reach zero CO2 emissions by 2100, so negative emissions are likely needed to counterbalance its residual CO2 emissions (high confidence) [4].

Ambitious mitigation in cities will encompass reducing or changing energy and material consumption, electrification, and enhancing carbon uptake and storage in the urban environment, and cities can achieve net-zero emissions only if emissions are reduced within and outside their boundaries (very high confidence) [4].

The potential and sequencing of city mitigation strategies vary with a city's land use, spatial form, development level and state of urbanisation, from retrofitting buildings and supporting walking, cycling and public transport in established cities to compact urban form in growing ones and energy-efficient infrastructure in new ones (high confidence) [4].

For cities, three broad strategies are effective when implemented together: reducing or changing energy and material use, electrification with low-emission energy, and enhancing carbon uptake and storage in the urban environment, for example through bio-based building materials, green roofs, trees and green spaces (very high confidence) [4].

Land-based removal: potential, benefits and trade-offs

Scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower commonly involve extensive mitigation in agriculture, forestry and other land use that also provides biomass for other sectors, and bioenergy is the most land-intensive renewable energy option (robust evidence, high agreement) [1].

Growing demands for food, feed, biomaterials and non-fossil fuels increase competition for land and biomass while climate change adds stresses on land, but appropriate integration of bioenergy and other mitigation options with existing land uses can improve resource use efficiency, ease pressure on ecosystems and support adaptation (medium evidence, high agreement) [1].

Chapter 12 finds that CDR options can have positive impacts on ecosystem services and the Sustainable Development Goals but also potential adverse side effects, and land-based measures may also involve trade-offs, so implementing them would depend on how societies prioritise mitigation against food, material well-being and nature conservation, and on society's future dependence on CDR (medium evidence, medium agreement) [1].

Governance of CDR and land-based mitigation can support effective and equitable policy implementation, but needs coordinated effort across many actors and faces challenges such as establishing reliable systems for monitoring, reporting and verification (medium evidence, high agreement) [1].

Integrating CDR into mitigation portfolios can build on existing rules, procedures and instruments for emissions abatement, and incentivising its deployment requires a political commitment to formal integration into existing climate policy frameworks, including reliable verification of carbon flows [1].

Chapter 12 adds that, to avoid CDR being misperceived as a substitute for deep emissions reductions, the prioritisation of emissions cuts can be signalled and achieved with differentiated target setting for reductions and removals [1].

The governance of land-based CDR can draw on lessons from regulating biofuels and forest carbon but requires integrated land-use planning beyond project-level approaches, while food systems governance may be pioneered through local food policy initiatives [1].

The economic mitigation potential of land-sector options between 2020 and 2050, at costs below USD100 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, is 8–14 GtCO2-eq per year, 30–50% of it available at less than USD20 and could be upscaled in the near term across most regions (high confidence), with the largest share [4.2–7.4 GtCO2-eq per year] from the conservation, improved management and restoration of forests and other ecosystems [4].

Improved crop and livestock management and carbon sequestration in agriculture, including soil carbon management, agroforestry and biochar, can contribute 1.8–4.1 GtCO2-eq per year, and demand-side and material substitution measures 2.1 [1.1–3.6] GtCO2-eq per year [4].

Demand-side measures with sustainable intensification of agriculture can free up land for reforestation and restoration, and wood products from sustainably managed forests also have potential through longer-lived products, recycling or material substitution [4].

These land-sector measures cannot compensate for delayed emission reductions in other sectors, and persistent and region-specific barriers continue to hamper their economic and political feasibility (medium confidence) [4].

Land-sector sequestration and emission-reduction options have both co-benefits and risks for biodiversity, food and water security, wood supply, livelihoods and the land rights of Indigenous Peoples, local communities and small land owners, with the scale of benefit or risk depending on the activity, deployment strategy and context, and risks can be avoided when the needs of multiple stakeholders are met (high confidence) [4].

Realising the land sector's mitigation potential entails overcoming institutional, economic and policy constraints, with barriers including insufficient support, uncertainty over long-term additionality, weak governance, insecure land ownership and the risk of reversal (high confidence) [4].

Limited access to technology, data and know-how is also a barrier, research and development are key, and the mitigation of agricultural methane and nitrous oxide is still constrained by cost and the complexity of agricultural systems (high confidence) [4].

Land-based options such as reforestation, forest conservation, ecosystem restoration, agroforestry and soil carbon management can have multiple synergies with the Sustainable Development Goals, with synergies and trade-offs depending on practices, scale, governance and the involvement of local communities and Indigenous Peoples (high confidence) [4].

Well-implemented land-based mitigation can avoid trade-offs in employment, water use, land-use competition and biodiversity, and the sustainability of bioenergy depends on feedstock, land management, climatic region and the timing, scale and speed of deployment (medium confidence) [4].

CDR methods such as soil carbon sequestration and biochar can improve soil quality and food production capacity, and ecosystem restoration and reforestation can enhance biodiversity and provide additional biomass but can displace food production and livelihoods, though limited application today leaves some uncertainty about potential benefits (high confidence) [4].

Mitigation options have synergies with many Sustainable Development Goals, but some can also have trade-offs, and these vary with context and scale [4].

What this adds up to

Taken together, these sources treat removal as a complement to cutting emissions rather than a substitute for it: it is needed to balance the emissions hardest to cut and, later, to take warming back down, and how much is needed depends on how fast emissions fall.
