Removing carbon dioxide from the air: what part can it play in reaching net zero?

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

What the assessments find

The IPCC's 2022 mitigation assessment finds that the deployment of carbon dioxide removal to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or greenhouse gas emissions are to be achieved (high confidence).

All global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and those that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), involve rapid and deep and in most cases immediate greenhouse gas emission reductions in all sectors, and their mitigation strategies include deploying removal methods to counterbalance residual greenhouse gas emissions (high confidence).

Its chapter on removal calls carbon dioxide removal a necessary element to achieve net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas emissions both globally and nationally, counterbalancing residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors (robust evidence, high agreement).

The assessment describes removal as able to fulfil three complementary roles in addition to deep, rapid and sustained emission reductions: lowering net emissions in the near term, counterbalancing hard-to-abate residual emissions such as those from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes to help reach net zero in the mid-term, and achieving net negative emissions in the long term if deployed at levels exceeding annual residual emissions (high confidence).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report (2024) says that the most important mitigation strategy in the near term is reducing emissions, and that removing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is also necessary, alongside rapidly reducing emissions, to meet climate goals.

In the IPCC's modelled pathways that reach global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, at the point they reach it, around 74% [54 to 90%] of global emission reductions are achieved by CO2 reductions in energy supply and demand, 13% [4 to 20%] by CO2 mitigation options in the land sector and 13% [10 to 18%] through the reduction of non-CO2 emissions (medium confidence).

In modelled pathways that reach global net zero CO2 emissions, at the point they reach net zero, 5–16 GtCO2 of emissions from some sectors are compensated for by net negative CO2 emissions in other sectors (high confidence).

Net-zero CO2 energy systems entail a substantial reduction in overall fossil fuel use, minimal use of unabated fossil fuels and use of CCS in the remaining fossil fuel system, electricity systems that emit no net CO2, widespread electrification of the energy system, and energy conservation and efficiency (high confidence).

The assessment finds that removal will be needed to counterbalance residual emissions in the energy sector (high confidence).

Maintaining emission-intensive energy systems may, in some regions and sectors, be more expensive than transitioning to low-emission systems, low-emission energy transitions will have multiple co-benefits, including improvements in air quality and health, and the long-term economic attractiveness of energy-system mitigation options depends on policy design, technology, institutional capacity, equity, finance and public support (high confidence).

Electricity systems powered predominantly by renewables are becoming increasingly viable, but it will be more challenging to supply the entire energy system with renewable energy, and a broad portfolio of options will ultimately be needed to accommodate large shares of renewables (high confidence).

In both 1.5°C (>50%) and 2°C (>67%) scenarios, the transport sector likely does not reach zero CO2 emissions by 2100, so negative emissions are likely needed to counterbalance its residual CO2 emissions (high confidence).

Options in agriculture, forestry and other land use, when sustainably implemented, can deliver large-scale emission reductions and enhanced removals, but cannot fully compensate for delayed action in other sectors (high confidence).

Now: how much is removed

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report estimates that around 2 GtCO2 per year of carbon dioxide removal is taking place already.

Almost all of this comes from conventional methods, principally afforestation and reforestation, which have delivered a relatively stable rate of removal over the past two decades.

Novel methods, which are generally at an earlier stage of development, contribute 1.3 million tons (0.0013 Gt) of removal per year, less than 0.1% of the total, although they are growing more rapidly than conventional methods.

Of this 1.3 million tons, less than 0.6 million tons per year involves geological storage of CO2.

The IPCC assessment likewise finds that afforestation, reforestation, improved forest management, agroforestry and soil carbon sequestration are currently the only widely practiced removal methods (high confidence).

It groups the processes by which CO2 is removed from the atmosphere as biological, geochemical or chemical (high confidence).

In the decades ahead

In the IPCC's modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, cumulative removal during 2020–2100 is 30–780 GtCO2 from bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) and 0–310 GtCO2 from direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS), and the land sector contributes 20–400 GtCO2 of net negative emissions (high confidence).

In pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), the corresponding figures are 170–650 GtCO2 from BECCS and 0–250 GtCO2 from DACCS, with 10–250 GtCO2 of net negative emissions from the land sector (high confidence).

The IPCC notes that aggregate levels of removal in these pathways are higher than total net negative CO2 emissions, because some of the removal deployed counterbalances remaining gross emissions, and that cumulative removal from the land sector cannot be quantified precisely.

Across the scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, the chapter gives median annual volumes in 2050 (5–95% range) of 2.75 (0.52–9.45) GtCO2 for BECCS, 2.98 (0.23–6.38) GtCO2 for removal from the land sector (mainly afforestation and reforestation) and 0.02 (0–1.74) GtCO2 for DACCS.

Global net zero CO2 emissions are reached in the early 2050s in modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and around the early 2070s in pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), and many of these pathways continue to net negative CO2 emissions after the point of net zero (high confidence).

At the time of global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, net negative CO2 emissions counterbalance metric-weighted non-CO2 greenhouse gas emissions, and about half of the assessed pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions during the second half of the 21st century, while in pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) there is no significant difference in warming by 2100 between those that reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions and those that do not (high confidence).

In pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower and that do reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions, net zero greenhouse gas emissions occur around 10–40 years later than net zero CO2 emissions (medium confidence).

The IPCC chapter estimates moderate to large future mitigation potentials for DACCS, enhanced weathering and ocean-based removal methods, despite their limited current deployment (medium evidence, medium agreement).

The chapter puts the potential for DACCS at 5–40 GtCO2 per year, limited mainly by requirements for low-carbon energy and by cost (USD100–300, full range USD84–386, per tonne of CO2), and DACCS is currently at a medium technology readiness level.

Enhanced weathering has the potential to remove 2–4 (full range: <1 to about 100) GtCO2 per year at costs of USD50 to 200 (full range: USD24–578) per tonne, and ocean-based methods have a combined potential to remove 1–100 GtCO2 per year at USD40–500 per tonne, but their feasibility is uncertain due to possible side effects on the marine environment.

Enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods are currently at a low technology readiness level.

The economic mitigation potential of land-sector options between 2020 and 2050, at costs below USD100 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, is projected at 8–14 GtCO2-eq per year (high confidence).

The largest share of that potential, 4.2–7.4 GtCO2-eq per year, comes from the conservation, improved management and restoration of forests and other ecosystems, while improved crop and livestock management and carbon sequestration in agriculture can contribute 1.8–4.1 GtCO2-eq per year and demand-side and material substitution measures 2.1 [1.1–3.6] GtCO2-eq per year.

Demand-side measures together with sustainable intensification of agriculture can also free up land for reforestation and restoration, and wood products from sustainably managed forests have further potential, but land-sector measures cannot compensate for delayed emission reductions in other sectors and persistent, region-specific barriers continue to hamper their feasibility (medium confidence).

All of the IPCC's Illustrative Mitigation Pathways use land-based biological removal (primarily afforestation and reforestation) and/or BECCS, and some include DACCS.

Across a subset of scenarios that the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report considers more sustainable, the central range of removal is 7 to 9 GtCO2 per year in 2050.

How much removal is needed depends on how fast emissions fall: the IPCC finds that pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot involve more rapid and deeper near-term emission reductions and less removal in the longer term than pathways that return warming to 1.5°C (>50%) after a high overshoot (high confidence).

Modelled global emission pathways consistent with the national pledges (NDCs) announced before COP26 will likely exceed 1.5°C during the 21st century.

Pathways that exceed 1.5°C and then return warming to 1.5°C (>50%) by 2100 involve global cumulative net-negative CO2 emissions of –380 [–860 to –200] GtCO2 in the second half of the century, and imply increased climate-related risk, increased feasibility concerns and greater social and environmental risks than pathways with no or limited overshoot (high confidence).

Dependence on removal is one of the implementation challenges that are significantly reduced in modelled pathways that assume using resources more efficiently or that shift global development towards sustainability (high confidence).

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal report finds that the amount of removal countries have proposed falls short of what is required to meet the Paris temperature goal.

The report adds that there are limits to removal's capability to counteract inadequate efforts to reduce emissions.

Limits and risks

Removal methods vary in their maturity, removal process, time scale of carbon storage, storage medium, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, impacts and risks, and governance requirements (high confidence).

The IPCC chapter adds that implementation strategies need to reflect these differences between methods.

Removal and storage of CO2 through vegetation and soil management can be reversed by human or natural disturbances and is prone to climate change impacts, while CO2 stored in geological and ocean reservoirs and as carbon in biochar is less prone to reversal (high confidence).

Estimated storage time scales range from decades to centuries for vegetation and soil carbon to 10,000 years or more for geological formations (high confidence).

Costs range from USD-45–100 per tonne of CO2 for soil carbon sequestration to USD100–300 per tonne for DACCS (medium confidence).

Reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon sequestration, peatland restoration and blue carbon management are examples of methods that can enhance biodiversity and ecosystem functions, employment and local livelihoods, depending on context (high confidence).

Afforestation or the production of biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, when poorly implemented, can have adverse impacts on biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially at large scales and where land tenure is insecure (high confidence).

Land-based carbon sequestration and emission reduction options have both co-benefits and risks for biodiversity and ecosystem conservation, food and water security, wood supply, livelihoods and the land rights of Indigenous Peoples, local communities and small land owners, and options that compete for land and land-based resources can pose risks (high confidence).

Large-scale or poorly planned deployment of bioenergy, biochar and afforestation of naturally unforested land can increase competition for land, water and biomass and so reduce adaptive capacity, in particular where land and water resources are very limited (high confidence).

Even if extensive global mitigation efforts are implemented, there will be a large need for financial, technical and human resources for adaptation, and limited resources in social and institutional systems can lead to poorly coordinated responses, thus increasing risk (high confidence).

Scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower by 2100 commonly involve extensive mitigation in agriculture, forestry and other land use that also provides biomass for mitigation in other sectors, and bioenergy is the most land-intensive renewable energy option (robust evidence, high agreement).

The chapter adds that growing demands for food, feed, biomaterials and non-fossil fuels increase the competition for land and biomass, and that appropriate integration of bioenergy and other mitigation options with existing land and biomass uses can improve resource use efficiency and mitigate pressures on natural ecosystems (medium evidence, high agreement).

Land-based options such as reforestation, forest conservation, agroforestry and soil carbon management can have multiple synergies with the Sustainable Development Goals (high confidence).

Soil carbon sequestration and biochar can improve soil quality and food production capacity, while ecosystem restoration and reforestation can enhance biodiversity but can displace food production and livelihoods, although because some of these options have limited application today there are some uncertainties about their potential benefits (high confidence).

The synergies and trade-offs of these land-based options vary with context and scale.

The IPCC finds that upscaling the deployment of removal depends on developing effective approaches to address feasibility and sustainability constraints, especially at large scales (high confidence).

Enablers of removal include accelerated research, development and demonstration, improved tools for risk assessment and management, targeted incentives and the development of agreed methods for measurement, reporting and verification of carbon flows (high confidence).

What this adds up to

Taken together, these assessments treat removing carbon dioxide as a complement to deep emission cuts rather than a substitute for them: needed now and later to balance emissions that are hard to eliminate and, beyond net zero, to make emissions net negative, but delivered today almost entirely by forests, at well below the 7 to 9 GtCO2 a year that the more sustainable scenarios use in 2050.
