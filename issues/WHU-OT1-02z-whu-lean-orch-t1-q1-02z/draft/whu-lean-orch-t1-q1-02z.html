Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A systematic review of 28 studies concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe this as emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. They also judge the quality of the included studies to be largely low [1]. On which kinds work best, the authors say that more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious and how best to deliver them [1].

What the review found

The evidence base

The review authors searched for studies up to March 2022, and the review was published in February 2025 [1]. Most included studies were aimed at children with disabilities, and a few targeted family members or service providers [1]. People with intellectual, learning or developmental impairments were the groups most frequently targeted by interventions [1]. Eight of the studies were from India and four were from Iran, with the rest spread across countries including China, South Africa, Egypt, Brazil, Kenya, Uganda and Zambia [1]. None of the studies were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. According to the source, "All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings" [1]. According to the source, a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. According to the source, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. According to the source, "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed" [1].

Skills for learning

Of the 18 studies analysed for effects on skills for learning, 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but they rest on a low number of studies [1]. For example, the pooled effect for numeracy was d = 4.60 from two studies with high heterogeneity, and the effect for handwriting was 4.97, also from only two studies [1]. For speech and communication, the pooled effect was insignificant (d = 0.70, from five studies with high heterogeneity) [1]. For school behaviour and adaptive functioning, the pooled effect was also insignificant (d = 0.49, from five studies) [1]. Even so, the narrative summary reports positive effects of interventions on speech and communication and on school behaviour and adaptive functioning [1]. The authors note that some of these skill categories are likely to suffer from publication bias, and that publication bias for numeracy and handwriting cannot be assessed because only two studies were available for each [1].

Inclusion, learning environments and attendance

Studies concerned with conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education showed a moderate effect (d = 0.35, 0.12 to 0.58) [1]. That effect was based on a low number of studies with high heterogeneity and potentially moderate publication bias [1]. Studies aimed at making learning environments more accessible reported positive impacts on teacher knowledge and attitudes, parent knowledge and attitudes, and violence reduction [1]. One of these was the Good School Toolkit, a school-wide programme evaluated in a randomised controlled trial with 3,820 participants in Uganda, which aimed to reduce violence from peers and staff toward students with and without disabilities [1]. Improving attendance, enrolment and completion was achieved in only one study, which improved teachers' knowledge and attitudes about inclusive education [1]. That study was a teacher-training programme in Kenya evaluated with an uncontrolled before-versus-after design [1]. No studies covered the accessibility of the built environment and learning materials, or inclusive education policies [1].

Limits of the evidence

The interventions and outcome measures used by the included studies were all different, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. Most study designs could not account for many potential confounders, and masking, baseline balance and attribution were poorly reported [1]. The review may also be biased because it included little grey literature and no non-English literature [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies were rated low confidence in their findings overall, so the evidence on these programmes is weak [1]. The review found that the lack of confidence in the evidence across studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs, and did not always reflect multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. The source says a twin-track approach is needed, one that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also ensures their inclusion in mainstream activities, for example by improving teacher skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1].

What holds up

What holds up is that programmes teaching individual children specific skills, such as literacy, numeracy, handwriting and cognitive skills, have shown significant effects, though these come from few studies of largely low quality. Programmes changing schools and teachers show a moderate effect on conditions for inclusion, but there is almost no evidence on getting children with disabilities to enrol, attend and complete school. The sources do not establish which kinds of programme work best, and the review's authors explicitly call for more research on that question. The authors argue that interventions are needed which target both the individual with a disability and their broader environment, such as teacher training programmes and curriculum modifications [1]. They also call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1].
