Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work? What the available passages can and cannot tell us

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

The passages come from a protocol for a systematic review, which describes a planned review rather than reporting its results, so they do not say whether these programmes work [2]. The protocol states that evidence on which interventions are effective in achieving the goals set by international directives on the right to education of people with disabilities has not been established or comprehensively reviewed [2]. Which interventions work, and which appear most effective for different types of disability, are listed as questions the review sets out to answer, not as findings [2]. The protocol notes that the article has since been updated by a full systematic review, but that review's findings are not among these passages [2].

What the protocol says about the problem, the evidence gap and the planned review

The educational gap for people with disabilities

Recent reports cited in the protocol showed that people with disabilities were consistently falling behind their peers without disabilities in school enrolment, school completion, mean years of schooling and literacy levels [2]. UNESCO's 2020 report, as cited, noted that children with disabilities make up 15% of the out-of-school population [2]. The same report noted that individuals with a sensory, physical or intellectual disability are two-and-a-half times more likely than nondisabled individuals to have never been in school [2]. The protocol says evidence suggests the attainment gap between people with and without disabilities is greatest in low- and middle-income countries [2]. A 2014 study it cites found children with disabilities to be 5–10 times more likely to be excluded from school than children without disabilities [2]. The protocol reports barriers at the level of the system, such as lack of policy, at the level of the school, such as inaccessible infrastructure or a lack of skilled teachers, and at the level of the family and child, such as poor health [2]. Large class sizes and poor attitudes among mainstream educators are also reported to hinder educational success among children with disabilities in these countries [2].

The state of the evidence on interventions

Past evidence syntheses cited in the protocol highlighted that very little literature in low- and middle-income countries has examined educational outcomes comparing disabled and nondisabled peers [2]. According to the protocol, most studies have focused on special school populations and on epidemiological questions rather than attendance or attainment [2]. The protocol concludes that there is a real need to evaluate interventions in this area to determine what works [2]. The existing Cochrane reviews it identifies are far narrower in scope [2]. Those reviews cover parent-mediated communication interventions, music education for dyslexia, and task-oriented interventions for developmental co-ordination disorder [2]. An earlier evidence and gap map of interventions for people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries included 46 studies related to education outcomes [2].

Kinds of programmes the review will examine

The protocol groups interventions in clusters along a causal chain [2]. The first cluster covers structural interventions and the conditions of the learning environment [2]. The second cluster covers skills for learning [2]. The third cluster covers attendance and enrolment [2]. The final cluster covers educational outcomes such as qualifications, education-related quality of life and transitions between levels of education [2]. Inclusive education models range from placing children with disabilities in mainstream schools without additional supports to providing a range of supports, such as teaching aides, occupational therapists and specialist disability training for teachers [2]. The review plans to arrange inclusive education interventions on a continuum from lowest to highest intensity [2]. On special versus inclusive settings, the protocol says only that children with certain types of impairments may benefit from separate instruction [2]. It also notes that there are issues with social integration, communication and friendship for deaf children in mainstream schools [2].

How the review plans to judge effectiveness

Only impact evaluations will be included, and descriptive studies such as qualitative interviews and single time-point cross-sectional surveys will be excluded [2]. Studies will be combined by meta-analysis only when two or more effect sizes use a similar outcome construct and a similar comparison group [2]. The narrative synthesis will describe findings by suboutcome and note the strength of the evidence, evidence gaps and confidence in the evidence [2]. For subgroups such as children, women and different impairment groups, the authors expect any findings to be descriptive and tentative [2].

What holds up

Nothing in these passages shows that any education programme for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries works. Nothing in them shows that one kind of programme works better than another. What does hold up is the protocol authors' own statement that evidence on which interventions are effective has not been established. Also well supported is their description of the existing research as thin and narrowly focused. The passages are firmer on the size of the educational gap and the barriers behind it than on any remedy. Answering which kinds of programme work best would require the results of the completed review, which these passages do not contain.
