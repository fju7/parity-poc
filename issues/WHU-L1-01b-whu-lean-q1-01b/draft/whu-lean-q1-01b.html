Do phone and tablet tracking tools improve primary care?

The question

The question is: “In primary health care, does using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improve the care people receive and their health?”

The short answer

The review we rely on concludes that digital tracking may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, the quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use.

It also reports that these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies.

Most of its findings rest on low-certainty evidence, often from a single study, so the honest answer is that these tools may help some parts of care and some health outcomes, usually by small amounts, and that for many outcomes the effect is small, absent or uncertain.

What the evidence is

The review looked for randomised and non-randomised trials in community and primary care settings, with primary care providers and their clients as participants and usual care without digital tracking as the comparison.

It compared three combinations against usual care: digital tracking with clinical decision support for health workers, digital tracking with targeted messages to clients, and digital tracking with both.

Its searches covered 2000 to November 2022.

It identified 18 eligible studies (11 randomised, seven non-randomised) conducted in Bangladesh, China, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Palestine, Uganda, and the USA.

All the non-randomised studies had a high risk of bias, and the review's results are from the randomised studies.

The review graded the certainty of its evidence with GRADE, and its words carry that grade: probably means moderate-certainty evidence, may means low-certainty evidence, and uncertain means very low-certainty evidence.

Tracking with decision support for health workers

For antenatal and postnatal care, tracking with decision support may slightly increase home visits in the week following delivery (2 studies, 4531 participants; RD 0.10 [0.07, 0.14]).

It may slightly increase counselling for initiating complementary feeding (2 studies, 4397 participants; RD 0.12 [0.08, 0.15]) and the mean number of home visits in the month following delivery (1 study, 3023 participants; MD 0.75 [0.47, 1.03]).

Its effect on home visits within 24 hours of delivery is uncertain.

Among clients, it may slightly increase skin-to-skin care (1 study, 1544 participants; RD 0.05 [0.00, 0.10]) and early breastfeeding (2 studies, 4540 participants; RD 0.08 [0.05, 0.12]).

Its effects are uncertain on applying nothing to the umbilical cord, taking at least 90 iron-folate tablets during pregnancy, exclusively breastfeeding for six months, delaying the newborn's bath at least two days and Kangaroo Mother Care.

On health, it may reduce low birthweight babies (1 study, 3023 participants; RR 0.53 [0.38, 0.73]) and may increase infants with pneumonia or fever seeking care (1 study, 3470 participants; RR 1.13 [1.03, 1.24]).

Its effects on stillbirths, neonatal and infant deaths, or testing positive for HIV during antenatal testing are uncertain.

Tracking with reminder messages to patients

The evidence on tracking with targeted client communication comes from one study of 1226 stroke patients over 12 months.

In that study, it may slightly increase blood pressure medication adherence (RR 1.10 [1.00, 1.21]), slightly reduce systolic blood pressure (MD 2.80 mmHg lower) and slightly improve EQ-5D quality-of-life scores (MD 0.04 [0.02, 0.06]).

It may also reduce deaths (RR 0.52 [0.28, 0.96]) and stroke hospitalisations (RR 0.45 [0.32, 0.64]).

Tracking with both decision support and reminder messages

With all three combined, it probably increases guideline adherence for antenatal screening and management of anaemia (1 study, 10,502 participants; OR 1.88 [1.52, 2.32]), diabetes (1 study, 8669 participants; OR 1.45 [1.14, 1.84]) and hypertension (1 study, 15,555 participants; OR 1.62 [1.29, 2.04]).

It probably leads to lower adherence for abnormal foetal growth (1 study, 1165 participants; OR 0.59 [0.37, 0.95]).

It may slightly increase nevirapine prophylaxis in infants of HIV-positive mothers (1 study, 609 participants; OR 1.75 [0.73, 4.19]).

In one study of 6367 pregnant women, it probably slightly reduces missing data for haemoglobin (RR 0.77 [0.71, 0.84]) but leaves slightly more missing data for blood pressure at delivery (RR 1.16 [1.08, 1.24]), and it may have little or no effect on missing data on gestational age or birthweight.

In HIV-positive mothers, it may have little or no effect on being on antiretroviral therapy at delivery (1 study, 438 participants) or on exclusive breastfeeding for six months (1 study, 695 participants).

Its effects on physical activity in adults at high cardiovascular risk are uncertain.

It may reduce the number of deaths in patients with hypertension and diabetes (1 study, 3698 participants; OR 0.61 [0.35, 1.06]) and new cardiovascular events in adults at high cardiovascular risk over 6 to 18 months (1 study, 8642 participants; OR 0.58 [0.42, 0.80]).

It may slightly decrease severe hypertension in antenatal women, but the confidence interval includes both a decrease and an increase (1 study, 6367 participants; OR 0.61 [0.27, 1.37]).

In women receiving antenatal care, it may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes (OR 0.99 [0.87, 1.12]), moderate or severe anaemia (OR 0.82 [0.51, 1.31]), or large-for-gestational-age babies (OR 1.06 [0.90, 1.25]).

In adults with hypertension or diabetes (1 study, 3324 participants), it may make little or no difference to HbA1c, total cholesterol, 10-year cardiovascular risk, tobacco use, alcohol use, or PHQ-9 depression scores.

Its effects are uncertain on maternal or infant mortality before the baby reaches 18 months in HIV-positive mothers, and on blood pressure control, body mass index, fasting glucose and quality of life in adults with hypertension or diabetes.

It may have little or no effect on missed early infant diagnosis visits (1 study, 1183 participants; OR 0.92 [0.63, 1.35]), and its effect on linkage to care is uncertain.

It probably increases slightly the number of adults with hypertension or diabetes reporting a better change in the quality of care (1 study, 3324 participants; RR 1.02 [1.00, 1.03]).

What no study measured

No studies in the review evaluated time between presentation and appropriate management, timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences.

What we held, read and searched

We hold the full text of the Agarwal 2025 Cochrane review [1].

We searched the full text of the Agarwal 2025 Cochrane review [1] and checked the passages relevant to each sentence; we did not read it in full.

We searched: Europe PMC, for titles containing both 'digital tracking' and 'targeted client communication'.

We did not search: other bibliographic databases; other systematic reviews; guidelines, including WHO's guideline on digital interventions; trial registries; the reports of the individual studies the review included.
