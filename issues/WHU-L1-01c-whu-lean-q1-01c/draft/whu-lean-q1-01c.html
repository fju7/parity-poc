Mobile tracking, prompts and reminders in primary care

The question

The question is: “In primary health care, does using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improve the care people receive and their health?”

The short answer

The most direct evidence on this question is a 2025 Cochrane systematic review that set out to assess the effects of using a mobile device to track service use when combined with clinical decision support, with targeted client communications, or with both, in community and primary care settings [1].

It included 18 studies, 11 randomised and seven non-randomised [1].

Its authors conclude that digital tracking may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, the quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use [1].

They add that these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies [1].

Most outcomes were assessed as moderate- or low-certainty evidence [1].

So the tools may improve some parts of care and some health outcomes, mostly by small amounts, and for many outcomes the evidence cannot say whether they help.

What the review found

The review uses "probably" for moderate-certainty evidence, "may" for low-certainty evidence and "uncertain" for very low-certainty evidence [1].

All the non-randomised studies had a high risk of bias, and the results below are from the randomised studies [1].

Tracking with prompts for health workers

Tracking combined with decision support may slightly increase home visits in the week following delivery (2 studies, 4531 participants; RD 0.10 [0.07, 0.14]) [1].

It may slightly increase counselling for initiating complementary feeding (2 studies, 4397 participants; RD 0.12 [0.08, 0.15]) [1].

It may reduce low birthweight babies (1 study, 3023 participants; RR 0.53 [0.38, 0.73]) [1].

Its effects on stillbirths and on neonatal and infant deaths are uncertain [1].

Tracking with reminder messages for patients

In one study of stroke patients followed for 12 months, tracking combined with targeted client communication may reduce deaths (1 study, 1226 participants; RR 0.52 [0.28, 0.96]) [1].

In the same study it may reduce stroke hospitalisations (1 study, 1226 participants; RR 0.45 [0.32, 0.64]) [1].

Tracking with both prompts and reminders

The combination probably increases health workers' guideline adherence for antenatal screening and management of anaemia, diabetes and hypertension, and probably leads to lower adherence for abnormal foetal growth (1 study, 1165 participants; OR 0.59 [0.37, 0.95]) [1].

It may reduce new cardiovascular events in adults at high cardiovascular risk over 6 to 18 months (1 study, 8642 participants; OR 0.58 [0.42, 0.80]) [1].

In women receiving antenatal care, it may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes (OR 0.99 [0.87, 1.12]), moderate or severe anaemia (OR 0.82 [0.51, 1.31]), or large-for-gestational-age babies (OR 1.06 [0.90, 1.25]) [1].

In adults with hypertension or diabetes, it may make little or no difference to HbA1c (MD 0.08 [-0.27, 0.43]), total cholesterol (MD -2.50 [-7.10, 2.10]) or 10-year cardiovascular risk (MD -0.40 [-2.30, 1.50]) [1].

Its effects on maternal or infant mortality in HIV-positive mothers, and on blood pressure control, body mass index, fasting glucose and quality of life in adults with hypertension or diabetes, are uncertain [1].

It probably increases slightly the number of adults with hypertension or diabetes reporting a "slightly/much better" change in the quality of care (1 study, 3324 participants; RR 1.02 [1.00, 1.03]) [1].

What the included studies did not measure

The review reports that no included study evaluated the time between presentation and appropriate management, timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences [1].

Where the studies were done

Of the 18 studies, 17 were from low- and middle-income settings where electronic medical record systems are rare or just being rolled out [1].

The authors note that their findings may not be relevant to high-income settings [1].

All studies focused on pregnant or post-natal women and their children, or on adults with chronic disease [1].

What WHO recommends

A 2019 WHO guideline recommends digital tracking of clients' health status and services combined with decision support only under conditions: where the health system can support these components in an integrated manner, and for tasks already within the health worker's scope of practice [2].

It makes the same conditional recommendation for tracking combined with both decision support and targeted client communication, with the added condition that concerns about data privacy and sending sensitive content to clients can be addressed [2].

The guideline's evidence summary says tracking with decision support may improve health service use and health outcomes, such as attendance at antenatal care appointments and immediate breastfeeding [2].

It also says that tracking with decision support probably makes little to no difference to other outcomes, such as the proportion of children under five who are vaccinated or of women who give birth in a facility [2].

The guideline's evidence summary also lists, among the health service use and health outcomes that tracking with decision support may improve, "receipt of the third dose of polio vaccine, and use of postpartum contraception six months after birth" [2].

For tracking with both decision support and targeted client communication, the guideline found limited evidence of effectiveness, with the certainty of that evidence assessed as very low [2].

The guideline also says that digital health interventions are not a substitute for functioning health systems, and that there are significant limitations to what digital health is able to address [2].

What holds up

The review's own summary is that using digital tools to help healthcare workers track patient information and make decisions has mixed results [1].

The belief that these tools improve care and health holds up only in part: the evidence, mostly of moderate or low certainty and for some outcomes very low, points to benefits in some situations, small or no differences in most studies, and no included study of their unintended consequences.

We searched: Europe PMC, for systematic reviews and trials of mobile-device tracking combined with decision support or client reminders in primary health care; the WHO institutional repository, for WHO guidance on digital health interventions.

We did not search: trial registries; MEDLINE, Embase and CENTRAL directly; for individual trials published after the review's November 2022 search date; national guidelines.

Findings in our sources we judged outside this question

Judged not material to this question: “No studies were conducted on other health areas, such as primary mental health care or treatment of non‐paediatric communicable diseases.” (the Cochrane review by Agarwal and colleagues (2025) [1]).
