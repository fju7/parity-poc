What Holds Up — reliability-study issue WHU-S01, not for publication

Does DNA-based TB testing change what happens to patients?

Study draft, 22 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

It probably gets more people a confirmed diagnosis and, in some trials, onto treatment sooner, but the randomised trials have not shown that it saves lives across everyone tested, and they have barely measured cure. The clearest sign of fewer deaths is among people with HIV, in whom the 2021 Cochrane review found the test probably reduced them.

What people have been told

In 2012 the Harvard Gazette reported a modelling study's estimate that replacing the current diagnostic approach, which relies on identifying TB in sputum using a microscope, with one based on the Xpert test would prevent 182,000 TB deaths in southern Africa over 10 years. When South Africa launched its GeneXpert machines in 2011, KwaZulu-Natal's TB head, Bruce Margot, told Health-e News that "we can save patients lives by starting them on treatment immediately". In June 2026 a Global Fund news release on a new molecular test for 13 countries was headlined as bringing lifesaving diagnosis closer to communities. Those are projections and expectations; the rest of this page is about what randomised trials measured.

Survival

A 2021 Cochrane review of the original Xpert MTB/RIF test against smear microscopy found inconclusive evidence of an effect on deaths from any cause (risk ratio 0.89, 95% confidence interval 0.75 to 1.05; five trials, 9,932 participants). Among people known to have HIV, the same review found that the test probably reduced deaths (odds ratio 0.80, 0.67 to 0.96; five trials, 5,855 participants). An analysis of individual patients' data from five trials found that, in the three trials that reported the primary outcome, 4.5% of patients tested with Xpert and 5.3% of those tested by smear had died (pooled adjusted odds ratio 0.88, 0.68 to 1.14). Its authors were unable to confidently rule in nor rule out a reduction in mortality among outpatients tested for tuberculosis. In HIV-positive patients that analysis found no significant difference in its primary outcome (odds ratio 0.83, 0.65 to 1.05), but a secondary time-to-death analysis of data from four studies found a lower rate of death with Xpert (hazard ratio 0.76, 0.60 to 0.97), which the authors said suggests the possibility of population-level impact. In the South African trial embedded in the national roll-out, 3.9% of the Xpert group and 5.0% of the microscopy group had died at six months (adjusted risk ratio 1.10, 95% CI 0.75–1.62), a difference the authors reported as no difference, and they concluded that Xpert did not reduce mortality at 6 months compared with sputum microscopy.

For the newer Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra test, a 2025 Cochrane review found high-certainty evidence from one trial that using it instead of smear microscopy made little to no difference to deaths from any cause at six months (risk ratio 0.79, 0.61 to 1.01) or at 18 months (adjusted rate ratio 0.77, 0.46 to 1.29). It looked for trials of other rapid molecular tests too, such as Truenat and TB-LAMP, but the two trials it could include both compared Xpert Ultra with smear microscopy, in 11,228 adults aged 18 years and above. In Uganda's XPEL-TB trial, the planned mortality analysis found an adjusted death rate ratio of 0.77 (95% CI 0.47 to 1.28) for the intervention against routine care. Its authors wrote that consistent point estimates favoring the intervention in their trial and previous ones suggest that Xpert testing may have an impact on mortality at community health centers, and that such estimates across studies suggest a likely population-level impact, but that the magnitude of effect is small and statistically significant results are unlikely to be attained within a single trial.

Cure

On treatment success, the 2021 review was uncertain whether Xpert had no effect or a modest one (odds ratio 1.10, 0.96 to 1.26; three trials, 4,802 participants). The 2025 review found no data on whether Xpert Ultra changes tuberculosis cure rates compared with smear microscopy or culture.

Diagnosis and the start of treatment

Where the reviews say the test probably makes a difference is earlier, between the test and the first dose. The 2021 review found that Xpert probably raised the share of treated people who had bacteriological confirmation (risk ratio 1.44, 1.29 to 1.61) and probably cut the share of patients with bacteriological confirmation lost to follow-up before treatment (risk ratio 0.59, 0.41 to 0.85). In a Cape Town primary care clinic, 13% of people with bacteriologically confirmed TB in the Xpert arm had not started treatment by three months, against 25% under the routine algorithm. A 2022 meta-analysis found that for drug-sensitive TB, Xpert shortened the delay to starting treatment by 2.55 days (95% CI 0.54 to 4.56) compared with sputum microscopy. The 2025 review judged that a testing strategy including Xpert Ultra probably reduces the time to treatment, based on two trials: an adjusted geometric mean ratio of 0.35 (0.21 to 0.58) in one trial of 10,644 participants and a hazard ratio of 3.00 (1.18 to 7.60) in another of 584 participants (moderate-certainty evidence). In that trial, XPEL-TB, more patients were treated for confirmed tuberculosis within 14 days of presenting (342 across ten intervention health centres against 220 across ten control centres; adjusted rate ratio 1.56, 1.21 to 2.01). But XPEL-TB tested a package, not the test alone: on-site molecular testing, guided restructuring of clinic workflows, and monthly feedback of quality metrics. The 2025 review also reported that slightly more people were lost to follow-up in XPEL-TB's Xpert Ultra arm (13.6%) than with smear microscopy (10.1%).

In Brazil, replacing two-sample smear microscopy with single-sample Xpert raised the notification rate of laboratory-confirmed TB by 59% (95% CI 31%, 88%), but the overall notification rate did not increase (15%, 95% CI −6%, 37%). Median time to treatment, measured from the laboratory test date to notification (when treatment usually starts in Brazil), fell from 11.4 to 8.1 days, although not among confirmed cases. A 2015 correction reported that the authors had discovered 147 duplicate entries in that trial's database during a secondary analysis and, after reanalysis, observed only minor changes in effect measures, which do not impact the authors' conclusions; for example, the rate ratio for laboratory-confirmed notifications went from 1.59 (95% CI 1.31 to 1.88) to 1.60 (1.31 to 1.89).

Why faster diagnosis has not shown up as fewer deaths

The 2021 review's authors note that outcomes such as mortality may depend on health system factors other than diagnosis, such as treatment completion rates, co-morbidities, and empirical treatment. In endemic settings, they write, treating without bacteriological confirmation on the basis of a chest X-ray or clinical judgement is common. The authors of the South African roll-out trial concluded that improving outcomes in drug-sensitive tuberculosis programmes might require not only better diagnostic tests but also better linkage to care.

How the coverage did

The Harvard Gazette's 182,000 figure was a model's projection, published before the trials on this page reported deaths. The official quoted by Health-e, speaking about drug-resistant TB patients who died while waiting for laboratory results, expected the test to save lives by starting treatment immediately. Since then, one South African trial has found that it shortened time to treatment, but a pooled analysis of four trials found no clear difference. The trials have given inconclusive evidence on overall mortality, and the review could not assess outcomes specific to drug-resistant TB.

What a reasonable person can conclude

For a programme deciding whether to replace smear microscopy, the trials support expecting more confirmed diagnoses and probably fewer confirmed patients lost before treatment, and a wider set of studies suggests treatment may start a few days sooner. They could not confidently rule in or rule out an effect of the test on deaths among everyone tested, though in people with HIV deaths were probably reduced. Whether it cures more people is unknown: the trials of Xpert MTB/RIF Ultra reported no data on cure, and for the original Xpert MTB/RIF, three trials with 4,802 participants left it uncertain whether it has a modest effect or none on successful treatment outcomes. Health centres given on-site molecular testing together with guided restructuring of clinic workflows and monthly quality feedback treated more patients for confirmed tuberculosis within 14 days than routine-care centres (342 vs. 220; adjusted rate ratio, 1.56; 95% CI, 1.21 to 2.01).
