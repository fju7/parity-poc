Could changing how cities are laid out, together with transport pricing, cut transport emissions?

The question

The question is whether changing how cities are laid out, for example making them denser, more mixed and better connected, together with measures such as transport pricing, could cut greenhouse gas emissions from transport.

We drew on six documents: two chapters and the Summary for Policymakers of the 2022 IPCC assessment of climate mitigation, a review of measured car-use reductions in European cities, and two studies of urban form and transport emissions.

The short answer

The IPCC's Summary for Policymakers states, with high confidence, that changes in urban form (density, land-use mix, connectivity and accessibility) in combination with programmes that encourage changes in consumer behaviour (such as transport pricing) could reduce transport-related greenhouse gas emissions in developed countries and slow growth in emissions in developing countries.

The measured studies on this page point the same way, but they count car traffic or measure associations, and they report that the effect varies from place to place.

Boeing, Pilgram and Lu add that, although these relationships vary across development levels and design paradigms, "most prior literature reports findings from urban areas that are outliers by global standards."

The Puget Sound study also finds that attitudinal factors are highly correlated with CO2e emissions: people who have positive attitudes toward the environment, walking, or public transit tend to produce fewer transportation emissions, while people who need flexibility for their daily activities tend to generate more, and the author concludes that travelers' attitudes are very influential determinants of CO2e emissions and thus should be considered in the land use–transportation emissions analysis.

What the assessment says

The Summary also states, with high confidence, that investments in public inter- and intra-city transport and active transport infrastructure, such as bicycle and pedestrian pathways, can further support the shift to less GHG-intensive transport modes.

It adds, with high confidence, that combinations of other systemic changes, including teleworking, digitalisation and smart and shared mobility, may reduce demand for passenger and freight services, and, with medium confidence, that some of these changes could lead to induced demand that may decrease their GHG emissions reduction potential.

On cities, it states, with very high confidence, that urban areas can create opportunities to increase resource efficiency and significantly reduce GHG emissions through the systemic transition of infrastructure and urban form through low-emission development pathways towards net-zero emissions.

It adds that ambitious mitigation efforts for established, rapidly growing and emerging cities will encompass reducing or changing energy and material consumption, electrification, and enhancing carbon uptake and storage in the urban environment, and that cities can achieve net-zero emissions, but only if emissions are reduced within and outside of their administrative boundaries through supply chains.

It states, with high confidence, that strategies for established cities to achieve large GHG emissions savings include targeted infilling and supporting non-motorised and public transport, and that rapidly growing cities can avoid future emissions by co-locating jobs and housing to achieve compact urban form.

It states, with very high confidence, that packages of multiple city-scale mitigation strategies can have cascading effects across sectors, and that the capacity of cities to implement them varies with regulatory and institutional settings and enabling conditions, including municipal budgetary powers.

The Summary's following statement (C.6.4), also with very high confidence, adds that the effectiveness of these strategies depends on cooperation and coordination with national and sub-national governments, industry, and civil society, that the full potential for reducing consumption-based urban emissions to net zero GHG can be met only when emissions beyond cities' administrative boundaries are also addressed, and that cities can play a positive role in reducing emissions across supply chains, for example through building codes and the choice of construction materials.

The Summary's next statement, on buildings, projects with high confidence that, in modelled global scenarios, existing buildings, if retrofitted, and buildings yet to be built approach net zero GHG emissions in 2050 if policy packages combining ambitious sufficiency, efficiency and renewable energy measures are effectively implemented and barriers to decarbonisation are removed, and warns that low ambition policies increase the risk of locking-in buildings' carbon for decades; it concerns buildings rather than transport.

In its statement on transport, the Summary states, with high confidence, that demand-side options and low-GHG emissions technologies can reduce transport sector emissions in developed countries and limit emissions growth in developing countries, and, with medium confidence, that demand-focused interventions can reduce demand for all transport services and support the shift to more energy efficient transport modes.

The same statement's other findings, outside this question's scope, are that electric vehicles powered by low-emissions electricity offer the largest decarbonisation potential for land-based transport, on a life cycle basis (high confidence); that sustainable biofuels can offer additional mitigation benefits in land-based transport in the short and medium term (medium confidence); and that sustainable biofuels, low-emissions hydrogen and derivatives can support mitigation of CO2 emissions from shipping, aviation and heavy-duty land transport but require production process improvements and cost reductions (medium confidence).

Its preceding statement, on buildings, states with high confidence that by 2050 up to 61% (8.2 GtCO2) of global building emissions could be mitigated, with sufficiency policies contributing 10% of this potential, energy efficiency policies 42% and renewable energy policies 9%, that the largest share of the mitigation potential of new buildings is in developing countries while in developed countries the highest mitigation potential lies in retrofitting existing buildings, and that the 2020–2030 decade is critical for building the capacity to capture it; it too concerns buildings rather than transport.

More broadly, the Summary describes demand-side mitigation as encompassing changes in infrastructure use, end-use technology adoption, and socio-cultural and behavioural change, and states with high confidence that demand-side measures and new ways of end-use service provision can reduce global GHG emissions in end-use sectors by 40–70% by 2050 compared to baseline scenarios.

It adds, with high confidence, that illustrative global low-demand scenarios indicate that more efficient end-use energy conversion can reduce the need for upstream energy by 45% by 2050 compared to 2020, that demand-side mitigation potential differs between and within regions, and that the lowest population quartile by income worldwide faces shortfalls in shelter, mobility, and nutrition.

It puts 5–30% of global annual GHG emissions from end-use sectors as avoidable by 2050, with high confidence, through changes in the built environment and service provision including compact cities, co-location of jobs and housing, and reallocation of street space for active mobility.

It also states, with high confidence, that choice architecture can help end-users adopt low-GHG-intensive options, among them electric light-duty vehicles and shifts to walking, cycling, shared pooled and public transit, and, with high confidence, that addressing inequality and many forms of status consumption and focusing on wellbeing supports climate change mitigation efforts.

The figure accompanying these statements carries the heading that demand-side mitigation can be achieved through changes in socio-cultural factors, infrastructure design and use, and end-use technology adoption by 2050; its panels cover nutrition; manufactured products, mobility and shelter; and the indicative impacts on electricity of change in service demand, including additional electrification.

For land transport, the figure lists teleworking or telecommuting and active mobility through walking and cycling as socio-cultural options, public transport, shared mobility, compact cities and spatial planning as infrastructure-use options, and electric vehicles and a shift to more efficient vehicles as end-use technology options.

On feasibility, it states, with high confidence, that the feasibility of mitigation options varies according to context and time, and that spatial planning has a higher potential at early stages of urban development.

It states, with high confidence, that combining mitigation with policies to shift development pathways, such as policies that induce lifestyle or behaviour changes, can overcome barriers, open up a broader range of mitigation options and facilitate the combination of mitigation and other development goals, and that measures promoting walkable urban areas combined with electrification and renewable energy can create health co-benefits from cleaner air and benefits from enhanced mobility.

It states, with medium confidence, that coordinated housing policies that broaden relocation options can make mitigation measures in transport more effective, and, with high confidence, that institutional and regulatory capacity, innovation, finance, improved governance and multi-objective policies enable enhanced mitigation, and that such interventions can be mutually reinforcing.

And it states, with high confidence, that a wide range of regulatory instruments at the sectoral level have proven effective in reducing emissions, that these instruments and relevant economic instruments are complementary, and that scaling up regulatory instruments could improve mitigation outcomes in sectoral applications including land use and zoning, adding, with medium confidence, that regulatory instruments designed to be implemented with flexibility mechanisms can reduce costs.

The IPCC's transport chapter states, with high confidence, that changes in urban form can support systemic changes that reduce demand for transport services or expand the use of more efficient transport modes.

It puts a size on this: cities can reduce their transport-related fuel consumption by around 25% through combinations of more compact land use and less car-dependent transport infrastructure.

It adds, with high confidence, that transport demand management incentives are expected to be necessary to support these changes.

The same chapter states, with high confidence, that meeting climate mitigation goals would require transformative changes in the transport sector, whose direct emissions were 8.7 GtCO2-eq in 2019, with 70% of direct transport emissions from road vehicles, and that transport-related emissions in developing regions have increased more rapidly than in Europe or North America, a trend that is likely to continue in coming decades; it adds that emissions from shipping and aviation continue to grow rapidly.

It reports, with medium confidence, that without intervention CO2 emissions from transport could grow in the range of 16% and 50% by 2050, and that scenarios that limit warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot suggest a 59% reduction (42–68% interquartile range) in transport-related CO2 emissions by 2050 compared to modelled 2020 levels is required.

It adds, with medium confidence, that a quarter of the 1.5°C scenarios describe transport-related CO2 emissions reductions in excess of 68%; it also notes that illustrative pathways describe reductions of 80% and 90% in the transport sector by 2050, and that transport-related emission reductions may not happen uniformly across regions: emissions decrease from 2020 levels by 2050 in Developed Countries and in Eastern Europe and West-Central Asia across all scenarios compatible with a 1.5°C goal, but could increase in Africa, Asia and Pacific, Latin America and Caribbean, and the Middle East in some of these scenarios.

The transport chapter also reports mixed evidence of the effect of circular economy, shared economy and digitalisation initiatives on demand for transport services: an increase in online shopping with priority delivery can increase demand for freight transport, and increased ridesharing could increase vehicle-km travelled.

The chapter puts the mitigation potential of urban planning at about 25% in 2050 compared with a business-as-usual scenario.

The urban systems chapter states, with very high confidence, that per capita emissions in developing countries, where most future urban population growth will occur, are expected to increase; it also states that how new cities and towns are designed, constructed, managed, and powered will lock-in behaviour, lifestyles, and future urban GHG emissions, and that there is risk that urbanisation can lead to increased global GHG emissions through increased emissions outside the city's boundaries.

It states, with very high confidence, that cities can only achieve net-zero GHG emissions through deep decarbonisation and systemic transformation, and, with high evidence and high agreement, that reducing or changing urban energy and material use towards more sustainable production and consumption across all sectors, including through compact and efficient urban forms and supporting infrastructure, is one of three strategies found effective in reducing emissions when implemented concurrently, and that cities can achieve net-zero emissions only if emissions are reduced within and outside of their administrative boundaries.

The urban systems chapter states, with robust evidence, high agreement and very high confidence, that integrated spatial planning for compact growth, with co-located residential and job densities, mixed land use and transit-oriented development, could reduce greenhouse gas emissions by between 23% and 26% by 2050 compared with business as usual.

In the chapter's body that range goes with a 20–25% cut in future urban energy use in 2050, from making the layout of emerging urbanisation more compact, walkable and co-located, so it covers urban emissions generally and not transport alone.

The chapter also states that compact cities with shortened distances between housing and jobs, and interventions that support a modal shift away from private motor vehicles towards walking, cycling, and low-emissions shared and public transportation, can have lower GHG emissions.

The same chapter states that road prices and congestion charges can provide the conditions for urban inhabitants to shift mobility demands and reduce vehicle use.

It also states, with high confidence, that packages of mitigation policies that implement multiple urban-scale interventions can reduce more emissions than the net sum of individual interventions.

Cities can implement such policy packages across sectors, especially through spatial planning, but the chapter notes that their institutional capacity to develop, coordinate and integrate sectoral mitigation strategies varies by context.

The potential depends on the city: the chapter states, with high confidence, that the potential and sequencing of mitigation strategies will vary with a city's land use, spatial form, development level and state of urbanisation.

For rapidly growing cities it points to co-locating jobs and housing to achieve compact urban form, and for established cities to targeted infilling and densifying, as well as modal shift.

It states that new and emerging cities have unparalleled potential to become low or net-zero GHG emissions by creating compact, co-located and walkable urban areas with mixed land use and transit-oriented design.

It adds, with high confidence, that the infrastructure development needs of new and emerging cities can be met through energy-efficient infrastructures and services and people-centred urban design.

It also states that urban infrastructures and urban form can enable socio-cultural and lifestyle changes that can significantly reduce carbon footprints.

Its Executive Summary also covers informal settlements: it states that the upgrading of informal settlements and inadequate housing to improve resilience and well-being offers a chance to create a low-carbon transition, and it reports limited quantifiable data on these practices and their cumulative impacts on GHG emissions; those practices concern housing, waste, energy, water and sanitation rather than transport.

It also states that the long lifespan of urban infrastructures locks in behaviour and committed emissions.

What measured studies find

Kuss and Nicholas screened nearly 800 peer-reviewed studies and case studies and analysed in depth 24 documents that met quality criteria and quantitatively estimated car-use reduction from interventions in European cities.

They identify the congestion charge, parking and traffic control, and limited traffic zones as the most effective at reducing overall car use.

Congestion charges reduced car traffic across the cordon of the charging zone by 33% in London, 31.1% in Milan, 22% in Stockholm and 12% in Gothenburg.

These figures are reductions in car traffic, not in emissions.

The review notes that all 12 intervention types entail multiple measures and apply at least two different policy instruments.

Boeing, Pilgram and Lu, studying every urban area in the world, report that straighter, more-connected and less-overbuilt street networks are associated with lower transport emissions, all else equal.

They also report that these relationships vary across development levels and design paradigms.

A study of households in the Puget Sound region reports that people living in denser neighbourhoods tend to generate fewer transport CO2-equivalent emissions, but that this effect becomes insignificant as population density reaches a certain level.

What this does not settle

The assessment's estimates of about 25% mitigation from urban planning and a 23% to 26% cut in GHG emissions from integrated spatial planning are potentials for 2050 against a business-as-usual scenario, not reductions measured after a city changed its layout; its separate figure that cities can cut transport-related fuel consumption by around 25% is given without a date or baseline.

Both studies of urban form and emissions on this page say they do not establish causation.

The congestion-charge figures measure traffic across a charging cordon in four European city centres, and they do not by themselves give the change in a whole city's transport emissions.

We did not obtain two meta-analyses of the built environment and driving, by Ewing and Cervero (2010) and by Stevens (2017), which are behind a subscription.

We did not search for evaluations of pricing schemes that report emissions rather than traffic.

What holds up

On these sources the answer is yes, with a qualification: the assessment judges with high confidence that changing urban form together with measures such as pricing could reduce transport emissions in developed countries and slow their growth in developing countries, while the measured studies support the direction but do not give a reliable size for a particular city.

Boeing, Pilgram and Lu also conclude that planners need a better empirical base for evidence-informed practice in under-studied regions, particularly the rapidly urbanizing Global South.

Boeing, Pilgram and Lu also report that longer road lengths per capita have a larger association with transport emissions in the low density deformed grid cluster (most cities in North America or Australia) and the modernist superblocks cluster (most cities in China and post-Soviet states), and 'no statistical relationship with emissions at all in the high density grid cluster.'

Boeing, Pilgram and Lu argue that "evidence-informed practice derived from global models or dissimilar world regions could yield conclusions inapplicable to local planning contexts."
