Further Warming: Water, Ice, Sea and Ocean

The question is how further warming, if greenhouse gas emissions continue, will change rainfall and the water cycle, very wet and very dry extremes, ice, sea level and the oceans. A common picture is that every one of these changes simply grows with the warming, everywhere. In our reading, the assessment we read projects most of them to grow with each increment of warming, but not uniformly: rainfall rises on average while parts of the subtropics dry, more drought is projected in some regions rather than all, sea level by 2100 depends strongly on emissions, and the ocean, ice and sea level keep changing for centuries.

Rainfall and the water cycle

More intense and more variable, wetter on average, but not wetter everywhere.

The IPCC’s 2021 physical science assessment finds strengthened evidence since its previous report that the global water cycle will continue to intensify as global temperatures rise (high confidence), with precipitation and surface water flows projected to become more variable over most land regions within seasons (high confidence) and from year to year (medium confidence).

Average annual global land precipitation is projected to increase by 0–5% under the very low emissions scenario, 1.5–8% under the intermediate scenario and 1–13% under the very high emissions scenario by 2081–2100 relative to 1995–2014 (likely ranges).

The increase is not everywhere: precipitation is projected to increase over high latitudes, the equatorial Pacific and parts of the monsoon regions, but decrease over parts of the subtropics and limited areas in the tropics under the intermediate, high and very high emissions scenarios (very likely).

The portion of the global land experiencing detectable increases or decreases in seasonal mean precipitation is projected to increase (medium confidence).

The assessment’s maps of projected change at 1.5°C, 2°C and 4°C of warming show that, across warming levels, changes in soil moisture largely follow changes in precipitation but also show some differences due to the influence of evapotranspiration, and caution that relatively small absolute changes may appear as large percentage changes in regions with dry baseline conditions.

Monsoon precipitation is projected to increase in the mid- to long term at the global scale, particularly over South and South East Asia, East Asia and West Africa apart from the far west Sahel (high confidence), and the monsoon season is projected to have a delayed onset over North and South America and West Africa (high confidence) and a delayed retreat over West Africa (medium confidence).

A southward shift and intensification of Southern Hemisphere summer storm tracks and their precipitation is likely in the long term under high emissions scenarios; the assessment has medium confidence in a continued poleward shift of storms and their precipitation in the North Pacific, and low confidence in projected changes in the North Atlantic storm tracks.

In snow-dominated regions globally it has high confidence in an earlier onset of spring snowmelt, with higher peak flows at the expense of summer flows.

Urbanization also increases mean and heavy precipitation over and/or downwind of cities (medium confidence) and resulting runoff intensity (high confidence).

Its water cycle chapter projects that increased evapotranspiration due to growing atmospheric water demand will decrease soil moisture over the Mediterranean, south-western North America, southern Africa, south-western South America, and south-western Australia (high confidence).

The same chapter has low confidence in the sign and magnitude of projected changes in global land runoff in all the scenarios.

These projections extend changes already observed: globally averaged precipitation over land has likely increased since 1950, with a faster rate of increase since the 1980s (medium confidence).

It is likely that human influence contributed to the pattern of observed precipitation changes since the mid-20th century and extremely likely that it contributed to the pattern of observed changes in near-surface ocean salinity.

Mid-latitude storm tracks have likely shifted poleward in both hemispheres since the 1980s, with marked seasonality in trends (medium confidence), and for the Southern Hemisphere human influence very likely contributed to the poleward shift of the closely related extratropical jet in austral summer.

Decreases in global land monsoon precipitation from the 1950s to the 1980s are partly attributed to human-caused Northern Hemisphere aerosol emissions, but increases since then have resulted from rising greenhouse gas concentrations and decadal to multi-decadal internal variability (medium confidence).

Over South Asia, East Asia and West Africa, increases in monsoon precipitation due to warming from greenhouse gases were counteracted over the 20th century by decreases due to cooling from human-caused aerosol emissions (high confidence).

Increases in West African monsoon precipitation since the 1980s are partly due to the growing influence of greenhouse gases and reductions in the cooling effect of human-caused aerosol emissions over Europe and North America (medium confidence).

Internal variability has largely been responsible for the amplification and attenuation of the observed human-caused decadal-to-multi-decadal mean precipitation changes in many land regions (high confidence).

In the near term, changes in monsoons will be dominated by the effects of internal variability (medium confidence), and projected changes in precipitation are also uncertain because of model uncertainty and uncertainty in forcings from natural and human-caused aerosols (medium confidence).

Very wet and very dry extremes

Heavier downpours in most regions; more drought in some regions, and more of them as warming rises.

With every additional increment of global warming, changes in extremes continue to become larger.

Every additional 0.5°C of global warming causes clearly discernible increases in the intensity and frequency of heavy precipitation (high confidence), as well as agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions (high confidence).

For meteorological droughts, discernible changes are seen in some regions for every additional 0.5°C, with more regions showing increases than decreases (medium confidence), and increases in hydrological droughts become larger with increasing global warming in some regions (medium confidence).

Projected percentage changes in frequency are larger for rarer events (high confidence).

There will be an increasing occurrence of some extreme events unprecedented in the observational record with additional global warming, even at 1.5°C of global warming.

It is very likely that heavy precipitation events will intensify and become more frequent in most regions with additional global warming, and at the global scale extreme daily precipitation events are projected to intensify by about 7% for each 1°C of global warming (high confidence).

The water cycle chapter finds that precipitation extremes will increase in almost all regions (high confidence), even where seasonal mean precipitation is projected to decrease (medium confidence).

The extremes chapter projects a likely doubling and tripling in the frequency of 10-year and 50-year heavy precipitation events, respectively, at 4°C of global warming compared with the recent past.

A warmer climate will intensify very wet and very dry weather and climate events and seasons, with implications for flooding or drought (high confidence), but the location and frequency of these events depend on projected changes in regional atmospheric circulation, including monsoons and mid-latitude storm tracks.

It is very likely that rainfall variability related to the El Niño–Southern Oscillation will be amplified by the second half of the 21st century under the intermediate, high and very high emissions scenarios.

Even at 1.5°C of global warming, heavy precipitation and associated flooding are projected to intensify and be more frequent in most regions in Africa and Asia (high confidence), North America (medium to high confidence) and Europe (medium confidence).

At 1.5°C, more frequent and/or severe agricultural and ecological droughts are also projected in a few regions in all inhabited continents except Asia compared to 1850–1900 (medium confidence), increases in meteorological droughts in a few regions (medium confidence), and increases or decreases in mean precipitation in a small number of regions (medium confidence).

At 2°C and above, region-specific changes include increases in river floods (medium to high confidence), reductions in mean precipitation and increases in aridity (medium to high confidence), and increases in fire weather (medium to high confidence).

At 4°C of global warming, about 50% of all inhabited regions in the extremes chapter’s set would be affected by increases in agricultural and ecological droughts, and only two regions, North Eastern Africa and South Asia, would experience decreases (medium confidence).

Many regions are projected to experience an increase in the probability of compound events with higher global warming (high confidence), and concurrent heatwaves and droughts are likely to become more frequent.

Concurrent extremes at multiple locations, including in crop-producing areas, become more frequent at 2°C and above compared to 1.5°C global warming (high confidence).

In the assessment’s regional synthesis of climatic impact-drivers, which it groups into seven types, all regions are projected to experience changes in at least 5, almost all (96%) in at least 10 and half in at least 15, with wide geographical variation in which ones, for a 20–30 year period centred around 2050 or consistent with 2°C of warming.

Climatic impact-drivers, in the assessment’s term, are physical climate system conditions such as means, events and extremes that affect an element of society or ecosystems; their types include heat and cold, wet and dry, wind, snow and ice, coastal and open ocean, and depending on system tolerance their changes can be detrimental, beneficial, neutral, or a mixture of each.

The proportion of intense tropical cyclones (Category 4–5) and peak wind speeds of the most intense tropical cyclones are projected to increase at the global scale with increasing global warming (high confidence).

It is likely that the global proportion of major (Category 3–5) tropical cyclone occurrence has increased over the last four decades, and very likely that the latitude where tropical cyclones in the western North Pacific reach their peak intensity has shifted northward, changes that cannot be explained by internal variability alone (medium confidence); there is low confidence in long-term trends in the frequency of all-category tropical cyclones.

Event attribution studies and physical understanding indicate that human-induced climate change increases heavy precipitation associated with tropical cyclones (high confidence), but data limitations inhibit clear detection of past trends on the global scale.

The frequency and intensity of heavy precipitation events have already increased since the 1950s over most land area for which observational data are sufficient for trend analysis (high confidence), and human-induced climate change is likely the main driver.

Human-induced climate change has already contributed to increases in agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions due to increased land evapotranspiration (medium confidence).

The assessment defines agricultural and ecological drought as a period with abnormal soil moisture deficit, which results from combined shortage of precipitation and excess evapotranspiration, assesses its projected changes primarily on total column soil moisture, and treats meteorological droughts (precipitation deficits) and hydrological droughts (streamflow deficits) as distinct from it.

Ice

Losses on every front, some committed for decades to centuries; Antarctica the least certain.

Additional warming is projected to further amplify permafrost thawing and loss of seasonal snow cover, of land ice and of Arctic sea ice (high confidence).

The Arctic is likely to be practically sea ice-free in September at least once before 2050 under the five illustrative scenarios considered in the report, with more frequent occurrences for higher warming levels.

Practically sea ice-free means a monthly average sea ice area of less than 1 million km², about 15% of the average September sea ice area observed in 1979–1988.

There is low confidence in the projected decrease of Antarctic sea ice.

Further decreases are projected in permafrost; snow, glaciers and ice sheets; and lake and Arctic sea ice (medium to high confidence), and these changes would be larger at 2°C global warming or above than at 1.5°C (high confidence).

Mountain and polar glaciers are committed to continue melting for decades or centuries (very high confidence).

The ocean and ice chapter projects that glaciers will lose 18% and 36% of their early-21st-century mass over 2015–2100 under a low and a high emissions pathway respectively (medium confidence).

Continued ice loss over the 21st century is virtually certain for the Greenland Ice Sheet and likely for the Antarctic Ice Sheet, and there is high confidence that total ice loss from the Greenland Ice Sheet will increase with cumulative emissions.

There is limited evidence for low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes, resulting from ice-sheet instability processes characterized by deep uncertainty, that would strongly increase ice loss from the Antarctic Ice Sheet for centuries under high emissions scenarios.

Loss of permafrost carbon following permafrost thaw is irreversible at centennial time scales (high confidence).

Northern Hemisphere spring snow cover extent will decrease by about 8% per 1°C of global surface air temperature change, up to 4°C above pre-industrial temperature (medium confidence).

The losses are already under way: in 2011–2020, annual average Arctic sea ice area reached its lowest level since at least 1850 (high confidence).

Human influence is very likely the main driver of the global retreat of glaciers since the 1990s and the decrease in Arctic sea ice area between 1979–1988 and 2010–2019.

There has been no significant trend in Antarctic sea ice area from 1979 to 2020 due to regionally opposing trends and large internal variability.

Human influence very likely contributed to the decrease in Northern Hemisphere spring snow cover since 1950, and it is very likely that it has contributed to the observed surface melting of the Greenland Ice Sheet over the past two decades, but there is only limited evidence, with medium agreement, of human influence on the Antarctic Ice Sheet mass loss.

Late summer Arctic sea ice area was smaller than at any time in at least the past 1000 years (medium confidence), and the global nature of glacier retreat since the 1950s, with almost all of the world’s glaciers retreating synchronously, is unprecedented in at least the last 2000 years (medium confidence).

Sea level

A rise that depends strongly on emissions by 2100, and continues for centuries regardless.

It is virtually certain that global mean sea level will continue to rise over the 21st century.

Relative to 1995–2014, the likely global mean sea level rise by 2100 is 0.28–0.55 m under the very low emissions scenario, 0.32–0.62 m under the low scenario, 0.44–0.76 m under the intermediate scenario and 0.63–1.01 m under the very high scenario (medium confidence).

To compare these ranges with the 1986–2005 baseline of the IPCC’s earlier reports, the assessment says to add 0.03 m, and to add 0.16 m to compare with a 1900 baseline.

Global mean sea level rise above the likely range, approaching 2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150 under a very high emissions scenario (low confidence), cannot be ruled out due to deep uncertainty in ice-sheet processes.

The ocean and ice chapter describes a low-likelihood, high-impact storyline in which, under high emissions, such ice-sheet processes could in combination contribute more than one additional metre of sea level rise by 2100.

The assessment defines low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes as those whose probability of occurrence is low or not well known but whose potential impacts on society and ecosystems could be high, and a tipping point as a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganizes, often abruptly and/or irreversibly.

In the longer term, sea level is committed to rise for centuries to millennia due to continuing deep-ocean warming and ice-sheet melt and will remain elevated for thousands of years (high confidence).

Over the next 2000 years, global mean sea level will rise by about 2 to 3 m if warming is limited to 1.5°C, 2 to 6 m if limited to 2°C and 19 to 22 m with 5°C of warming (low confidence).

Projections of multi-millennial global mean sea level rise are consistent with reconstructed levels during past warm periods: likely 5–10 m higher than today around 125,000 years ago, when global temperatures were very likely 0.5°C–1.5°C higher than 1850–1900, and very likely 5–25 m higher roughly 3 million years ago, when they were 2.5°C–4°C higher (medium confidence).

Approximately two-thirds of the global coastline has a projected regional relative sea level rise within ±20% of the global mean increase (medium confidence).

It is very likely to virtually certain that regional mean relative sea level rise will continue throughout the 21st century, except in a few regions with substantial geologic land uplift rates.

Due to relative sea level rise, extreme sea level events that occurred once per century in the recent past are projected to occur at least annually at more than half of all tide gauge locations by 2100 (high confidence).

Relative sea level rise contributes to increases in the frequency and severity of coastal flooding in low-lying areas and to coastal erosion along most sandy coasts (high confidence).

In coastal cities, the combination of more frequent extreme sea level events and extreme rainfall or river flow events will make flooding more probable (high confidence).

Global mean sea level increased by 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, and the average rate of rise went from 1.3 mm a year between 1901 and 1971 to 3.7 mm a year between 2006 and 2018 (high confidence).

Global mean sea level has risen faster since 1900 than over any preceding century in at least the last 3000 years (high confidence).

Human influence was very likely the main driver of these increases since at least 1971.

Thermal expansion explained 50% of sea level rise during 1971–2018, while ice loss from glaciers contributed 22%, ice sheets 20% and changes in land-water storage 8%, a rise the assessment attributes to heating of the climate system through ice loss on land and thermal expansion from ocean warming.

The rate of ice-sheet loss increased by a factor of four between 1992–1999 and 2010–2019, and together ice-sheet and glacier mass loss were the dominant contributors to global mean sea level rise during 2006–2018 (high confidence).

Even if global net negative CO2 emissions were achieved and sustained, the CO2-induced surface temperature increase would be gradually reversed but other climate changes would continue in their current direction for decades to millennia (high confidence), and it would take several centuries to millennia for global mean sea level to reverse course (high confidence).

The oceans

Warmer, more acidic, lower in oxygen, with more marine heatwaves and an Atlantic overturning very likely to weaken.

Past greenhouse gas emissions since 1750 have committed the global ocean to future warming (high confidence).

Over the rest of the 21st century, likely ocean warming ranges from 2–4 times the 1971–2018 change under the low emissions scenario to 4–8 times under the very high scenario.

Upper ocean stratification (virtually certain), ocean acidification (virtually certain) and ocean deoxygenation (high confidence) will continue to increase in the 21st century, at rates dependent on future emissions.

Changes are irreversible on centennial to millennial time scales in global ocean temperature (very high confidence), deep-ocean acidification (very high confidence) and deoxygenation (medium confidence).

With additional global warming, the frequency of marine heatwaves will continue to increase (high confidence), particularly in the tropical ocean and the Arctic (medium confidence).

The ocean and ice chapter projects marine heatwaves at global scale becoming four times more frequent in 2081–2100 compared to 1995–2014 under the low emissions scenario, and eight times more frequent under the very high scenario (likely ranges).

The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios; there is high confidence in the 21st century decline, but only low confidence in the magnitude of the trend.

There is medium confidence that there will not be an abrupt collapse before 2100; if such a collapse were to occur, it would very likely cause abrupt shifts in regional weather patterns and water cycle, such as a southward shift in the tropical rain belt, weakening of the African and Asian monsoons and drying in Europe.

Under higher emissions the land and ocean carbon sinks take up more CO2 in absolute terms but become less effective, leaving a higher proportion of emitted CO2 in the atmosphere (high confidence).

Under the intermediate scenario the rates of CO2 taken up by the land and ocean are projected to decrease in the second half of the 21st century (high confidence); under the very low and low scenarios the land and ocean begin to take up less carbon as atmospheric CO2 declines (high confidence) and turn into a weak net source by 2100 under the very low scenario (medium confidence); and it is very unlikely that the combined sink turns into a source by 2100 in scenarios without net negative emissions.

It is virtually certain that the global upper ocean (0–700 m) has warmed since the 1970s and extremely likely that human influence is the main driver, and virtually certain that human-caused CO2 emissions are the main driver of current global acidification of the surface open ocean.

There is high confidence that oxygen levels have dropped in many upper ocean regions since the mid-20th century and medium confidence that human influence contributed to this drop.

Ocean warming accounted for 91% of the heating in the climate system, with land warming, ice loss and atmospheric warming accounting for about 5%, 3% and 1%, as the observed average rate of heating rose from 0.50 W m–2 for 1971–2006 to 0.79 W m–2 for 2006–2018, an accumulation of energy that human-caused radiative forcing causes (high confidence).

The global ocean has warmed faster over the past century than since the end of the last deglacial transition, around 11,000 years ago (medium confidence).

A long-term increase in surface open ocean pH occurred over the past 50 million years (high confidence), but surface open ocean pH as low as recent decades is unusual in the last 2 million years (medium confidence).

Marine heatwaves have approximately doubled in frequency since the 1980s (high confidence), and human influence has very likely contributed to most of them since at least 2006.

How the changes scale, and what cannot be ruled out

Mostly larger with each increment of warming, not always in proportion, and with tails that widen.

Many changes in the climate system become larger in direct relation to increasing global warming.

The warming these changes are indexed to is, for 2081–2100 compared with 1850–1900, very likely 1.0°C to 1.8°C under the very low emissions scenario, 2.1°C to 3.5°C under the intermediate scenario and 3.3°C to 5.7°C under the very high scenario; the last time global surface temperature was sustained at or above 2.5°C higher than 1850–1900 was over 3 million years ago (medium confidence).

They include increases in the frequency and intensity of marine heatwaves, heavy precipitation and, in some regions, agricultural and ecological droughts, an increase in the proportion of intense tropical cyclones, and reductions in Arctic sea ice, snow cover and permafrost.

The water cycle chapter adds that in some cases regional water cycle changes are not linearly related to global warming, which it explains by the interaction of multiple drivers, feedbacks and time scales (high confidence).

Projected human-caused changes in mean climate and extremes will be either amplified or attenuated by internal variability (high confidence).

The main internal variability phenomena behind this include the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, Pacific Decadal Variability and Atlantic Multi-decadal Variability, through their regional influence.

It is likely that at least one large explosive volcanic eruption would occur during the 21st century, which would reduce global surface temperature and precipitation, especially over land, for one to three years, alter the global monsoon circulation, modify extreme precipitation and change many climatic impact-drivers (medium confidence), temporarily and partially masking human-caused climate change.

If global warming exceeds the assessed very likely range for a given emissions scenario, changes in many aspects of the climate system, such as regional precipitation, would also exceed their assessed very likely ranges (high confidence).

Such low-likelihood, high-warming outcomes are associated with potentially very large impacts, such as through more intense and more frequent heatwaves and heavy precipitation.

The probability of low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes increases with higher global warming levels (high confidence), and abrupt responses and tipping points of the climate system, such as strongly increased Antarctic ice-sheet melt, cannot be ruled out (high confidence).

Low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes could occur at global and regional scales even for global warming within the very likely range for a given emissions scenario.

If global warming increases, some compound extreme events with low likelihood in past and current climate will become more frequent, and there will be a higher likelihood of events unprecedented in the observational record (high confidence).

Lower emissions change the picture: by the end of the century, scenarios with very low and low emissions would strongly limit the increases in the frequency of extreme sea level events, heavy precipitation and pluvial flooding, relative to higher emissions scenarios (high confidence).

Scenarios with very low and low emissions would lead to substantially smaller changes in a range of climatic impact-drivers beyond 2040 than high and very high emissions scenarios, and changes would also be smaller in very low compared with low emissions scenarios, and in intermediate compared with high or very high ones (high confidence).

Very low or low emissions would have rapid and sustained effects to limit human-caused climate change, but early responses can be masked by natural variability: differences in 20-year trends of global surface temperature would likely emerge in the near term, while the response of many other climate variables would emerge from natural variability at different times later in the 21st century (high confidence).

What this adds up to

Right in direction for most of the question, wrong in its uniformity and its timing.

On what we read, the common picture is right that continued emissions bring a more intense water cycle, heavier downpours, shrinking ice, rising seas and a warmer, more acidic ocean, and that most of these grow with each increment of warming.

It is wrong in its uniformity: wetter on average does not mean wetter everywhere, since parts of the subtropics are projected to get less rain and several named regions to lose soil moisture, and worse droughts are projected in some regions rather than in all.

A rise of about a metre by 2100 is the top of the likely range under very high emissions, not a figure for every path; the likely ranges under the other four scenarios run from 0.28 m to 0.76 m, and higher outcomes cannot be ruled out.

And the ocean, the ice sheets and sea level do not simply follow the warming: the assessment finds them committed to keep changing for centuries to millennia.

What we did not read

We hold four documents, all from the IPCC’s 2021 Working Group I report: its Summary for Policymakers and its chapters on water cycle changes (Chapter 8), on the ocean, cryosphere and sea level (Chapter 9), and on weather and climate extremes (Chapter 11).

Of the Summary for Policymakers, we drew on its numbered statements; of the three chapters, we drew on their executive summaries and did not read the rest, and we read no other part of the report.

The assessment dates from 2021; we did not search the literature published since then, which may have revised some of these judgements.
