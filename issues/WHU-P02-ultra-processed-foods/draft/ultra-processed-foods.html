Do ultra-processed foods harm your health?

Study draft, 20 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

Greater exposure to ultra-processed food is associated with a higher risk of some adverse health outcomes, especially cardiometabolic, common mental disorder and mortality outcomes; separately, SACN notes that subgroup analyses indicate increased risk for some food categories within ultra-processed food, such as processed meat and sugar and non-sugar sweetened beverages, but that the reasons for heterogeneity in findings for other food-category subgroups remain unclear. People who eat more ultra-processed food have higher rates of illness, especially cardiometabolic disease, common mental disorders and early death. What the studies do not establish is that the processing itself causes the harm, or that every food under the label carries the same risk.

What counts as ultra-processed

The term comes from the Nova classification, which groups foods by the extent and purpose of their industrial processing. Its authors define ultra-processed foods as formulations of ingredients, mostly of exclusive industrial use, made through a series of industrial processes. The category is wide: a 2025 UK trial gives breakfast cereals, sweets and mass-produced bread as examples.

The claim, as its proponents make it

In 2024 an umbrella review in The BMJ by Lane and colleagues gathered 45 pooled analyses and found direct associations between ultra-processed food and 32 of the health measures they covered. In 2025 the opening paper of a Lancet series, led by Carlos Monteiro, argued that the evidence covers adverse outcomes across nearly all organ systems. The series concluded that displacement of traditional diets by ultra-processed foods is a key driver of the rising global burden of diet-related chronic disease.

What the cohort evidence shows

On its own criteria the BMJ review rated four associations as convincing: cardiovascular death (risk ratio 1.50, 95% confidence interval 1.37 to 1.63), type 2 diabetes (dose-response risk ratio 1.12, 1.11 to 1.13), anxiety (odds ratio 1.48, 1.37 to 1.59) and common mental disorders (odds ratio 1.53, 1.43 to 1.63), and under GRADE it rated the first of these very low quality and the last two low. Under the separate GRADE quality ratings, the same review graded 22 of its pooled analyses low quality, 19 very low and four moderate. The review found no pooled analyses of randomised trials, so everything it pooled came from observational studies. Its authors name residual confounding as perhaps the most pertinent limitation of such studies, though they argue the consistency of the findings supports the notion that it does not fully explain them.

What the trials show

The two randomised trials on this page test the effect of processing more directly, and both measure eating or weight over weeks, not disease. In a 2019 inpatient trial at the US National Institutes of Health, Kevin Hall's team studied 20 weight-stable adults who ate ultra-processed and unprocessed diets for two weeks each, matched for presented calories, energy density, macronutrients, sugar, sodium and fibre. On the ultra-processed diet they ate 508 kcal a day more, gaining 0.9 kg, and they lost 0.9 kg on the unprocessed diet. A correction published in 2020 changed the trial's reported diet differences at breakfast, lunch and dinner but, its authors wrote, not the primary outcome of total daily intake or the snack intake data. A free-living trial in people with overweight or obesity compared minimally processed and ultra-processed diets, both following UK healthy-eating guidelines, with outcomes assessed at 8 weeks. Weight change at eight weeks was −2.06% (95% CI −2.99 to −1.13) on the minimally processed diet and −1.05% (95% CI −1.98 to −0.13) on the ultra-processed one. The extra weight loss on the minimally processed diet did not translate into significantly better cardiometabolic risk factors, except triglycerides. LDL cholesterol was lower on the ultra-processed diet. The BMJ review's authors write that long-term trials of ultra-processed diets on outcomes such as heart disease or cancer will not be possible, for ethical reasons.

Not every ultra-processed food behaves the same

In three large US cohorts, the highest fifth of ultra-processed food intake had a hazard ratio of 1.46 (95% CI 1.39 to 1.54) for type 2 diabetes against the lowest, but cereals, dark and whole-grain breads, packaged snacks, fruit-based products, and yogurt and dairy-based desserts were associated with lower risk. For cardiovascular disease, the same cohorts linked sweetened drinks and processed meats with higher risk, and bread and cold cereals, yoghurt and dairy desserts, and savoury snacks with lower risk. In the European EPIC cohort, animal-based products and sweetened drinks were associated with multimorbidity of cancer and cardiometabolic disease, while ultra-processed breads and cereals were not. A 2024 BMJ study of US nurses and health professionals found 4% higher all-cause mortality in the highest quarter of intake, and no association with cancer or cardiovascular death. In that study, meat, poultry and seafood based ready-to-eat products, such as processed meat, showed the strongest associations with death. The UK's Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN) noted in 2025 that subgroup analyses point to higher risk from processed meat and sweetened drinks, foods it has already made recommendations on, and that the reasons for differences between other subgroups remain unclear.

The processing, or what the food contains?

Whether the processing itself does the harm is where the sources part ways: one finds most associations remain significant and unchanged after adjustment for diet quality or pattern, while SACN says it remains unclear to what extent established nutritional factors explain them. SACN found it unclear how far the associations are explained by established links between nutrients and health. It also named confounding by other known risks as a continuing limitation of the evidence. A 2021 review by Dicken and Batterham read the cohorts the other way, finding that most associations stayed significant and unchanged in size after adjustment for diet quality or pattern. In the 2024 BMJ mortality study, by contrast, ultra-processed food showed no consistent association with death within each quarter of diet quality, while better diet quality was linked to lower mortality within each quarter of ultra-processed intake. A 2024 analysis by Robinson and Jones calculated that unaccounted-for confounders with small-to-moderate associations could account for the link between ultra-processed food and weight gain. The UPDATE authors name energy density as one potential mechanism: their ultra-processed diet was more energy dense than the minimally processed one.

How the coverage did

FOX6 Milwaukee reported that the BMJ review suggests ultra-processed foods can lead to a higher risk of 32 damaging health outcomes. Even hedged with "suggests", "can lead to" is causal language for a review that pooled only observational studies. When the Lancet series launched, LBC's headline said ultra-processed food had been linked to harm in every human organ.

What a reasonable person can conclude

Greater exposure to ultra-processed food is associated with a higher risk of adverse health outcomes, especially cardiometabolic, common mental disorder, and mortality outcomes, and one study investigated whether ultra-processed foods affect energy intake in 20 weight-stable adults. For processed meat and sweetened drinks, the cohort evidence and existing UK advice point the same way. For wholegrain breads, cereals and yogurt, the cohorts here do not show the same harm, and some show lower risk. Whether processing itself harms health, rather than what these foods contain and what they displace, is not settled by the studies on this page.
