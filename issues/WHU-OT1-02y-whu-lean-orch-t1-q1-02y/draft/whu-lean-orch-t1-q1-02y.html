Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe this evidence as providing emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The clearest effects were for skills-for-learning interventions, with significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, although these large effects rest on a low number of studies [1]. Because the included interventions and outcome measures were all different, the review says direct comparison is difficult, so the passages do not settle which kind works best overall [1].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers without disabilities in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. The gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is largest in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Before this review, its authors noted that evidence on which interventions are actually effective had not been established [1].

Skills-for-learning interventions

Of the 18 studies analysed for effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but each was based on a low number of studies [1]. Examples of included studies are a language and literacy intervention in South Africa, a randomised controlled trial with 65 participants, and a handwriting programme for children with Developmental Coordination Disorder in India, a controlled before-and-after study with 34 participants [1].

Learning environments and teachers

'Accessible learning environments' was the second most common category of intervention, including programmes to improve social skills or to reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1]. Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. Among the included studies was the Good School Toolkit in Uganda, a randomised controlled trial with 3,820 participants of a school-wide intervention to reduce violence from peers and staff toward students with and without disabilities [1]. Also included was a Kenyan inclusive education training programme for teachers, evaluated with an uncontrolled before-and-after design with 130 participants [1].

Gaps in the evidence

Most interventions tried to improve children's skills but did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. No studies represented the categories of 'Accessibility of built environment and learning materials' or 'Inclusive education policies' [1]. Only one study each fell under 'Structural interventions' and 'Anti-bullying policies and programmes' [1]. None of the studies were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. The review set out to ask which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability, but the excerpts provided here do not report its answer to that question [1].

Programme examples from a UNESCO guide

A UNESCO guide offers examples of good practice drawn on international evidence and programme experience, rather than a systematic review of effects [6]. In Ethiopia, Handicap International piloted six inclusive 'cluster schools' that supported 30 satellite schools [6]. The guide reports that this pilot led to improved inclusive education practice, better physical accessibility, increased enrolment for children with disabilities and a better learning environment for all students [6]. In Cambodia, itinerant teachers supported children with disabilities, their teachers and parents [6]. The guide recounts one boy with Down's syndrome who was able to return to school after awareness-raising sessions increased solidarity among his peers [6]. The guide states there is strong evidence of the potential of approaches that encourage cooperation between students, including students with disabilities, while stressing that this needs skill to orchestrate [6]. It also warns that inclusion efforts relying on individualised practices imported from special education tend to foster new and more subtle forms of segregation within mainstream settings [6].

What holds up

What holds up best is that individual skills-focused interventions, especially for literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, show significant and large effects, though from few studies. Interventions on conditions for inclusion and teacher knowledge also show significant effects, but on thinner evidence. Claims about which kind works best are weak, because the interventions and outcomes were too varied to compare directly and whole categories such as built-environment accessibility and inclusive education policy were not studied at all. The UNESCO examples from Ethiopia and Cambodia are reported successes of specific programmes, not tested comparisons. The review's authors call for more systemic approaches and for interventions that address attendance, enrolment, completion and transition, not only individual skills [1].
