Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review describes its evidence as providing 'emerging support' for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The clearest reported effects are for interventions that build learners' skills, with significant and large effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but these effects are based on a low number of studies [1]. Because the interventions and outcome measures in the included studies were all different, direct comparison is difficult, so the sources do not establish a firm ranking of which kinds work best [1].

What the evidence shows

Effects reported by the systematic review

Of the 18 studies included in the review's analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. The second most common category of intervention was 'Accessible learning environments', which includes, for instance, programmes aimed at improving social skills or reducing victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, "A twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom)" [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source states that, consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment is needed [1]. According to the report, it "illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs" [3]. The source, titled "Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low‐ and middle‐income Asia‐Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map," is described only by its title, and the text provided contains no finding and no certainty or confidence wording to quote [3].

Teacher training

An evidence and gap map found 50 studies on teacher training for the inclusion of students with disabilities in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries [3]. Only 16 of 41 countries reported evidence on in-service teacher professional development for disability inclusion [3]. A significant number of these interventions focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability, as many of these countries are in the early stages of the inclusive education agenda [3]. Some qualitative studies in the map record teachers reporting benefits, such as training seminars and workshops in Vietnam improving the general quality of teaching in their schools [3]. Only three of the mapped studies discuss interventions for supporting mental health among students with a disability, and none of the identified interventions support students with disabilities during emergency or crisis situations [3].

Where the evidence is concentrated

Most interventions tried to improve children's skills but did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. A broader evidence and gap map found that the focus of studies is on 'fixing individuals', for instance improving social or learning skills, with fewer studies on improving infrastructure or institutions [2]. Over half of the impact evaluations in that map come from upper-middle-income countries, with notable gaps for low-income countries (n = 9) [2]. The map also reports a significant gap with respect to study quality, especially for impact evaluations [2]. None of the studies in the systematic review were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high, which limits confidence in the pooled evidence [1]. All but one of the studies reviewed were rated low confidence in their findings overall, so the evidence on whether these programmes work is weak [1]. The low confidence in the evidence across studies was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs, and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. The source says a twin-track approach is needed, meeting children with disabilities' specific needs while also including them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1]. No studies were found that reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour for children with disabilities [3].

What holds up

It holds up that a range of individual-level interventions, especially those building skills such as literacy and numeracy, have shown significant effects, though the authors call the support 'emerging' and the effects rest on few studies [1]. Whether systemic or school-level approaches work cannot be judged well, because there is little research on them; the review's authors nonetheless argue that more systemic approaches are needed [1]. The review set out to ask which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability, but the passages used here do not report an answer to that question [1]. The authors call for impact evaluations with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. The evidence and gap map calls for more studies from low-income countries and on all types of impairment, including visual and hearing impairments [2].
