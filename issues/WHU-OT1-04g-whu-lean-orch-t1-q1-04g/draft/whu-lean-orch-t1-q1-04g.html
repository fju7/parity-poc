Carbon dioxide removal and net zero: what the assessments say

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

Carbon dioxide removal (CDR) is assessed as a necessary element to achieve net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas (GHG) emissions both globally and nationally, because it counterbalances residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors (robust evidence, high agreement) [1]. It does not replace cutting emissions: reaching net zero CO2 or net zero GHG emissions requires deep and rapid reductions in gross emissions, while deploying CDR to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable (high confidence) [2]. The role of CDR changes over time: it further reduces net emissions in the near term, counterbalances residual emissions to help reach net zero in the mid term, and achieves and sustains net-negative emissions in the long term [1].

What the sources report

What counts as carbon dioxide removal

CDR refers to human activities that remove CO2 from the atmosphere and durably store it in geological, terrestrial or ocean reservoirs, or in products [1, 3]. It includes enhancing biological or geochemical sinks and direct air capture and storage, but it excludes natural CO2 uptake that human activities do not directly cause [3]. Carbon capture and storage or utilisation counts as CDR only if the CO2 has been captured from the atmosphere, either indirectly as biomass or directly from ambient air, and stored durably [1]. According to the IPCC, limiting human-caused global warming requires net zero CO2 emissions; cumulative carbon emissions until net zero CO2 is reached and the level of greenhouse gas emission reductions this decade largely determine whether warming can be limited to 1.5°C or 2°C (high confidence), and projected CO2 emissions from existing fossil fuel infrastructure without additional abatement would exceed the remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C (50%) (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC, limiting human-caused global warming to a specific level requires limiting cumulative CO2 emissions, reaching at least net zero CO2 emissions, and strongly reducing other greenhouse gas emissions; reaching net zero GHG emissions primarily requires deep reductions in CO2, methane, and other GHGs and implies net negative CO2 emissions, carbon dioxide removal will be necessary to achieve net negative CO2 emissions, and net zero GHG emissions, if sustained, are projected to result in a gradual decline in global surface temperatures after an earlier peak (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, B.5.3), if annual CO2 emissions between 2020–2030 stayed on average at 2019 levels, the resulting cumulative emissions would almost exhaust the remaining carbon budget for 1.5°C (50%) and deplete more than a third of the remaining carbon budget for 2°C (67%); estimates of future CO2 emissions from existing fossil fuel infrastructure without additional abatement already exceed the remaining carbon budget for limiting warming to 1.5°C (50%) (high confidence), and projected cumulative future CO2 emissions over the lifetime of existing and planned fossil fuel infrastructure, if historical operating patterns are maintained and without additional abatement, are approximately equal to the remaining carbon budget for limiting warming to 2°C with a likelihood of 83% (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, B.6), all global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and those that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), involve rapid and deep and, in most cases, immediate greenhouse gas emissions reductions in all sectors this decade, with global net zero CO2 emissions reached in the early 2050s and around the early 2070s, respectively (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report (B.6.1), global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, or to 2°C (>67%), are characterized by deep, rapid and, in most cases, immediate GHG emissions reductions, with 1.5°C pathways reaching net zero CO2 in the early 2050s and 2°C pathways in the early 2070s, and global GHG emissions projected to peak between 2020 and at the latest before 2025 in pathways that assume immediate action (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report (section B.6.3), global modelled mitigation pathways reaching net zero CO2 and GHG emissions include transitioning from fossil fuels without carbon capture and storage (CCS) to very low- or zero-carbon energy sources (such as renewables or fossil fuels with CCS), demand-side measures and improving efficiency, reducing non-CO2 GHG emissions, and CDR, and in most global modelled pathways land-use change and forestry (via reforestation and reduced deforestation) and the energy supply sector reach net zero CO2 emissions earlier than the buildings, industry and transport sectors, a finding the source rates as high confidence [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, B.6.4), mitigation options often have synergies with other aspects of sustainable development but some can also have trade-offs, for example energy efficiency and renewable energy offer potential synergies and, depending on context, biological CDR methods such as reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon sequestration, peatland restoration and coastal blue carbon management can enhance biodiversity, ecosystem functions, employment and local livelihoods, whereas afforestation or biomass crop production can have adverse socio-economic and environmental impacts, especially at large scales and where land tenure is insecure, and modelled pathways that use resources more efficiently or shift development toward sustainability face fewer challenges, such as less dependence on CDR and less pressure on land and biodiversity (high confidence) [2]. According to the source, if warming exceeds a specified level such as 1.5°C it could gradually be reduced again by achieving and sustaining net negative global CO2 emissions, but this would require additional deployment of carbon dioxide removal compared to pathways without overshoot, leading to greater feasibility and sustainability concerns, and overshoot entails adverse impacts, some irreversible, and additional risks for human and natural systems, all growing with the magnitude and duration of overshoot (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, B.7.1), only a small number of the most ambitious global modelled pathways limit global warming to 1.5°C (>50%) by 2100 without temporarily exceeding that level; achieving and sustaining net negative global CO2 emissions, with annual rates of CDR greater than residual CO2 emissions, would gradually reduce the warming level again (high confidence), while adverse impacts during overshoot that cause additional warming via feedback mechanisms, such as increased wildfires, mass mortality of trees, drying of peatlands, and permafrost thawing, weakening natural land carbon sinks and increasing releases of GHGs, would make the return more challenging (medium confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (statement B.7.2), the higher the magnitude and the longer the duration of overshoot, the more ecosystems and societies are exposed to greater and more widespread changes in climatic impact-drivers, increasing risks for many natural and human systems; compared to pathways without overshoot, societies would face higher risks to infrastructure, low-lying coastal settlements, and associated livelihoods, and overshooting 1.5°C will result in irreversible adverse impacts on certain ecosystems with low resilience, such as polar, mountain, and coastal ecosystems, impacted by ice-sheet melt, glacier melt, or by accelerating and higher committed sea level rise (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (SR1.5 Summary for Policymakers, B.7.3), the larger the overshoot, the more net negative CO2 emissions would be needed to return to 1.5°C by 2100, and transitioning towards net zero CO2 emissions faster and reducing non-CO2 emissions such as methane more rapidly would limit peak warming levels and reduce the requirement for net negative CO2 emissions, thereby reducing feasibility and sustainability concerns, and social and environmental risks associated with CDR deployment at large scales (high confidence) [2]. The IPCC's Sixth Assessment Report Synthesis Report states with high confidence that many agriculture, forestry, and other land use (AFOLU) options provide adaptation and mitigation benefits that could be upscaled in the near term across most regions, that conservation, improved management, and restoration of forests and other ecosystems offer the largest share of economic mitigation potential (with reduced tropical deforestation having the highest total mitigation potential), that ecosystem restoration, reforestation, and afforestation can create trade-offs from competing land demands that require integrated approaches to minimize, and that some options, such as conserving high-carbon ecosystems, deliver immediate benefits while others, such as restoring them, take decades to deliver measurable results [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers, C.3.6), maintaining the resilience of biodiversity and ecosystem services at a global scale depends on effective and equitable conservation of approximately 30% to 50% of Earth's land, freshwater and ocean areas, including currently near-natural ecosystems (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, the economic effects of strengthened mitigation action vary across regions and countries, depending notably on economic structure, regional emissions reductions, policy design and level of international cooperation (high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report, in pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, net global GHG emissions are projected to fall by 43 [34 to 60]% below 2019 levels by 2030, 60 [49 to 77]% by 2035, 69 [58 to 90]% by 2040 and 84 [73 to 98]% by 2050, with the source assigning high confidence to these projections [2]. According to the IPCC AR6 Synthesis Report (A.3.6), current global financial flows for adaptation, including from public and private sources, are insufficient and constrain the implementation of adaptation options, especially in developing countries (high confidence), and there are widening disparities between estimated adaptation costs and the finance allocated to adaptation (high confidence), with adaptation finance coming predominantly from public sources and only a small proportion of global tracked climate finance targeted to adaptation versus an overwhelming majority to mitigation (very high confidence) [2]. According to the IPCC (AR6 Synthesis Report, SPM A.4.3), a substantial 'emissions gap' exists between 2030 global GHG emissions implied by NDCs announced prior to COP26 and those in modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot or to 2°C (>67%) assuming immediate action (high confidence), which would make it likely that warming will exceed 1.5°C during the 21st century (high confidence); those modelled pathways imply deep global GHG emissions reductions this decade (high confidence), while pathways consistent with pre-COP26 NDCs to 2030 and no increase in ambition thereafter lead to a median global warming of 2.8 [2.1 to 3.4] °C by 2100 (medium confidence), and although many countries have signalled an intention to reach net zero GHG or CO2 around mid-century, these pledges differ in scope and specificity and limited policies are in place to date to deliver on them [2].

Cutting emissions comes first

Reaching net zero GHG emissions primarily requires deep reductions in CO2, methane and other GHG emissions, and implies net negative CO2 emissions [2]. Modelled pathways that reach net zero combine several elements: moving away from fossil fuels without CCS towards very low- or zero-carbon energy, demand-side measures and efficiency, cuts in non-CO2 emissions, and CDR [2]. Avoiding overshoot and reliance on future large-scale CDR can only be achieved if global CO2 emissions start to decline well before 2030 (high confidence) [3]. Significant near-term emissions reductions, together with measures to lower energy and land demand, can limit CDR deployment to a few hundred GtCO2 without reliance on bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) (high confidence) [3]. For national portfolios, the choice of CDR methods and the scale and timing of their deployment will depend on several factors: how ambitious gross emissions reductions are, how sustainability and feasibility constraints are managed, and how political preferences and social acceptability evolve [1].

Residual emissions and the timing of net zero

Some hard-to-abate residual emissions will remain and would need to be counterbalanced by CDR to achieve net zero CO2 or GHG emissions, such as some emissions from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes (high confidence) [2]. As a result, net zero CO2 is reached earlier than net zero GHGs (high confidence) [2]. Because residual non-CO2 emissions are expected to be significant, the gap between the two could reach one to several decades [1]. Counterbalancing residual non-CO2 emissions with CO2 removals will lead to net-negative CO2 emissions at the time of net zero GHG emissions [1]. CDR will be necessary to achieve net negative CO2 emissions [2]. In model pathways with no or limited overshoot of 1.5°C, global net CO2 emissions reach net zero around 2050 (2045–2055 interquartile range) [3]. In pathways limiting warming to below 2°C, global net CO2 emissions reach net zero around 2070 (2065–2080 interquartile range) [3].

Beyond net zero: net-negative emissions and overshoot

In 1.5°C pathways, CDR would be used to compensate for residual emissions and, in most cases, to achieve net negative emissions that return warming to 1.5°C after a peak (high confidence) [3]. Net zero GHG emissions, if sustained, are projected to result in a gradual decline in global surface temperatures after an earlier peak (high confidence) [2]. However, understanding of the carbon cycle and climate system is still limited about how effective net negative emissions are at reducing temperatures after they peak (high confidence) [3]. Achieving net-negative CO2 emissions globally does not necessarily involve all countries reaching net-negative levels [1]. Some countries might already be net-negative at the time of global net zero, which would allow other countries more time [1].

Which methods, and how mature they are

Some methods have been practised for decades to millennia, though not necessarily to remove carbon, including afforestation, improved forest management, wetland restoration and soil carbon sequestration [1]. Other methods are novel, including direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS), BECCS and enhanced weathering, and their demonstration and deployment are limited in scale [1]. These newer approaches have medium to low technology readiness levels and are therefore subject to significant uncertainty [1]. CDR methods differ in removal process, timescale of carbon storage, technological maturity, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, adverse side effects and governance requirements [1]. Studies have shown that the life-cycle net emissions of DACCS systems can be negative, even with some current energy mixes, with the GHG intensity of the energy used being a key factor [1]. Estimates of DACCS's future role vary [1]. One modelling study identified a substantial role for DACCS, whereas the IEA's Net Zero scenario deploys CDR on a limited scale, with DACCS removing 0.6 GtCO2 in 2050 [1].

Limits, risks and doubts

CDR deployment of several hundreds of GtCO2 is subject to multiple feasibility and sustainability constraints (high confidence) [3]. Most current and potential CDR measures could have significant impacts on land, energy, water or nutrients if deployed at large scale (high confidence) [3]. Afforestation and bioenergy may compete with other land uses (high confidence) [3]. Effective governance is needed to limit such trade-offs and to ensure that carbon removals are permanent (high confidence) [3]. Soil carbon sinks saturate, and they are reversible if the management practice that promotes them stops [5]. One review notes that available scenarios achieve large-scale removal almost exclusively with BECCS [5]. The same review reports great scepticism that modelled removals of 10–20 GtCO2 per year late this century can be achieved by BECCS alone or by any other single method [5]. It also finds that adding negative emission technologies to cost-optimising scenarios reduces long-term mitigation costs but impedes early emissions reductions [5].

How policy is responding

Countries with national net-zero targets have begun to include CDR in modelled national pathways, to increase research and demonstration, and to consider CDR-specific incentives and policies [1]. This attention has not yet extended to net-negative targets and the policies needed to achieve them [1]. In the UK, the latest national plan, published in October 2021, proposes afforestation, wood in construction, soil carbon and BECCS, as well as DACCS, biochar and enhanced weathering [1]. Most countries report anthropogenic land CO2 fluxes, including those caused by human-driven environmental change such as CO2 fertilisation, on 'managed' land in their national greenhouse gas inventories [2].

What holds up

The best-supported finding is that removing CO2 is a necessary complement to deep emissions cuts, not a substitute for them: it is needed mainly to balance residual emissions from hard-to-abate sectors. The sources are confident that net zero CO2 comes before net zero GHGs, and that reaching net zero GHGs implies net-negative CO2 emissions, which in turn require CDR. The sources are much less certain about scale: very large removals face feasibility, sustainability and land constraints, and the newer methods remain limited in deployment. How well net-negative emissions would reverse a temperature peak is still poorly understood. The sources also indicate that cutting emissions sooner reduces how much removal will be needed later.
