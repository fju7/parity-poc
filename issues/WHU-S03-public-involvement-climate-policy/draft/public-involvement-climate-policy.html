What Holds Up — reliability-study issue WHU-S03, not for publication

Does involving the public make climate policy more popular and more effective?

Study draft, 23 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

Involving the public can change what governments do, but the evidence held here does not show that it reliably wins wider backing, and none of it measures whether the resulting policies cut emissions. France's citizens' climate convention saw most of its recommendations followed up in some form, yet observers found it failed to generate significant support among the broader public. In Luxembourg, whether people accepted a climate assembly's proposals depended mainly on whether they agreed with them, although a process seen as fair softened opposition among those who disagreed. The evidence comes from citizens' assemblies in Europe; none of the sources held here studies the involvement of businesses, workers, young people or Indigenous communities as distinct groups.

What people have been told

UK100, a network of local councils, says its Local Climate Engagement programme demonstrated that when local authorities receive proper support, they can transform their approach to community engagement and build genuine public support for climate action. A 2021 piece in Advanced Science News put it more cautiously: incorporating public input might lead to better decisions and increase popular support for climate policies. The same piece warned that participation offered too late may lead to fake participation, which fuels public resistance.

Did the policies get adopted?

France's Citizens' Convention for the Climate brought together 150 randomly chosen citizens, and in June 2020 it adopted a report containing 149 recommendations. A 2025 analysis for KNOCA, a European network on climate assemblies, looked back at what had happened to those recommendations five years after the Convention. It found that over 70 percent had been followed up by the government, either in full or in a modified form: 20 percent had been fully implemented or even reinforced, and 51 percent implemented partially or modified. The government was more likely to implement recommendations based on incentives rather than regulations, and more likely to modify recommendations for regulations so as to implement them using incentives. The more detailed the guidance citizens put into a recommendation, the more likely it was to be implemented to some degree. The authors also found that the Convention played an agenda-setting role, with some of its recommendations not previously articulated in established political debates making it into law or policy.

The same authors caution that impact on policy cannot simply be traced to the Convention alone, because the wider climate movement and other actors had been advocating many of these ideas. They also note that counting implemented recommendations tells us little about whether the spirit of the Convention was embraced. President Macron vetoed three high-profile recommendations when he received the report, after promising that the measures would go forward with "no filter".

Did the public come with it?

Researchers who observed the French Convention concluded that, while it succeeded in creating consensus among the citizens who were involved, it failed to generate significant support among the broader public. They describe the relationship between the citizens and the general public as one of mutual skepticism. Members of the public contributed nearly 3400 proposals, which remained unused.

In Luxembourg, a panel survey of 1777 people followed the country's first climate assembly, the Klima Biergerrot, which brought together 100 randomly selected citizens. Asked how acceptable it would be for the authorities to implement five of its recommendations for stricter climate policies, a majority of respondents opposed implementation. Agreement with the content of the proposals was the strongest predictor of accepting them. Among people who disagreed with the proposals, acceptance rose the more they saw climate assemblies as legitimate and fair ways of making decisions. Across the whole sample, how people rated the assemblies' procedures had no significant relationship with acceptance. The authors conclude that climate assemblies alone cannot solve the challenge of public reluctance toward climate action.

A survey experiment with 5556 people in France, Germany, Ireland, Italy and Poland tested whether reading about a climate citizens' assembly changed people's views. Its authors found that for most people reading about a climate citizens' assembly makes little difference. They found some minimal effects for the wider public related to voice, and larger effects for some more sceptical groups.

A UK study followed 41 citizens who deliberated over five months on how the country could reach net zero by 2050. They identified government as the prime actor needed to drive the transition, but doubted that it would act.

Did the policies get results?

None of the studies held here measures whether policies made with public involvement cut emissions more than policies made without it. The IPCC's 2022 assessment of national policies and institutions says that participatory forms of governance such as climate assemblies and citizen juries can help enhance the legitimacy of institutional decisions, while empirical assessments suggest that these approaches continue to face practical challenges. Discussing citizen movements, the same chapter says that only a limited number of case studies measure the direct effect of participation as it relates to climate outcomes. It concludes, with medium evidence and high agreement, that institutions and governance frameworks that enable consideration of justice and just transitions are likely to build broader support for climate policymaking.

What this means for a decision

An official weighing a citizens' assembly can expect it to put new proposals on the table, and in France a large share of them found their way into law or policy in some form. What the held evidence does not support is the expectation that involving the public will by itself make stricter climate policies popular: in Luxembourg acceptance followed agreement with the content, and in a five-country experiment hearing about an assembly changed little for most people. Whether participation makes climate policies cut more emissions is not answered by any of these sources.
