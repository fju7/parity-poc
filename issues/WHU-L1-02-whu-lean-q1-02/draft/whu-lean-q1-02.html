Do education programmes for children with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

Programmes aimed at individual children's skills often showed positive effects on skills for learning, but nearly all of the studies were rated low confidence, none measured enrolment, attendance or completion, and the review behind this page cannot say which kinds of programme work best.

What the review covered

The Campbell systematic review by Hunt and colleagues (2025) included 28 studies that evaluated interventions to improve the educational outcomes of people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries [1]. Its authors searched for studies up to March 2022 [1]. Most of the studies (25) targeted children with disabilities; two targeted family members and three targeted service providers [1]. People with intellectual or learning and developmental impairments were the group most often targeted, in 17 studies [1]. The most common kind of programme provided support for educational attainment, followed by programmes to improve the accessibility of learning environments [1].

Do the programmes work?

Of the 18 studies in the review's analysis of effects on skills for learning, 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. The review's abstract reports that the effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy are significant, and that all these effects are large but are based on a low number of studies [1]. Studies of speech and of school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Studies of the conditions for including people with disabilities in education showed what the review calls a moderately significant effect, and one study of teacher knowledge showed a significant effect [1]. Across studies, heterogeneity was high, and the risk of publication bias varied but was frequently high [1].

The review's results section does not agree with its abstract on every outcome: it says the effect on cognitive skills, numeracy, speech and communication is insignificant [1].

No study in the review looked at outcomes related to attendance, enrolment or completion of education [1]. No study in the review reported on qualifications gained, transition to higher levels of education, or education-related quality of life [1].

How much confidence the evidence carries

All but one of the 28 studies received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. The authors say this was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. Most studies used controlled before-and-after designs, followed by uncontrolled before-and-after designs, and a minority were randomised controlled trials [1]. The authors describe the evidence as emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of interventions to improve the education outcomes of people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries [1].

Which kinds work best?

The review does not settle this: its authors write that, because of the broad variety of interventions and outcomes and the limited number of high-quality randomised and quasi-experimental studies, more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious, and how best to deliver them [1]. The interventions and outcome measures used by the included studies were all different, which made direct comparison difficult [1]. Thirteen interventions were conducted in mainstream settings and 12 in segregated or specialised settings [1]. The review did not find any systematic difference in effectiveness between interventions delivered in mainstream settings and those delivered in segregated or specialised settings, which its authors say was likely due to the majority of interventions reporting positive findings [1].

The authors conclude that children with disabilities fall behind in educational outcomes because current school systems are not set up to teach children with different impairment types [1]. They write that there is no one “magic bullet” intervention [1]. They call for a twin-track approach that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities, for example through improving the skills of teachers and the accessibility of the classroom [1]. Most interventions in the review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, and did not focus on bringing these children into mainstream schools through system-level or school-level changes [1]. The authors therefore call for evaluation of interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment [1].

Limits

We hold the full text of the Campbell systematic review by Hunt and colleagues (2025) [1].

We searched: Europe PMC, on 5 November 2026, for systematic reviews and evidence and gap maps of programmes to improve the education of children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries.

We did not search: other bibliographic databases (for example ERIC, Scopus or Web of Science); reports and evaluations published by governments, agencies and charities outside journals; the individual studies the review includes, beyond what the review reports of them.
