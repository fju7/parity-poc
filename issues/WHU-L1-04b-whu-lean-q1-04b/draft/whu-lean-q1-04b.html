What part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero?

Both carbon dioxide removal and emissions reductions are needed to reach the Paris temperature goal, and across cost-effective Paris-compatible scenarios emission reductions contribute at least 80% of the effort to reach net zero CO2.

The question

The question is: “Alongside cutting emissions, what part can removing carbon dioxide from the air play in reaching net zero, now and in the decades ahead?”

The short answer

The IPCC's 2022 mitigation report assessed, with high confidence, that the deployment of carbon dioxide removal (CDR) to counterbalance hard-to-abate residual emissions is unavoidable if net zero CO2 or greenhouse gas emissions are to be achieved.

Its chapter on CDR calls it a necessary element to achieve net zero CO2 and greenhouse gas emissions both globally and nationally, counterbalancing residual emissions from hard-to-transition sectors (robust evidence, high agreement).

The same report assessed, with high confidence, that in addition to deep, rapid and sustained emission reductions, CDR can fulfil three complementary roles: lowering net CO2 or net greenhouse gas emissions in the near term; counterbalancing hard-to-abate residual emissions, such as those from agriculture, aviation, shipping and industrial processes, to help reach net zero in the mid-term; and achieving net negative emissions in the long term if deployed at levels exceeding annual residual emissions.

The 2026 State of Carbon Dioxide Removal assessment puts the division of labour in numbers: across cost-effective scenarios compatible with the Paris Agreement, reductions in emissions contribute at least 80% of the effort to achieve net zero CO2 emissions, and CDR contributes the remainder.

For as long as any emissions continue, CDR will be needed to halt the rise in global temperature, that assessment adds.

How much is removed now

Current removal is almost entirely from land-based, conventional CDR, the well-established methods that largely involve forests and soils.

The State of Carbon Dioxide Removal puts total removal at 2.2 GtCO2 per year, equivalent to 5% of gross CO2 emissions, with conventional CDR making up 99.9% of that total.

Novel CDR, such as biochar, enhanced weathering and direct air carbon capture and storage, is 0.002 GtCO2 per year.

Novel CDR has been growing at 40% per year, which the assessment calls similar to successful technologies like solar energy but insufficient for the scale-up required to meet the Paris temperature goal.

Biochar and bioenergy with carbon capture and storage (BECCS) account for almost all novel removals.

Novel projects now running plus those in construction would reach 0.008 GtCO2 per year of capacity in 2030, although only 20% of planned capacity has been built in recent years.

The IPCC assessed, with high confidence, that afforestation, reforestation, improved forest management, agroforestry and soil carbon sequestration are currently the only widely practiced CDR methods.

How much the pathways use in the decades ahead

Global net zero CO2 emissions are reached in the early 2050s in modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and around the early 2070s in modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%); many of these pathways continue to net negative CO2 emissions after the point of net zero (high confidence).

The level of peak warming depends on cumulative CO2 emissions until the time of net zero CO2 and the change in non-CO2 climate forcers by the time of peaking (high confidence).

At the time of global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, net negative CO2 emissions counterbalance metric-weighted non-CO2 greenhouse gas emissions.

Typical pathways that reach and sustain global net zero greenhouse gas emissions based on the 100-year global warming potential are projected to result in a gradual decline of global warming.

About half of the assessed pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot reach net zero greenhouse gas emissions during the second half of the 21st century, and these show greater reduction in global warming after the peak, to 1.2 [1.1–1.4] °C by 2100, than pathways in the same category that do not, which result in warming of 1.4 [1.3–1.5] °C by 2100.

In modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), there is no significant difference in warming by 2100 between those pathways that reach net zero greenhouse gases (around 30%) and those that do not (high confidence).

All global modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, and those that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), involve rapid and deep and in most cases immediate greenhouse gas emission reductions in all sectors, and deploying CDR to counterbalance residual emissions is among their modelled strategies (high confidence).

At the point modelled pathways reach net zero CO2, 5–16 GtCO2 of emissions from some sectors are compensated for by net negative CO2 emissions in other sectors (high confidence).

In modelled pathways that report CDR and limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, global cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 is 30–780 GtCO2 from BECCS and 0–310 GtCO2 from direct air carbon dioxide capture and storage (DACCS), and the land sector contributes 20–400 GtCO2 of net negative emissions (high confidence).

Total cumulative net negative CO2 emissions including CDR deployment across all options represented in these modelled pathways are 20–660 GtCO2 (high confidence).

In modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%), global cumulative CDR during 2020–2100 from BECCS and DACCS is 170–650 GtCO2 and 0–250 GtCO2 respectively, the land sector contributes 10–250 GtCO2 net negative emissions, and total cumulative net negative CO2 emissions are around 40 [0–290] GtCO2 (high confidence).

Aggregate levels of CDR deployment in these pathways are higher than total net negative CO2 emissions, because some of the deployed CDR is used to counterbalance remaining gross emissions, and total net negative CO2 emissions in modelled pathways might not match the net negative CO2 emissions attributed to individual CDR methods.

The ranges refer to the 5–95th percentile across modelled pathways that include the specific CDR method, and cumulative levels of CDR from the land sector cannot be quantified precisely, because some pathways assess CDR deployment relative to a baseline and different models use different reporting methodologies; total CDR from the land sector equals or exceeds the net negative emissions given.

In modelled pathways that reach global net zero greenhouse gas emissions, at the point they reach it, around 74% [54 to 90%] of global emission reductions are achieved by CO2 reductions in energy supply and demand, 13% [4 to 20%] by CO2 mitigation options in the land sector and 13% [10 to 18%] through the reduction of non-CO2 emissions (medium confidence).

All of the IPCC's Illustrative Mitigation Pathways use land-based biological CDR, primarily afforestation and reforestation, and/or BECCS, and some include DACCS.

As median values across scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, the chapter gives cumulative volumes for 2020–2100 of 328 (168–763) GtCO2 for BECCS, 252 (20–418) GtCO2 for removal in agriculture, forestry and other land use (mainly afforestation and reforestation), and 29 (0–339) GtCO2 for DACCS.

For 2050 the annual medians in those scenarios are 2.75 (0.52–9.45) GtCO2 per year for BECCS, 2.98 (0.23–6.38) GtCO2 per year for land-based removal and 0.02 (0–1.74) GtCO2 per year for DACCS.

Non-CO2 greenhouse gas emissions at the time of net zero CO2 are projected to be of similar magnitude in pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower, about 8 [5–11] GtCO2-eq per year, with the largest fraction from methane (60% [55–80%]), followed by N2O (30% [20–35%]) and F-gases (3% [2–20%]).

Future non-CO2 warming depends on reductions in non-CO2 greenhouse gases, aerosols and their precursors, and ozone precursor emissions, and in low-emission pathways the projected reduction of cooling and warming aerosol emissions leads to net warming in the near to mid-term, mostly because of reduced fossil fuel combustion that was not equipped with effective air pollution controls.

Because methane is short-lived in the atmosphere, projected deep reduction of methane emissions up until the time of net zero CO2 effectively reduces peak global warming (high confidence).

In scenarios that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot, global transport-related CO2 emissions fall by 59% (42–68% interquartile range) by 2050 relative to modelled 2020 emissions, and in scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) by 29% [14–44% interquartile range] (high confidence).

In both categories the transport sector likely does not reach zero CO2 emissions by 2100, so negative emissions are likely needed to counterbalance residual CO2 emissions from the sector (high confidence).

Country pledges reach 2.5 GtCO2 per year of CDR in 2030, against a median of 2.9 GtCO2 per year in the highest-ambition Paris-compatible scenarios, according to the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal.

It puts the resulting gap at 0.3 GtCO2 per year in 2030, growing to 1.2 GtCO2 per year in 2035 and 5.2 GtCO2 per year in 2050.

In the scenarios it assessed, novel CDR scales up by a factor of five between 2030 and 2035 and accelerates to over 3.5 GtCO2 per year by 2050.

Cutting faster means relying less on removal

Modelled pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot involve more rapid and deeper near-term emission reductions, and are projected to have less net negative CO2 emissions and less CDR in the longer term, than pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot (high confidence).

Modelled pathways that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) have on average lower net negative CO2 emissions than pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot and pathways that return warming to 1.5°C after a high overshoot, and the high-overshoot pathways show near-term greenhouse gas emission reductions similar to those of the 2°C pathways (high confidence).

Deep greenhouse gas emission reductions by 2030 and 2040, particularly reductions of methane emissions, lead to less reliance on net negative CO2 emissions that reverse warming in the latter half of the century (high confidence).

Global emission pathways consistent with the national pledges announced before COP26 will likely exceed 1.5°C during the 21st century, and those that then return warming to 1.5°C by 2100 with a likelihood of 50% or greater imply a temperature overshoot of 0.15°C–0.3°C (high confidence).

In such pathways, global cumulative net-negative CO2 emissions are –380 [–860 to –200] GtCO2 in the second half of the century, and there is a rapid acceleration of other mitigation efforts across all sectors after 2030.

Such overshoot pathways imply increased climate-related risk, increased feasibility concerns and greater social and environmental risks, compared with pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot (high confidence).

Projected global emissions from the pledges announced before COP26 would make it likely that warming will exceed 1.5°C and also make it harder after 2030 to limit warming to below 2°C.

A ten-year delay in emissions reductions would raise peak temperature and significantly increase cumulative needs for CDR, the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal concludes, and dependence on such high levels of CDR risks crossing sustainability thresholds.

Land-based options, when sustainably implemented, can deliver large-scale emission reductions and enhanced removals, but cannot fully compensate for delayed action in other sectors (high confidence).

All mitigation strategies face implementation challenges, including technology risks, scaling and costs, and dependence on CDR is among the challenges that are significantly reduced in modelled pathways that assume using resources more efficiently or that shift global development towards sustainability (high confidence).

Land, food and the land sector's limits

Scenarios that limit warming to 2°C (>67%) or lower by 2100 commonly involve extensive mitigation in agriculture, forestry and other land use that at the same time provides biomass for mitigation in other sectors, and bioenergy is the most land-intensive renewable energy option (robust evidence, high agreement).

Growing demands for food, feed, biomaterials and non-fossil fuels increase the competition for land and biomass while climate change creates additional stresses on land, and appropriate integration of bioenergy and other mitigation options with existing land and biomass uses can improve resource use efficiency and mitigate pressures on natural ecosystems (medium evidence, high agreement).

The projected economic mitigation potential of land-sector options between 2020 and 2050, at costs below USD100 per tonne of CO2-equivalent, is 8–14 GtCO2-eq per year, and 30–50% of this potential is available at less than USD20 per tonne and could be upscaled in the near term across most regions (high confidence).

The largest share of that potential, 4.2–7.4 GtCO2-eq per year, comes from the conservation, improved management and restoration of forests and other ecosystems, while improved crop and livestock management and carbon sequestration in agriculture can contribute 1.8–4.1 GtCO2-eq per year and demand-side and material substitution measures 2.1 [1.1–3.6] GtCO2-eq per year.

Demand-side measures together with the sustainable intensification of agriculture can free up land for reforestation and restoration, and the improved and expanded use of wood products from sustainably managed forests also has potential.

Land-sector mitigation measures cannot compensate for delayed emission reductions in other sectors, persistent and region-specific barriers continue to hamper their economic and political feasibility, and assisting countries to overcome those barriers will help to achieve significant mitigation (medium confidence).

Land-sector carbon sequestration and emission reduction options have both co-benefits and risks for biodiversity and ecosystem conservation, food and water security, wood supply, livelihoods and the land rights of Indigenous Peoples, local communities and small land owners, and those that compete for land and land-based resources can pose risks (high confidence).

The scale of benefit or risk largely depends on the type of activity, the deployment strategy and the context, and risks can be avoided when land-sector mitigation is pursued in response to the needs and perspectives of multiple stakeholders.

Realising the land sector's mitigation potential entails overcoming institutional, economic and policy constraints and managing potential trade-offs (high confidence).

Its barriers include insufficient institutional and financial support, uncertainty over long-term additionality and trade-offs, weak governance, insecure land ownership, low incomes and the risk of reversal, as well as limited access to technology, data and know-how, and land-use decisions are spread across a wide range of land owners.

Research and development are key for all land-sector measures, and measures for the mitigation of agricultural methane and N2O emissions with emerging technologies show promising results, but that mitigation is still constrained by cost, the complexity of agricultural systems and increasing demands for yields and livestock products.

Land-based options such as reforestation, forest conservation, ecosystem restoration, sustainable forest management, agroforestry and soil carbon management can have multiple synergies with the Sustainable Development Goals, including sustainable agricultural productivity, food security and addressing land degradation, and maximising those synergies depends on practices, scale, governance and the involvement of local communities and Indigenous Peoples (high confidence).

Trade-offs in employment, water use, land-use competition, biodiversity and access to energy, food and water can be avoided by well-implemented land-based options, and the sustainability of bioenergy and other bio-based products is influenced by feedstock, land management, climatic region and the timing, scale and speed of deployment (medium confidence).

Soil carbon sequestration and biochar can improve soil quality and food production capacity, and ecosystem restoration and reforestation sequester carbon in plants and soil and can enhance biodiversity, but can displace food production and livelihoods, which calls for integrated approaches to land-use planning (high confidence).

Because of the limited application of some of these options today, there are some uncertainties about their potential benefits, and the synergies and trade-offs of mitigation options with the Sustainable Development Goals vary with context and scale.

Some mitigation options can increase competition for land, water and biomass and so reduce adaptive capacity, especially at larger scale and with high expansion rates, in particular where land and water resources are very limited; examples include the large-scale or poorly planned deployment of bioenergy, biochar and afforestation of naturally unforested land (high confidence).

Coordinated policies and the integration of adaptation and mitigation can maximise synergies and minimise trade-offs (medium confidence), and even if extensive global mitigation efforts are implemented, there will be a large need for financial, technical and human resources for adaptation.

Absence or limited resources in social and institutional systems can lead to poorly coordinated responses, reducing the potential for maximising mitigation and adaptation benefits and increasing risk (high confidence).

Limits, costs and side effects

CDR methods vary in terms of their maturity, removal process, time scale of carbon storage, storage medium, mitigation potential, cost, co-benefits, impacts and risks, and governance requirements (high confidence), and implementation strategies need to reflect those differences.

The processes by which CO2 is removed from the atmosphere are categorised as biological, geochemical or chemical.

Estimated storage time scales vary from decades to centuries for methods that store carbon in vegetation and through soil carbon management, to 10,000 years or more for methods that store carbon in geological formations (high confidence).

Costs range from lower cost, such as USD -45 to 100 per tonne of CO2 for soil carbon sequestration, to higher cost, such as USD 100–300 per tonne for DACCS (medium confidence).

Expected costs for removals vary widely, from less than $10/tonne to over $1000/tonne, with most methods having upper limits exceeding $200/tonne, well above current carbon prices, according to the State of Carbon Dioxide Removal.

The same assessment gives the more reliable lower-range estimates of removal potential for most methods as around 1 GtCO2 per year.

Despite limited current deployment, the IPCC chapter estimates moderate to large future mitigation potentials for DACCS, enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods (medium evidence, medium agreement).

The potential for DACCS, 5–40 GtCO2 per year, is limited mainly by requirements for low-carbon energy and by cost.

DACCS is currently at a medium technology readiness level, while enhanced weathering and ocean-based methods are at a low technology readiness level.

Enhanced weathering has the potential to remove 2–4 (full range: <1 to about 100) GtCO2 per year, at costs ranging from USD 50 to 200 (full range: USD 24–578) per tonne of CO2.

Ocean-based methods have a combined potential to remove 1–100 GtCO2 per year, but their feasibility is uncertain due to possible side effects on the marine environment.

The removal and storage of CO2 through vegetation and soil management can be reversed by human or natural disturbances and is prone to climate change impacts, while CO2 stored in geological and ocean reservoirs and as carbon in biochar is less prone to reversal (high confidence).

Reforestation, improved forest management, soil carbon sequestration, peatland restoration and blue carbon management can enhance biodiversity, ecosystem functions, employment and local livelihoods, depending on context (high confidence).

Afforestation or production of biomass crops for BECCS or biochar, when poorly implemented, can have adverse socio-economic and environmental impacts, including on biodiversity, food and water security, local livelihoods and the rights of Indigenous Peoples, especially at large scales and where land tenure is insecure (high confidence).

Upscaling CDR depends on effective approaches to sustainability and feasibility constraints, and its enablers include accelerated research, development and demonstration, improved tools for risk assessment and management, targeted incentives and agreed methods for measurement, reporting and verification of carbon flows (high confidence).

Some mitigation options are feasible to deploy at scale in the near term, but feasibility differs across sectors and regions and according to capacities and the speed and scale of implementation, and barriers would need to be reduced and enabling conditions strengthened to deploy options at scale (high confidence).

These barriers and enablers include geophysical, environmental-ecological, technological and economic factors, and especially institutional and socio-cultural factors, and strengthened near-term action beyond the pledges announced before COP26 can reduce or avoid long-term feasibility challenges of pathways that limit warming to 1.5°C (>50%) with no or limited overshoot (high confidence).

Several options, including improved forest and crop or grassland management, are technically viable, increasingly cost-effective and generally supported by the public, which enables deployment in many regions (high confidence), while many options have adverse environmental impacts when applied at very large scale, for example very large scale bioenergy, and almost all face institutional barriers (medium confidence).

Understanding the co-benefits and trade-offs of mitigation is key to supporting societies to prioritise among policy options (medium evidence, medium agreement), and CDR options can have positive impacts on ecosystem services and the Sustainable Development Goals but also potential adverse side effects.

The implementation of mitigation options would depend on how societies prioritise mitigation against other products and services, including food, material well-being, nature conservation and biodiversity protection, and on considerations such as society's future dependence on CDR.

Effectively responding to climate change while advancing sustainable development will require coordinated efforts among a diverse set of state and non-state actors at global, national and sub-national levels, and food systems governance at the national level tends to be fragmented.

Governance of CDR can support effective and equitable policy implementation, and effectively integrating CDR into mitigation portfolios can build on already existing rules, procedures and instruments for emissions abatement (medium evidence, high agreement).

To accelerate research, development and demonstration and to incentivise CDR deployment, a political commitment to formal integration into existing climate policy frameworks is required, including reliable monitoring, reporting and verification of carbon flows.

The governance of land-based CDR can draw on lessons from previous experience with regulating biofuels and forest carbon, but integrating these insights requires governance that goes beyond project-level approaches.

What it adds up to

On these assessments, removing carbon dioxide is a complement to deep emission cuts rather than a substitute for them: cuts do at least 80% of the work of reaching net zero CO2, removal today comes almost entirely from forests and soils, and the novel methods that pathways scale up after 2030 are still a tiny fraction of total removal today.
