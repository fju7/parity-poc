One section per item. Each must be closed before this issue can
publish. An open item blocks; an item closed "no merit", with the
reason on the record, does not.

For an OBJECTION, from a source a machine could read:

    MERIT:  yes | partly | no   — and why, citing what you read
            (or not_ruled_protected, only on a protected sentence)
    EFFECT: changes | narrows | sharpens | corrects | none
    DID:    what actually changed, or nothing and why

For an OMISSION (type LEAVES_OUT), closed by the omission ruling:

    OMISSION: add | out_of_scope | not_material | not_in_source | not_added_cap
    DID:      what was added, or why nothing was

For a QUESTION, from a source only a person is permitted to read:

    ANSWERED BY: a person's name. Not "the team", not a role.
    ON:          the date they opened the document
    ANSWER:      what it says, quoted
    LOCATOR:     section, table or page
    EFFECT:      changes | narrows | sharpens | corrects | none
    DID:         what changed

Or, for a QUESTION the record already answers, a withdrawal:

    WITHDRAWN BY: a person's name, or the role that withdrew it
    ON:           the date
    WHY:          what on the record already settles it
    DID:          what was done instead

The second form exists because of NCCN v6.2026: one document, readable
by no automated check here, holding the fact that decided the piece.
Everything else on the page could be machine-checked, so the pipeline
behaved as though the page had been checked. Where only a person can
read the source, the person is the check.

### S001-01 — MINOR / NARROWS

**Type.** LEAVES_OUT

**Nearest page sentence.** The passages do not show which interventions work best for different types of disability; the review asked this question, but its answer is not given in the passages here [1].

**They object.** The review does report how its evidence base is distributed by impairment type, including a complete absence of psychosocial or mental impairment studies, and this explains why 'what works best for whom' cannot be answered. The page treats the answer as simply missing from the passages.

**Their document says.** No studies identified in our review captured education interventions for people with psychosocial/mental impairments.
  — *Section 5.1, Target group (participants by impairment type)*

**Why it matters.** The page could say the review had too few studies for most impairment types, and none for psychosocial impairments, to support impairment-specific effectiveness claims. It could also say the authors explicitly reject any single best intervention.

### S001-02 — MINOR / NARROWS

**Type.** LEAVES_OUT

**Nearest page sentence.** In that review, interventions aimed at 'Skills for learning' and at 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' both showed significant effects, but the large effects on specific skills are based on a low number of studies [1].

**They object.** The page attaches the low-number caveat only to the skills effects. The 'Conditions for inclusion' pooled effect is also based on very few studies and has very high heterogeneity, so the two categories are presented as more evenly supported than they are.

**Their document says.** Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education d = 0.35 (0.12 to 0.58) k = 4 n = 401 I² = 86% Egger's test 2.01 ( t = 1.43, p = 0.29) Moderate effect based on a low number of studies with high heterogeneity and potentially moderate publication bias
  — *Section 5.3, Table 14 Effects of interventions*

**Why it matters.** The 'both showed significant effects' claim holds. It rests on four studies with I² of 86%, which is a weaker basis than the sentence implies.

OMISSION: add
DID:      to be inserted word for word after "In that review, interventions aimed at 'Skills for learning' and at 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' both showed significant effects, but the large effects on specific skills are based on a low number of studies [1].": "The review's summary table reports a pooled effect for 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' of d = 0.35 (0.12 to 0.58) from k = 4 studies (n = 401, I² = 86%), which it describes as a \"Moderate effect based on a low number of studies with high heterogeneity and potentially moderate publication bias\" [1]."


### S001-03 — MINOR / NARROWS

**Type.** LEAVES_OUT

**Nearest page sentence.** Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1].

**They object.** The page gives no study count or heterogeneity for this effect, although the review reports both. Without them a reader cannot judge how much weight 'moderately significant' deserves.

  — *5.3. Effects of interventions, Table 14 Effects of interventions*

**Why it matters.** The effect is real in the data but rests on four heterogeneous studies. That affects how far it can support claims about inclusion-focused interventions.

### S001-04 — MINOR / NARROWS

**Type.** LEAVES_OUT

**Nearest page sentence.** The effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but each is based on a low number of studies [1].

**They object.** 'A low number of studies' understates the fragility of at least one headline effect. The handwriting estimate comes from two studies and has an extremely wide confidence interval.

**Their document says.** Handwriting d = 4.86 (0.25 to 9.48) k = 2 n = 101 I ² = 0% Egger's test Low sample size Large effect based on a low number of studies with low heterogeneity.
  — *Table 14 Effects of interventions (effect summary table), Handwriting row*

**Why it matters.** A confidence interval running from 0.25 to 9.48 means the size of the handwriting effect is essentially unknown. The page's 'large' needs that qualifier, and the same applies to the repeat at sentence 21.

OMISSION: not_material
DID:      nothing added; not material; listed for readers

**They object.** The page repeats the large-effects claim without the review's statement that the skills-for-learning effects are heterogeneous. It also omits the handwriting confidence interval (0.25 to 9.48) and that the other studies in the category did not all show significant effects.

### S001-06 — MINOR / NARROWS

**Type.** LEAVES_OUT

**Nearest page sentence.** According to the source, a twin‐track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1].

**They object.** The page presents the twin-track recommendation without saying the review's own data could not distinguish mainstream from specialised delivery. The recommendation is the authors' judgement, not an empirical finding of the pooled effects.

**Their document says.** Our review did not reveal any systematic differences between the effectiveness of interventions delivered in mainstream or segregated/specialised settings, but this was likely due to the majority of interventions reporting positive findings.
  — *Discussion, paragraph comparing with prior reviews (before the Wapling (2016) sentence)*

**Why it matters.** Twin-track is a policy inference. The review could not test it against any setting comparison, and the page should not let it read as evidence-derived.

OMISSION: add
