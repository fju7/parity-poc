Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. In that review, interventions aimed at 'Skills for learning' and at 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' both showed significant effects, but the large effects on specific skills are based on a low number of studies [1]. The passages do not show which interventions work best for different types of disability; the review asked this question, but its answer is not given in the passages here [1].

What the evidence shows

Why this matters

People with disabilities consistently fall behind their peers without disabilities in school enrolment, school completion, mean years of schooling and literacy levels [1]. Evidence suggests that this gap in educational attainment is greatest in LMICs [1]. All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, a twin‐track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities but also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. According to the source, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source states that, "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1] According to the source, "This report illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs." [2] The source, titled "Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low‐ and middle‐income Asia‐Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map," gives only a title and no findings, so it contains no certainty or confidence wording to attribute [2].

Effects found in the 2025 systematic review

Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. Within that group, one study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. Of the 18 studies included in the analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. The effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy were significant and large, but each is based on a low number of studies [1]. The second most common category of intervention was 'Accessible learning environments', which included, for instance, programmes to improve social skills or to reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1].

Teacher training in the Asia-Pacific region

An evidence and gap map found 50 studies on teacher training for the inclusion of students with disabilities in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries [2]. Only 16 of 41 countries in that region reported evidence on in-service teacher professional development for disability inclusion [2]. Some included studies were qualitative and relied on teachers' own reports. In Vietnam, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2]. In Malaysia, special educators agreed that sharing technology-based inclusive practices helped them improve their professional knowledge [2]. One study reported that a one-day teacher workshop on an 'Equality and Non-discrimination Module' influenced students' perspectives of themselves and developed their social awareness skills [2]. No studies were found that reported on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [2].

Gaps in the evidence

Studies have mostly tried to change characteristics of the person with a disability, such as social skills, rather than structures such as the readiness of schools to include people with learning disabilities [3]. Fewer studies focus on improving infrastructure or institutions, and an evidence and gap map describes the absence of evidence on what works to promote disability inclusion as a very striking gap [3]. Another source makes the same point, noting that most research focuses on improving skills in students with disabilities rather than addressing institutional or environmental barriers [2]. Most studies come from upper-middle-income countries, with limited evidence from low-income countries [3]. More studies are needed on all types of impairment, including visual and hearing impairments [3]. Earlier syntheses noted that very little literature compared educational outcomes for disabled and nondisabled peers, and that most studies focused on special school populations [4]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high, which lowers confidence in the pooled results [1]. All but one of the included studies received an overall rating of low confidence in their findings [1]. The review found that its lack of confidence across studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through school-level or system-level changes [1]. The finding says evaluation is needed of interventions that target not only the individual with a disability but also their broader environment [1]. Only three studies reported interventions aimed at supporting mental health among students with a disability, so evidence on this type of programme is sparse [2].

What holds up

The best-supported claim is that a range of individual-level interventions, mostly targeted at people with disabilities themselves, work to improve their educational outcomes in LMICs [1]. Skills-for-learning interventions show large, significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but each of these effects rests on a low number of studies [1]. There is less evidence that changing schools or systems works, because little research has been done on it, not because studies have shown it fails [1, 3]. The review authors state that more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to education and successful engagement in it [1]. So the passages cannot say which kind of programme works best overall or for particular disabilities, and the evidence comes mainly from upper-middle-income countries.
