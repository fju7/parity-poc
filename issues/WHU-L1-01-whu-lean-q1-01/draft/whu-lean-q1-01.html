Do phone-based tracking, prompts and reminders improve primary care?

The 2025 Cochrane review concludes that these tools may improve some care practices and some health outcomes. It also concludes that they led to small or no outcome differences in most studies.

The question

The question is: “In primary health care, does using mobile phones or tablets to keep track of the care each patient gets, together with on-screen prompts that guide health workers, reminder messages sent to patients, or both, improve the care people receive and their health?”

The evidence

The 2025 Cochrane review set out to assess the effects of using a mobile device to track service use combined with decision support for health workers, with targeted messages to clients, or with both.

It identified 18 eligible studies, 11 randomised and seven non-randomised, conducted in Bangladesh, China, Ethiopia, India, Kenya, Palestine, Uganda and the USA.

All the non-randomised studies had a high risk of bias; the main results are from the randomised studies, and the review reports findings from non-randomised studies separately, graded as very low-certainty evidence.

The review uses may for low-certainty evidence, probably for moderate-certainty evidence, and uncertain for very low-certainty evidence.

The care people receive

With tracking and decision support in antenatal and postnatal care, the tools may slightly increase home visits in the week following delivery (2 studies, 4531 participants).

With tracking, decision support and client messages together, the tools probably increase guideline adherence for antenatal screening and management of anaemia, diabetes and hypertension, but probably lead to lower adherence for abnormal foetal growth, each finding from one study.

Tracking with decision support and client messages probably slightly increases the number of adults with hypertension or diabetes who report a better change in the quality of their care (1 study, 3324 participants).

The review reports that no studies evaluated the time between presentation and appropriate management, the timeliness of receiving or accessing care, provider acceptability or satisfaction, resource use, or unintended consequences.

Their health

With tracking and decision support, the tools may reduce low-birthweight babies (1 study, 3023 participants), but their effects on stillbirths and on neonatal and infant deaths are uncertain.

With tracking and client messages, in one study of 1226 stroke patients over 12 months, the tools may reduce deaths and stroke hospitalisations. In the same 12-month study of 1226 stroke patients, tracking with client messages may also slightly increase blood pressure medication adherence (risk ratio (RR) 1.10 [1.00, 1.21]), may slightly reduce systolic blood pressure (mean difference (MD) -2.80 mmHg [-4.90, -0.70]) and may slightly improve EQ-5D quality-of-life scores (MD 0.04 [0.02, 0.06]).

With all three together, the tools may reduce new cardiovascular events in adults at high cardiovascular risk (1 study, 8642 participants).

In one study of 6367 women receiving antenatal care, all three together may make little or no difference to adverse pregnancy outcomes, moderate or severe anaemia, or large-for-gestational-age babies.

In one study of 3324 adults with hypertension or diabetes, all three together may make little or no difference to HbA1c, total cholesterol or 10-year cardiovascular risk.

The effects of all three together are uncertain on maternal or infant mortality in HIV-positive mothers, and on blood pressure control, fasting glucose and quality of life in adults with hypertension or diabetes.

How sure anyone can be

The review's authors write that they have little confidence in the findings because there were not many studies, some studies were small, and others had design problems.

Seventeen of the 18 studies were from low- and middle-income settings where electronic medical record systems are rare or just being rolled out.

The review examined studies conducted between 2000 and November 2022.

What this means

The review's conclusion is that digital tracking may improve primary care workers' ability to follow recommended antenatal and chronic disease practices, the quality of patient records, patient health outcomes and service use.

Its authors add that these interventions led to small or no outcome differences in most studies.

On the evidence we hold, then, the answer is a qualified one: the tools may help in some situations, mostly on low-certainty evidence, and in most studies the differences were small or absent.

What we read, and its limits

We hold the full text of the 2025 Cochrane review [1].

Of the 2025 Cochrane review [1], we read: the abstract; the plain language summary.

We searched: Europe PMC, for systematic reviews and trials of digital tracking combined with provider decision support or targeted client communication via mobile devices in primary health care.

We did not search: trial registries; guideline repositories; databases other than Europe PMC; grey literature.
