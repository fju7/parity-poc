Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries, but that there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review describes this as emerging support for the efficacy and effectiveness of such interventions, not settled proof [1]. The strongest reported effects are for programmes that build children's skills for learning, such as literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, but these large effects are based on a low number of studies [1]. The passages do not report which interventions work best for different types of disability, although the review set out to ask this question [1].

What the evidence shows

Why the question matters

People with disabilities often fall behind their peers without disabilities in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. Evidence suggests that the gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is greatest in low- and middle-income countries [1]. One evidence map notes that, although international directives place obligations on states, evidence on which interventions are actually effective has not been established [1]. According to the source, "All but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings." [1] According to the source, a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. However, currently most interventions included in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system‐level or school‐level changes [1]. The source states: "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed." [1] The source states: "This report illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs." [2] The provided text is only the title of the source, "Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low‐ and middle‐income Asia‐Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map", and it contains no finding or certainty/confidence wording, so no such wording can be attributed to it [2].

Effects by type of intervention

Of the 18 studies in the review's analysis of effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. By outcome, the review reports significant effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, all large but based on a low number of studies [1]. Studies on 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. Only one study concerned with teacher knowledge was analysed, and it showed a significant effect size [1]. The second most common category of intervention was 'Accessible learning environments', such as programmes to improve social skills or reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1].

Teacher training

An evidence and gap map found 50 studies on teacher training for the inclusion of students with disabilities in low- and middle-income Asia-Pacific countries, with only 16 of 41 countries reporting evidence [2]. A significant number of these interventions focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability, and only three studies discuss supporting mental health among students with a disability [2]. Some evidence is qualitative: in one Vietnamese study, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [2]. The map's authors stress the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability-inclusive teacher professional development in these countries [2].

Gaps in the evidence

Most interventions tried to improve children's skills but did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. A broader evidence map similarly found that studies focus on 'fixing individuals', with fewer studies addressing infrastructure, institutions or social barriers to inclusion [3]. The included studies used different interventions and outcome measures, which makes direct comparison difficult [1]. None of the review's studies were done in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated findings by gender [1]. The broader evidence map reports a significant gap in study quality, especially for impact evaluations, and limited evidence from low-income countries [3]. The review's authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the studies reviewed received an overall rating of low confidence in their findings [1]. The review found that the lack of confidence across studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs, and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. The source says a twin-track approach is needed, one that both meets the specific needs of children with disabilities and includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teacher skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in the systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source says evaluation is needed of interventions that target not only the individual with a disability but also their broader environment [1].

What holds up

It holds up that a range of individual-level programmes, especially those building literacy, numeracy, cognitive and handwriting skills, have shown significant effects in low- and middle-income countries, though the support is described as emerging and rests on few studies [1]. Whether school-level or system-level approaches work better cannot be judged from these passages, because such approaches have been little studied [1, 3]. The review's authors argue that more systemic approaches are needed to improve access to, and successful engagement in, education [1]. Claims about which interventions suit which types of disability, or about effects in humanitarian or low-income settings, go beyond what this evidence supports.
