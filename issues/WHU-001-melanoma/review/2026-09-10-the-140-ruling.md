## 1. The urgent question: was melanoma's green computed over its whole page?

`rule_rows` says *"Every sentence on the page, no exemptions"* and iterates
`bindings.json`. `scan()` creates a row only where an anchor is detectable. So
rule 1's population is **sentences with a detectable anchor**, not sentences on
the page — and the docstring claims otherwise.

Which means the reported numbers have the wrong denominator. "135 of 169 fail" is
a fraction of a selected population, and **318 of cdk46's 487 body sentences are
examined by neither rule.** The 135 are at least reported. The 140 are invisible,
and invisible is worse: a page can be entirely unbound and produce a clean-looking
rule 1 line as long as nothing in it is anchor-detectable.

**And melanoma was published yesterday on the strength of "rules 1 and 2 green on
all 131 sentences."** I am not going to conclude anything from that — the last
three times I reasoned from a summary I was wrong, and this is the fourth
opportunity. But the question is exact and it is the first thing that needs
answering:

> For **melanoma**: what is `page_sentences`' count, what is `rule_rows`' count,
> and were rules 1 and 2 evaluated over the first or only over the second?

- If 131 is the page-sentence count and both rules ran over all of it, melanoma
  is sound, cdk46's gap is a property of an older page written before the
  apparatus, and yesterday's publish stands.
- If 131 is the rowed population and melanoma's page has substantially more body
  sentences, then **yesterday's publish rested on a check that examined part of
  the page and reported "all"** — and that is a live matter about a live page,
  not a backlog.

Report the two numbers before anything else in this block.

## 2. The docstring is the finding, and of all the places for it to be

> `rule_rows` opens "Every sentence on the page, no exemptions" and does not do
> that.

Failure 15 — a check whose scope is narrower than its name — **in the file that
defines this publication's foundational rule.** It is also the unread-signature
failure: a control described in words that nothing performs. Both entries already
exist; this instance is more serious than either because everything else rests on
it.

Two things follow and they are different:

**The scope claim must be corrected today**, whatever the melanoma answer is. A
docstring that overstates what a function examines is how the next person
concludes that a green rule 1 means the page is bound. That is one line and it
costs nothing.

**The check itself needs a third outcome**, exactly as the epistemic check does:
sentences with no detectable anchor are **NOT EXAMINED**, reported and counted,
never absent. The principle is already recorded — *a check that cannot evaluate
something reports it, never silently passes it* — and this is the same shape one
layer down. Rule 1's output should read "N examined, M not examined" and the
second number should be in Appendix D, beside the epistemic coverage.

That is not a redesign. It is making an existing check say what it actually did.

## 3. My hypothesis was wrong, and that is the measurement doing its job

I predicted most of the 135 would be judgements lacking a declaration.
Judgements have no rows at all, so none of them are in the 135. Wrong, and
settled in one pass.

Worth recording plainly, because it is the counter-example to the pattern this
week has been full of: **I offered a hypothesis and asked for the measurement
instead of acting on the hypothesis.** Had I directed a plan on the strength of
"most of them are probably judgements", the work would have gone to declarations
while 140 unseen empirical sentences stayed unseen. The rule that saved it —
decompose and measure before deciding — is failure 18, written yesterday, and
this is the first time it has been followed rather than violated.

Note it in that entry as its first successful application. An entry with only
violations under it is a complaint; one with an application is a rule.

## 4. The three questions — notes for the operator, not decisions

These are the operator's, and I am not taking them. But two of the three turn on
whether a claim meets this publication's own standard, which is method rather
than editorial judgement, and on those I have a view worth having in front of him.

**G2 — the scoped negative. This one has a defect independent of what the page
should argue.**

> *"We asked, on 3 September 2026, and there is no such sentence."*

It names a date and nothing else — not which version of the guideline, not which
sections, not what was searched for. **A reader cannot repeat it.** Appendix C
exists to list universal negatives *so that a reader can try to falsify them*,
and a negative whose scope is unstated cannot be falsified; it can only be
believed. That is the Morning Glory class and the RV-09 class in a new instance —
a search declared conclusive without its bounds stated.

Whatever is decided about the guideline's verbatim text, that sentence needs its
scope: which document, which version, which sections, what string. It is a
correction to how the claim is made, not to what the page thinks.

**G3 — marking a reading as ours may not be enough, and the reason is question 3.**

The page says the guideline nowhere defines "directly compared", and that our
reading is ours. It does not say what else the phrase could mean. Run it through
the passage stage's third question: *could a careful reader leave believing
something false that no sentence states?* A reader told only that a reading is
ours, with no alternative named, has been given a label but not the material to
judge it — and will reasonably infer that ours is the only available reading,
which is the thing the marking was meant to prevent. **Marking is not
disclosing.**

**G1 — the test direction. This one is properly and entirely the operator's.**
It is a judgement about what the page should say, not about whether a claim meets
a standard, and I have no business ruling on it. The only observation I would
offer is the one already made under OR-C: whatever is said, the reader should be
able to see the sequence rather than be told a count.
