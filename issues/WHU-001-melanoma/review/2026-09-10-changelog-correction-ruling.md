# Ruling — the change-log sentence, and the premises that never get checked

---

## 1. My Appendix D claim was wrong, and the reason is the useful part

I wrote that Appendix D omits the largest instance of its own subject and that
fixing it requires a republish. Appendix D is not on the page. It is in the
review packet, it goes to the outside reviewer, and you had already wired the
number into `coverage_md()` hours earlier.

What is worth recording is not the error but its selection. **In the same message
I insisted the page-claim question be checked verbatim before drafting anything,
and asserted the Appendix D fact without checking it at all.** The difference was
not importance. It was that I noticed I did not know one and did not notice about
the other, because Appendix D had appeared in a dozen of my own rulings and had
become furniture in my own reasoning.

> **The premises most in need of verification are the ones that feel settled
> because they have been repeated, not because they were ever checked.** A fact
> you have used ten times reads as established; the repetition is doing the work
> that evidence should.

That belongs beside the "verify the premise" rule, because it says which
premises. Anything I have been referring to confidently for days is a candidate,
and confidence acquired by repetition is the tell.

---

## 2. The change-log sentence — correct it, do not delete it

> *"Publishing the corrections above meant binding every sentence on this page to
> the words it rests on, which is the check our own account of ourselves depends
> on and which had fallen behind."*

131 of 343. The sentence is false, it is unscoped, it is reader-facing, it is on
a live page — and it sits inside the entry headed **"And what the review did not
catch."** A claim of complete verification, inside the passage about what
verification missed, published on the strength of a function whose docstring
overstated its own scope. If this publication has a single worked example of its
own subject, that is it.

**Delete would be the flattering move** — the false claim disappears and nothing
is said. Same as the Morning Glory clause: what is owed is a correction, not a
quiet removal.

**Draft, to be checked against the page rather than dropped in:**

> Publishing the corrections above meant binding the sentences on this page that
> carry a figure, a quotation or a named source to the words they rest on — 131
> of this page's 343 sentences. We first wrote that it meant binding *every*
> sentence. That was not true. The other 212 were never examined by that check;
> the function that reported the result opened with "every sentence on the page,
> no exemptions" and in fact examined only the sentences in which it could detect
> an anchor. Not examined is not the same as unsupported, and nothing on this page
> has been shown wrong by any of it — but we did not know which it was, and we
> said that we did.

The last clause is the whole correction. Everything before it is arithmetic.

**And the correction-log entry** should carry the placement, because it is not
decoration: the false claim was in the entry about what was missed, and it was
missed. Also the corpus figure — 412 of 1,256 across three issues — since the
sentence being corrected is a claim about coverage and a reader who has just been
told this page's number is owed the publication's.

## 3. The derived list — the design decision is right and the reason should be
## the recorded one

> *"Collapsing 'here is what is open' into 'here is what stops you' would take
> rule 13's second requirement away from you as well."*

Correct, and better than anything I would have specified. Rule 13's two
requirements are knowing what is open and choosing to publish anyway. A list that
pre-decides which items are fatal supplies the first and quietly removes the
second — the operator is left agreeing with a verdict rather than making a
decision. Record that as the reason the list does not rank.

**Six of eight invisible to the signer** is the measurement, and your distinction
matters: not concealed, several printing in gate output at the time, but nobody
assembling them. That is the difference between information being available and
information being *delivered*, and rule 13 needs the second.

Three of the eight I had never seen and would not have thought to ask for — the
two failed correction lookups reported as "NOT known to be clean" rather than as
clean, the five documents checked only against a length floor, and the two
sentences resting on a human attestation no check may read. That last one is
worth a line of its own in the acceptance: **there are sentences on this page
whose support is a person's word, and the apparatus knows exactly which two.**
Naming them is better than any assurance about them.

## 4. What the republish is, now that it is one thing

The change-log correction is the only reader-facing repair outstanding on
melanoma, and it is the occasion for the fresh acceptance. One republish, one
correction entry, one acceptance whose open list nobody assembled from memory —
the first in this publication's history.

The page carries the coverage number because the corrected sentence is about
coverage. It does not carry the other seven items; those are the acceptance's
instrument, not the reader's.
