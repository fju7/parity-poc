## Tier 1 — factual errors and hard contradictions

### F1. "and the register says so" is false

**The sentence.** "The August announcement was a prespecified interim look, and the
register says so."

**The problem.** It does not. I pulled the full ClinicalTrials.gov record for
NCT05933577 through the API. The string `interim` appears **zero times** in the
entire record, in any case. Worse, the record's `lastUpdatePostDate` is

The interim fact is real; it comes from the companies. Moderna's release says:
"At a pre-specified interim analysis, intismeran in combination with KEYTRUDA as
adjuvant therapy demonstrated statistically significant and clinically meaningful
improvements in RFS and DMFS compared to KEYTRUDA alone."

**Why it matters beyond the one clause.** The same sentence leans on the registry as
a place where a Phase 3 number "does not appear." A record last updated in September
2025 is not evidence about an August 2026 disclosure — it is a stale file. The
registry's *emptiness* is currently doing evidentiary work it cannot do.

**Fix.** Attribute the interim-analysis fact to the two company releases. Wherever
the registry is used as a negative, state its last-update date so the reader can see
what the silence is worth.

### F3. The confidence interval moved the other way

**The sentence.** "At five years the point estimate was unchanged at 0.510 and the
interval read 0.294–0.887 — the upper bound had come in from 0.906, **the lower bound
had drifted out from 0.288**, and the width was close to unchanged."

**The problem.** 0.288 → 0.294 is the lower bound moving **in**, not out. Both bounds
moved inward; the interval narrowed at both ends (width 0.618 → 0.593, about 4%
narrower on the raw scale). "Out" fails on every reading available: 0.294 is closer
to 1.0 than 0.288, closer to the 0.510 point estimate, and further from zero only in
the direction that makes the interval tighter.

**Fix.** "Both bounds came in — the upper from 0.906 to 0.887 and the lower from
0.288 to 0.294 — so the interval narrowed only slightly."

### F4. The adverse-event contrast mixes two categories and two data cuts

**The sentence.** "Any-grade immune-related adverse events were similar — 45.2% on the
combination versus 44% on pembrolizumab alone — but grade 3 or worse treatment-related
events were higher: 25% versus 18%."

**The problem.** Verified against both source documents:

| figure | category | document | follow-up |
|---|---|---|---|
| 45.2% vs 44% | immune-related, any grade | Merck 5-year release (S002) | 5 years |
| 25% vs 18% | **treatment-related**, grade ≥3 | Lancet 2024 (S003) | ~2 years |

The sentence's grammar — "similar … but … higher" — presents these as one measure at
two severities. They are two different categories at two different data cuts. A
like-for-like pair exists inside a single document you hold: the Lancet abstract
reports immune-mediated AEs at **36% (37/107) vs 36% (18/50)** *and* grade ≥3
treatment-related at 25% vs 18%, same patients, same cut.

This is the article's own thesis one level down: two correctly-sourced numbers placed
side by side, doing work neither can do.

**Fix.** Use the Lancet's own pair (36/36 and 25/18) for the contrast, and report the
5-year 45.2/44 separately with its follow-up named. If you keep the 5-year figures,
note that the 5-year release's severity breakdown for intismeran-attributed events is
grade 3 in 4.8% with no grade 4–5 — a different denominator again.

### F6. The score cannot move "in whichever direction the numbers point"

**The sentences.** "That single release … would move data support from 1 to 4 or 5 and
source quality from 3 to 5. Expect the score to move by roughly a full point when it
lands, in whichever direction the numbers point."

### F8. The three-trial denominator is padded

**The sentence.** "in the three trials whose registry records we hold — KEYNOTE-054,

it shows.

## Tier 3 — the teaching sections contradict themselves and their sources

### F11. The article's central rule is the practice its own statistics source condemns

**The sentence.** "The single most useful question to ask of any interval: does it cross
1.0?" — reinforced visually, with bars coloured **amber if they cross and green if they
clear**.

**The problem.** The piece's authority on statistical inference is Greenland, Senn,
Rothman, Carlin, Poole, Goodman and Altman (S022). That paper exists to argue against
exactly this move. The piece quotes the relevant span itself, in J09: "the P value is
degraded into a dichotomy in which results are declared 'statistically significant' if
P falls on or below a cut-off (usually 0.05) and declared 'nonsignificant' otherwise."

Asking whether an interval crosses 1.0 is that dichotomy in interval form. The colour
coding hard-codes it. So the article teaches, as its single most useful rule and its
main visual device, the practice its own cited authority calls a degradation — while
elsewhere criticising the reflex it is training.

J04 knows this: it labels itself "Our own editorial advice, not a finding." The reader
is never told.

**Fix.** Demote the rule from "the single most useful question" to one of two, and give
the second: how wide is it, and what does the far end mean? Then let the colours encode
width or precision rather than a threshold — or add a line under the chart saying the
amber/green split is a convention, not a verdict, and that the article uses it because
readers meet it everywhere.

### F12. The chart judges the trial by a standard the article says the trial never used

**The tension.** The chart marks KEYNOTE-942's 2023 recurrence bar amber because its
**two-sided 95%** interval, 0.309–1.017, crosses 1.0. Two sections later the article
establishes that the trial prespecified a **one-sided alpha of 0.10** and concludes:
"0.053 is a near miss only against a 0.05 line this trial never used."

A two-sided 95% interval *is* the 0.05 line, in interval form. The chart applies it and
the p-value section disowns it.

You hold the matching interval. The three-year paper (S007) reports the trial's own
**80% CI: 0.351–0.743**, which does not include 1.0. Your source note explains the 95%
interval was chosen "so that it can be read beside the others on this page" — a
defensible visual choice, buried where no reader will find it.

**Fix.** Put the 80% interval on the chart as a second, lighter bar for the 2023
readout, or add a caption line: "These are 95% intervals, the convention readers will
meet elsewhere. This trial registered a one-sided alpha of 0.10; on its own standard the
2023 interval (80% CI 0.351–0.743) did not include 1.0."

### F13. The p-value defence omits which way the trial's threshold leaned

**The sentences.** The section teaches 0.05 as "an arbitrary line, but a widely used
one," then: "the trial was designed with approximately 80% power to detect a hazard
ratio of 0.5 against a one-sided alpha of 0.10 … So 0.0266 was inside its own
prespecified threshold by a wide margin."

**What the reader is not told.** A one-sided alpha of 0.10 corresponds to a two-sided
0.20 — four times more permissive than the convention the reader was taught two
paragraphs earlier. The trial's bar was lower than the standard bar, and clearing it by
"a wide margin" means clearing a low bar comfortably.

This is not an accusation: a permissive one-sided alpha is a normal, defensible choice
for a mid-stage signal-finding trial, and the article already says so. The problem is
asymmetry. The piece is meticulous about flagging when a comparison flatters. Here it
supplies the exculpating context and omits the qualifying half of it.

**Fix.** One clause: "— a permissive threshold, equivalent to a two-sided 0.20, which is
a normal choice for a phase 2b trial designed to find a signal rather than settle one."

### F14. S023 was never opened, and it contains the caveat your explainer needs

You marked Spruance, Reid, Grace & Samore, *Hazard Ratio in Clinical Trials*
(Antimicrob Agents Chemother 2004;48(8):2787–92, PMID 15273082, PMCID PMC478551) as
`not_opened`. I read it. It carries two things the hazard-ratio explainer is missing.

**(a) Proportional hazards — the assumption the explainer asserts without naming.**

Your gloss: "It means that **at any given moment during the trial**, someone in the
treatment group was recurring at about half the rate of someone in the comparison
group."

That sentence *states the proportional hazards assumption as a fact*. A single hazard
ratio summarising five years of follow-up assumes the ratio is constant across those
five years. Spruance is explicit that the Cox model imposes this and that it can be
violated. For a therapeutic vaccine — where the biological story is that immune priming
builds over months — non-proportional hazards is not an academic worry, it is the most
likely way a single HR misdescribes what happened. And it bears directly on the
article's own observation that DMFS "eased from 62% at three years to 59% at five,"
which is what a drifting hazard ratio looks like from outside.

**(b) A patient-level gloss you are allowed to use.**

The explainer correctly forbids "49% of patients were saved" and then leaves the reader
with rate language, which is honest and unsatisfying. Spruance supplies the legitimate
patient-level translation: the probability that the treated patient does better is
**P = HR / (1 + HR)**. Worked for this trial's recurrence hazard of 0.510: take a
treated and an untreated patient at random, and there is roughly a **66% chance** the
treated one goes longer without recurrence — against 50% if the therapy did nothing.

Spruance's own framing is the best sentence in the paper for your audience: the
difference between hazard-based and time-based measures is "analogous to the odds of
winning a race and the margin of victory." The hazard ratio tells you who is likely to
win. It does not tell you by how much.

**Fix.** Add both. A "the assumption underneath" note in the hazard-ratio card naming
proportional hazards, and the 66% figure with the race analogy in "The number that
matters more."

### F18. The strongest unstated assumption on the page: functional unblinding

Two facts appear on the page and are never joined.

1. The article's reassurance: "It is double-blind and placebo-controlled, so nobody
   assessing a recurrence knows which treatment produced it… an assessor who does not
   know the arm cannot favour it."
2. The article's own safety figures, from the same programme: **injection-site pain
   59.6%**, **chills 51.0%**, fatigue 59.6%.

The Phase 3 comparator is an intramuscular injection of placebo — the registry names it
"Normal saline, Dextrose." An intramuscular therapeutic given against saline, producing
injection-site pain in three of every five recipients and chills in half, is a
reactogenic drug against an inert one. Patients can often tell. Treating physicians can
often tell. And the primary endpoint is assessed **by the investigator**.

That is the mechanism by which a formally double-blind trial retains the assessment bias
the article says the design removes. The article holds both halves and never puts them
together — and this is the one place where doing so changes a conclusion, not just a
sentence: it is the strongest available argument that the Phase 3's rigor is a 4 rather
than a 5, and it is an argument the article's own numbers make.

I am not claiming the trial was unblinded. I am claiming the article asserts blinding
solves the problem, holds the evidence that it may only partly solve it, and does not
say so.

**Fix.** In "Why the blinding row matters," after the reassurance: "One caveat the
figures on this page raise themselves. Intismeran is an intramuscular injection that
caused injection-site pain in 59.6% of patients and chills in 51.0% in the earlier
trial; the Phase 3 comparator is a saline injection. Where a therapy is that much more
reactogenic than its placebo, patients and their doctors can sometimes infer the arm,
and the primary endpoint is investigator-assessed. Blinding on paper is not always
blinding in the room. Central adjudication is the standard answer to this, and this
trial does not use it."
