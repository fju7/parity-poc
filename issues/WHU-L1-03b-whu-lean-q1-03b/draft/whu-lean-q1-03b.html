If greenhouse gas emissions continue, how will further warming change the climate?

The question

The question is: “If greenhouse gas emissions continue, how will further warming change the climate: rainfall and the water cycle, very wet and very dry extremes, ice, sea level and the oceans?”

The short answer

Under every emissions scenario the assessment considered, global surface temperature will continue to increase until at least mid-century. Global warming of 1.5°C and 2°C will be exceeded during the 21st century unless deep reductions in CO2 and other greenhouse gas emissions occur in the coming decades. The IPCC assesses that many changes in the climate system become larger in direct relation to increasing global warming, including heavy precipitation, agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions, marine heatwaves, and reductions in Arctic sea ice, snow cover and permafrost. Continued global warming is projected to further intensify the global water cycle, including its variability, global monsoon precipitation and the severity of wet and dry events. Many changes due to past and future greenhouse gas emissions are irreversible for centuries to millennia, especially changes in the ocean, ice sheets and global sea level. Compared with 1850–1900, global surface temperature averaged over 2081–2100 is very likely to be higher by 1.0°C to 1.8°C under the very low emissions scenario (SSP1-1.9), by 2.1°C to 3.5°C under the intermediate scenario (SSP2-4.5) and by 3.3°C to 5.7°C under the very high scenario (SSP5-8.5). The last time global surface temperature was sustained at or above 2.5°C higher than 1850–1900 was over 3 million years ago (medium confidence). It is virtually certain that the land surface will continue to warm more than the ocean surface (likely 1.4 to 1.7 times more), and virtually certain that the Arctic will continue to warm more than global surface temperature, with high confidence above two times the rate of global warming. It notes that its estimate of when 1.5°C is first exceeded is not directly comparable to the earlier special report's finding of likely reaching it between 2030 and 2052, which came from a simple linear extrapolation of recent warming rates. The assessment uses 1850–1900, the earliest period of sufficiently globally complete observations, as an approximation for pre-industrial conditions.

Rainfall and the water cycle

The assessment reports strengthened evidence since its previous report that the global water cycle will continue to intensify as global temperatures rise (high confidence). Precipitation and surface water flows are projected to become more variable over most land regions within seasons (high confidence) and from year to year (medium confidence). By 2081–2100, relative to 1995–2014, average annual global land precipitation is projected to increase by 0–5% under the very low emissions scenario, 1.5–8% under the intermediate scenario and 1–13% under the very high scenario (likely ranges). Under the intermediate, high and very high emissions scenarios, precipitation is very likely to increase over high latitudes, the equatorial Pacific and parts of the monsoon regions, and to decrease over parts of the subtropics and limited areas in the tropics. There is high confidence in an earlier onset of spring snowmelt, with higher peak flows at the expense of summer flows in snow-dominated regions globally. The portion of the global land experiencing detectable increases or decreases in seasonal mean precipitation is projected to increase (medium confidence). Monsoon precipitation is projected to increase in the mid- to long term at the global scale, particularly over South and South East Asia, East Asia and West Africa apart from the far west Sahel (high confidence). The monsoon season is projected to have a delayed onset over North and South America and West Africa (high confidence) and a delayed retreat over West Africa (medium confidence). Rainfall variability related to the El Niño–Southern Oscillation is very likely to be amplified by the second half of the 21st century under the intermediate, high and very high emissions scenarios. In the long term under high emissions scenarios, a southward shift and intensification of Southern Hemisphere summer mid-latitude storm tracks and their precipitation is likely, but in the near term stratospheric ozone recovery counteracts these changes (high confidence). There is medium confidence in a continued poleward shift of storms and their precipitation in the North Pacific, and low confidence in projected changes in the North Atlantic storm tracks. At 1.5°C of global warming, a small number of regions are projected to experience increases or decreases in mean precipitation (medium confidence). At 2°C of global warming and above, mean precipitation is projected to increase in all polar, northern European and northern North American regions, most Asian regions and two regions of South America (high confidence). At 2°C and above, region-specific changes include increases in river floods (medium to high confidence) and reductions in mean precipitation with increases in aridity (medium to high confidence). In the assessment's maps of simulated change at 1.5°C, 2°C and 4°C of global warming, observed and simulated patterns are consistent across most regions, and across warming levels land areas warm more than ocean areas, and the Arctic and Antarctica warm more than the tropics. In the same maps, precipitation increases over high latitudes, the equatorial Pacific and parts of the monsoon regions and decreases over parts of the subtropics and in limited areas of the tropics, and changes in soil moisture largely follow changes in precipitation but also show some differences due to the influence of evapotranspiration. Urbanization also increases mean and heavy precipitation over and/or downwind of cities (medium confidence) and resulting runoff intensity (high confidence).

Very wet and very dry extremes

A warmer climate will intensify very wet and very dry weather and climate events and seasons, with implications for flooding or drought (high confidence). Where and how often these events occur depends on projected changes in regional atmospheric circulation, including monsoons and mid-latitude storm tracks. With every additional increment of global warming, changes in extremes continue to become larger. Every additional 0.5°C of global warming causes clearly discernible increases in the intensity and frequency of hot extremes, including heatwaves (very likely), and of heavy precipitation (high confidence), as well as of agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions (high confidence). Projected changes in agricultural and ecological droughts are primarily assessed based on total column soil moisture. Meteorological droughts show discernible changes in some regions for every additional 0.5°C of warming, with more regions showing increases than decreases (medium confidence), and increases in hydrological droughts become larger with warming in some regions (medium confidence). Some extreme events unprecedented in the observational record will occur increasingly with additional warming, even at 1.5°C, and projected percentage changes in frequency are larger for rarer events (high confidence). It is very likely that heavy precipitation events will intensify and become more frequent in most regions with additional global warming. At the global scale, extreme daily precipitation events are projected to intensify by about 7% for each 1°C of global warming (high confidence). The proportion of intense tropical cyclones (Category 4–5) and peak wind speeds of the most intense tropical cyclones are projected to increase at the global scale with increasing global warming (high confidence). At 1.5°C of global warming, heavy precipitation and associated flooding are projected to intensify and be more frequent in most regions in Africa and Asia (high confidence), North America (medium to high confidence) and Europe (medium confidence). At 1.5°C, more frequent or more severe agricultural and ecological droughts are projected in a few regions in all inhabited continents except Asia, compared with 1850–1900 (medium confidence). At 2°C of global warming and above, the level of confidence in and the magnitude of the change in droughts and in heavy and mean precipitation increase compared with 1.5°C. At that level, heavy precipitation and associated flooding events are projected to become more intense and frequent in the Pacific Islands and across many regions of North America and Europe (medium to high confidence). Several regions in Africa, South America and Europe are then projected to see more frequent or more severe agricultural and ecological droughts, with medium to high confidence. Many regions are projected to see an increase in the probability of compound events with higher global warming (high confidence), and concurrent heatwaves and droughts are likely to become more frequent. Concurrent extremes at multiple locations, including in crop-producing areas, become more frequent at 2°C and above compared with 1.5°C of global warming (high confidence). In the assessment's synthesis of climatic impact-drivers, for a 20–30 year period centred around 2050 or consistent with 2°C of global warming, all regions are projected to experience changes in at least 5 of them, almost all (96%) in at least 10 and half in at least 15, with wide geographical variation. In coastal cities, more frequent extreme sea level events combined with extreme rainfall or river flow events will make flooding more probable (high confidence). Some mid-latitude and semi-arid regions, and the South American Monsoon region, are projected to see the highest increase in the temperature of the hottest days, at about 1.5 to 2 times the rate of global warming (high confidence). The Arctic is projected to experience the highest increase in the temperature of the coldest days, at about three times the rate of global warming (high confidence). All regions are projected to experience further increases in hot climatic impact-drivers and decreases in cold ones (high confidence), and extreme heat thresholds relevant to agriculture and health are projected to be exceeded more frequently at higher global warming levels (high confidence). Cities intensify human-induced warming locally, and further urbanization together with more frequent hot extremes will increase the severity of heatwaves (very high confidence).

Ice

Additional warming is projected to further amplify permafrost thawing and loss of seasonal snow cover, of land ice and of Arctic sea ice (high confidence). The Arctic is likely to be practically sea ice-free in September at least once before 2050 under the five illustrative scenarios the assessment considered, with more frequent occurrences for higher warming levels. Practically sea ice-free means a monthly average sea ice area of less than 1 million km2, about 15% of the average September sea ice area observed in 1979–1988. There is low confidence in the projected decrease of Antarctic sea ice. All regions are projected to see further decreases in permafrost, in snow, glaciers and ice sheets, and in lake and Arctic sea ice (medium to high confidence), and these changes would be larger at 2°C of global warming or above than at 1.5°C (high confidence). Mountain and polar glaciers are committed to continue melting for decades or centuries (very high confidence). Loss of permafrost carbon following permafrost thaw is irreversible at centennial time scales (high confidence). Continued ice loss over the 21st century is virtually certain for the Greenland Ice Sheet and likely for the Antarctic Ice Sheet, and there is high confidence that total ice loss from the Greenland Ice Sheet will increase with cumulative emissions. There is limited evidence for low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes, from ice-sheet instability processes characterized by deep uncertainty, that would strongly increase ice loss from the Antarctic Ice Sheet for centuries under high emissions scenarios.

Sea level

It is virtually certain that global mean sea level will continue to rise over the 21st century. Relative to 1995–2014, the likely global mean sea level rise by 2100 is 0.28–0.55 m under the very low emissions scenario, 0.32–0.62 m under the low scenario, 0.44–0.76 m under the intermediate scenario and 0.63–1.01 m under the very high scenario (medium confidence). To compare with the 1986–2005 baseline used in AR5 and SROCC, 0.03 m is added to these estimates, and 0.16 m to compare with a 1900 baseline. By 2150 the likely rise is 0.37–0.86 m under the very low scenario and 0.98–1.88 m under the very high scenario (medium confidence). Global mean sea level rise above the likely range, approaching 2 m by 2100 and 5 m by 2150 under a very high emissions scenario (low confidence), cannot be ruled out because of deep uncertainty in ice-sheet processes. In the longer term, sea level is committed to rise for centuries to millennia because of continuing deep-ocean warming and ice-sheet melt, and will remain elevated for thousands of years (high confidence). Over the next 2000 years, global mean sea level will rise by about 2 to 3 m if warming is limited to 1.5°C, 2 to 6 m if limited to 2°C and 19 to 22 m with 5°C of warming (low confidence). Projections of multi-millennial global mean sea level rise are consistent with reconstructed levels during past warm periods: likely 5–10 m higher than today around 125,000 years ago, and very likely 5–25 m higher roughly 3 million years ago (medium confidence). It is very likely to virtually certain that regional mean relative sea level rise will continue throughout the 21st century, except in a few regions with substantial geologic land uplift rates. Approximately two-thirds of the global coastline has a projected regional relative sea level rise within ±20% of the global mean increase (medium confidence). Extreme sea level events that occurred once per century in the recent past are projected to occur at least annually at more than half of all tide gauge locations by 2100 (high confidence). Relative sea level rise contributes to increases in the frequency and severity of coastal flooding in low-lying areas and to coastal erosion along most sandy coasts (high confidence).

The oceans

Past greenhouse gas emissions since 1750 have committed the global ocean to future warming (high confidence). Over the rest of the 21st century, ocean warming is likely to be 2–4 times the 1971–2018 change under the low emissions scenario (SSP1-2.6) and 4–8 times under the very high scenario (SSP5-8.5). Upper ocean stratification (virtually certain), ocean acidification (virtually certain) and ocean deoxygenation (high confidence) will continue to increase in the 21st century, at rates dependent on future emissions. Changes are irreversible on centennial to millennial time scales in global ocean temperature (very high confidence), deep-ocean acidification (very high confidence) and deoxygenation (medium confidence). With additional global warming, the frequency of marine heatwaves will continue to increase (high confidence), particularly in the tropical ocean and the Arctic (medium confidence). The ocean and land carbon sinks take up a smaller proportion of emissions as cumulative CO2 emissions increase, which is projected to leave a higher proportion of emitted CO2 in the atmosphere (high confidence). Under the intermediate emissions scenario, which stabilizes atmospheric CO2 concentrations this century, the rates of CO2 taken up by the land and ocean are projected to decrease in the second half of the 21st century (high confidence). Under the very low and low emissions scenarios, where CO2 concentrations peak and decline during the 21st century, the land and ocean begin to take up less carbon (high confidence) and turn into a weak net source by 2100 under the very low scenario (medium confidence). It is very unlikely that the combined global land and ocean sink will turn into a source by 2100 under scenarios without net negative emissions. The Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation is very likely to weaken over the 21st century for all emissions scenarios, with high confidence in the decline but only low confidence in the magnitude of the trend. There is medium confidence that it will not collapse abruptly before 2100, and if it did, it would very likely cause abrupt shifts in regional weather patterns and the water cycle, such as a southward shift in the tropical rain belt and drying in Europe.

Outcomes the assessment cannot rule out

Low-likelihood outcomes, such as ice-sheet collapse, abrupt ocean circulation changes, some compound extreme events, and warming substantially larger than the assessed very likely range of future warming, cannot be ruled out and are part of risk assessment. Low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes could occur at global and regional scales even for global warming within the very likely range for a given emissions scenario. The assessment defines low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes as those whose probability of occurrence is low or not well known, as in the context of deep uncertainty, but whose potential impacts on society and ecosystems could be high, and a tipping point as a critical threshold beyond which a system reorganizes, often abruptly and/or irreversibly. If global warming exceeds the assessed very likely range for a given emissions scenario, changes in many aspects of the climate system, such as regional precipitation, would also exceed their assessed very likely ranges (high confidence). Such low-likelihood, high-warming outcomes are associated with potentially very large impacts, such as through more intense and more frequent heatwaves and heavy precipitation, and high risks for human and ecological systems, particularly for high emissions scenarios. The probability of low-likelihood, high-impact outcomes increases with higher global warming levels (high confidence), and abrupt responses such as strongly increased Antarctic ice-sheet melt cannot be ruled out (high confidence). If global warming increases, some compound extreme events with low likelihood in past and current climate will become more frequent (high confidence). By the end of the century, very low and low emissions scenarios would strongly limit the increases in the frequency of extreme sea level events, heavy precipitation and pluvial flooding, relative to higher emissions scenarios (high confidence). Scenarios with very low and low emissions would lead to substantially smaller changes in a range of climatic impact-drivers beyond 2040 than high and very high emissions scenarios. Changes would also be smaller in very low compared with low emissions scenarios, and for intermediate compared with high or very high emissions scenarios (high confidence). Scenarios with very low or low emissions lead within years to discernible effects on greenhouse gas and aerosol concentrations and air quality, relative to high and very high emissions scenarios. Under these contrasting scenarios, discernible differences in trends of global surface temperature would begin to emerge from natural variability within around 20 years, and over longer time periods for many other climatic impact-drivers (high confidence). Scenarios with very low or low emissions would have rapid and sustained effects to limit human-caused climate change, compared with scenarios with high or very high emissions, but early responses of the climate system can be masked by natural variability. For global surface temperature, differences in 20-year trends would likely emerge during the near term under a very low emissions scenario, relative to a high or very high one, and the response of many other climate variables would emerge from natural variability at different times later in the 21st century (high confidence). If global net negative CO2 emissions were achieved and sustained, the CO2-induced surface temperature increase would be gradually reversed but other climate changes would continue in their current direction for decades to millennia (high confidence). It would take several centuries to millennia for global mean sea level to reverse course even under large net negative CO2 emissions (high confidence). Projected human-caused changes in mean climate and in extremes will be either amplified or attenuated by internal variability (high confidence). The main internal variability phenomena include the El Niño–Southern Oscillation, Pacific Decadal Variability and Atlantic Multi-decadal Variability, through their regional influence. The assessment calls physical climate system conditions such as means, events and extremes that affect an element of society or ecosystems climatic impact-drivers; depending on system tolerance their changes can be detrimental, beneficial, neutral or a mixture, and their types include heat and cold, wet and dry, wind, snow and ice, coastal and open ocean. Decadal variability has both enhanced and masked underlying human-caused long-term changes in the historical global surface temperature record, and this variability will continue into the future (very high confidence). Internal decadal variability and variations in solar and volcanic drivers partially masked human-caused surface global warming during 1998–2012 (high confidence), but the heating of the climate system continued, as reflected in the continued warming of the global ocean (very high confidence) and the continued rise of hot extremes over land (medium confidence). Internal variability has largely been responsible for the amplification and attenuation of the observed human-caused decadal-to-multi-decadal mean precipitation changes in many land regions (high confidence). At global and regional scales, near-term changes in monsoons will be dominated by the effects of internal variability (medium confidence), and near-term projected changes in precipitation are also uncertain because of model uncertainty and uncertainty in forcings from natural and human-caused aerosols (medium confidence). Based on paleoclimate and historical evidence, it is likely that at least one large explosive volcanic eruption would occur during the 21st century. Such an eruption would reduce global surface temperature and precipitation, especially over land, for one to three years, alter the global monsoon circulation, modify extreme precipitation and change many climatic impact-drivers (medium confidence), and would therefore temporarily and partially mask human-caused climate change.

What has already changed

Globally averaged precipitation over land has likely increased since 1950, with a faster rate of increase since the 1980s (medium confidence). It is likely that human influence contributed to the pattern of observed precipitation changes since the mid-20th century, and extremely likely that it contributed to the pattern of observed changes in near-surface ocean salinity. Mid-latitude storm tracks have likely shifted poleward in both hemispheres since the 1980s, with marked seasonality in trends (medium confidence), and for the Southern Hemisphere human influence very likely contributed to the poleward shift of the closely related extratropical jet in austral summer. The frequency and intensity of heavy precipitation events have increased since the 1950s over most land area for which observational data are sufficient for trend analysis (high confidence), and human-induced climate change is likely the main driver. It is virtually certain that hot extremes, including heatwaves, have become more frequent and more intense across most land regions since the 1950s, while cold extremes have become less frequent and less severe, with high confidence that human-induced climate change is the main driver of these changes. Some recent hot extremes observed over the past decade would have been extremely unlikely to occur without human influence on the climate system. Marine heatwaves have approximately doubled in frequency since the 1980s (high confidence), and human influence has very likely contributed to most of them since at least 2006. It is likely that the global proportion of major (Category 3–5) tropical cyclone occurrence has increased over the last four decades, and very likely that the latitude where tropical cyclones in the western North Pacific reach their peak intensity has shifted northward; these changes cannot be explained by internal variability alone (medium confidence). There is low confidence in long-term (multi-decadal to centennial) trends in the frequency of all-category tropical cyclones. Human-induced climate change increases heavy precipitation associated with tropical cyclones (high confidence), but data limitations inhibit clear detection of past trends on the global scale. Human-induced climate change has contributed to increases in agricultural and ecological droughts in some regions due to increased land evapotranspiration (medium confidence). The assessment defines agricultural and ecological drought as a period with abnormal soil moisture deficit, resulting from combined shortage of precipitation and excess evapotranspiration, and treats observed changes in meteorological droughts (precipitation deficits) and hydrological droughts (streamflow deficits) as distinct from it. Human influence is very likely the main driver of the global retreat of glaciers since the 1990s and of the decrease in Arctic sea ice area between 1979–1988 and 2010–2019. There has been no significant trend in Antarctic sea ice area from 1979 to 2020 due to regionally opposing trends and large internal variability. Human influence very likely contributed to the decrease in Northern Hemisphere spring snow cover since 1950. It is very likely that human influence has contributed to the observed surface melting of the Greenland Ice Sheet over the past two decades, but there is only limited evidence, with medium agreement, of human influence on the Antarctic Ice Sheet mass loss. In 2011–2020, annual average Arctic sea ice area reached its lowest level since at least 1850 (high confidence). Late summer Arctic sea ice area was smaller than at any time in at least the past 1000 years (medium confidence). The global nature of glacier retreat since the 1950s, with almost all of the world’s glaciers retreating synchronously, is unprecedented in at least the last 2000 years (medium confidence). It is virtually certain that the global upper ocean (0–700 m) has warmed since the 1970s, extremely likely with human influence as the main driver, and virtually certain that human-caused CO2 emissions are the main driver of current global acidification of the surface open ocean. There is high confidence that oxygen levels have dropped in many upper ocean regions since the mid-20th century and medium confidence that human influence contributed to this drop. Global mean sea level increased by 0.20 m between 1901 and 2018, and its average rate of rise increased from 1.3 mm per year between 1901 and 1971 to 3.7 mm per year between 2006 and 2018 (high confidence); human influence was very likely the main driver of these increases since at least 1971. Throughout the assessment, main driver means responsible for more than 50% of the change. Global mean sea level has risen faster since 1900 than over any preceding century in at least the last 3000 years (high confidence). Human-caused net positive radiative forcing causes an accumulation of additional energy in the climate system, partly reduced by increased energy loss to space in response to surface warming. The observed average rate of heating of the climate system increased from 0.50 W m–2 for 1971–2006 to 0.79 W m–2 for 2006–2018 (high confidence), and ocean warming accounted for 91% of the heating, with land warming, ice loss and atmospheric warming accounting for about 5%, 3% and 1% (high confidence). The global ocean has warmed faster over the past century than since the end of the last deglacial transition, around 11,000 years ago (medium confidence). Surface open ocean pH as low as in recent decades is unusual in the last 2 million years (medium confidence), after a long-term increase in surface open ocean pH over the past 50 million years (high confidence). Heating of the climate system has caused global mean sea level rise through ice loss on land and thermal expansion from ocean warming, with thermal expansion explaining 50% of the rise during 1971–2018, and ice-sheet and glacier mass loss together were the dominant contributors to global mean sea level rise during 2006–2018 (high confidence).
