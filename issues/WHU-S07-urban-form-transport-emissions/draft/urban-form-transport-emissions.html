Could denser, better-connected cities and transport pricing cut transport emissions?

Yes, but the measures' reported effects differ: compact, mixed-use development is estimated to cut VMT, energy use and CO2 by less than 1 percent to 11 percent by 2050, while Stockholm's 2006 charges cut traffic across the cordon by approximately 20%, though these figures measure different things. The assessments that put a figure on it are scenarios to 2050, while the measured effects come both from pricing, such as the roughly 20% traffic reduction across Stockholm's charging cordon, and from studies of urban form, in which density and destination accessibility have the highest elasticities.

What the assessments say

The Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC) concludes, with high confidence, that changes in urban form such as density, land-use mix, connectivity and accessibility, in combination with programmes that encourage changes in consumer behaviour, such as transport pricing, could reduce transport-related greenhouse gas emissions in developed countries and slow growth in emissions in developing countries.

Its chapter on urban systems puts a figure on the planning side: integrated spatial planning for compact growth, with co-located housing and jobs, mixed land use and transit-oriented development, could reduce greenhouse gas emissions by between 23% and 26% by 2050 compared with business as usual.

In the body of that chapter, the 23–26% figure is attached to modifying the layout of emerging urbanisation, and the studies it cites include Creutzig et al. 2015.

That analysis found that for affluent and mature cities, higher gasoline prices combined with compact urban form can save both residential and transport energy, while for cities with emerging infrastructure, compact form and transport planning can avoid locking in high-carbon travel patterns.

It projected that urban planning and transport policies could limit urban energy use in 2050 to 540 EJ, against 730 EJ if current trends in urban expansion continue.

The IPCC chapter on transport says cities can reduce their transport-related fuel consumption by around 25% through combinations of more compact land use and less car-dependent transport infrastructure, and that transport demand management incentives are expected to be necessary to support these changes.

These are modelled estimates for whole urban systems over decades, not the measured result of any one policy.

How much density alone changes driving

A study of US cities by Duranton and Turner, which tries to separate the effect of density from the choices of households that prefer to drive less, estimates the elasticity of driving with respect to nearby density at between -7% and -10%, about -8% in its preferred specification.

On those estimates, a 20% reduction in driving through density alone would require a 25-fold increase in density for everyone.

A more realistic policy that moved 1% of a metropolitan population from middle-density to high-density areas would reduce aggregate driving by about 0.07%, because driving rises slightly in the areas that lose population.

The authors conclude that densification policies are unlikely to be a cost-effective way to reduce driving-related carbon emissions compared with gasoline taxes and congestion pricing.

A 2009 US National Research Council committee found that the literature suggests doubling residential density across a metropolitan area might lower household vehicle travel by about 5 to 12 percent, and perhaps by as much as 25 percent if coupled with higher employment concentrations, significant public transit improvements, mixed uses and other supportive demand management measures.

In scenarios that build a share of new and replacement housing more compactly, the committee estimated that the resulting reductions in vehicle travel, energy use and CO2 emissions would be in the range of less than 1 percent to 11 percent by 2050, although committee members disagreed about whether the changes in development patterns and public policies needed to reach the high end are plausible; a scenario in which residents of compact developments drive 12 percent less yielded reductions in fuel use and CO2 emissions of about 1 percent in 2030 and 1.3 to 1.7 percent in 2050.

The committee disagreed about whether the changes in development patterns and public policies needed to reach the high end of that range are plausible.

The IPCC urban chapter also reports that the effect of urban form on VMT per capita varies by a number of factors, but that density and destination accessibility have the highest elasticities, followed by design.

It adds that the benefits of spatial planning can be attained only when the existing spatial structure of an urban area does not limit locational and mobility options.

What pricing has done where it was tried

When Stockholm introduced its congestion charge in 2006, the reduction of traffic across the charging cordon stabilised at approximately 20%.

In Gothenburg, which introduced a charge in 2013, the reduction in traffic across the cordon during charged hours stabilised at approximately 12%.

Ten years on, the price elasticity of traffic across the cordon was lower when charging levels were increased than when charges were first introduced, in both Stockholm and Gothenburg, and substantially higher in the off-peak than in the peak; the long-term elasticity was increasing in Stockholm but declining in Gothenburg.

A review of European interventions, which screened nearly 800 studies, found the congestion charge among the three intervention types most effective at reducing overall car use.

In that review, London's congestion charge was the most effective at reducing car traffic across the cordon of a charging zone, with a reduction of 33%.

An analysis of road transport emissions in European countries detected ten policy interventions that reduced emissions by between 8% and 26%, and found that the most successful mixes combined carbon or fuel taxes with incentives for cleaner vehicles.

The IPCC transport chapter cites a meta-analysis by Labandeira et al. (2017) that reports average long-term price elasticities of demand of -0.773 for gasoline and -0.443 for diesel.

These pricing results are falls in traffic across charging cordons, with travel-time gains on the wider road network, or in road emissions at national level, rather than city-wide results of pricing and urban form combined.

What this means for a decision

The assessments support the belief that denser, mixed, connected cities together with pricing could cut transport emissions, but the sources narrow it in two ways.

Making the layout of emerging urbanisation more compact, walkable and co-located is modelled to cut future urban energy use by 20–25% by 2050; established cities' largest savings come from retrofitting buildings, modal shift, electrification, and infilling and densifying; and a causal study of US cities finds that increases in density cause only small decreases in driving.

Set against what is known about the effects of gasoline taxes and congestion prices, densification looks unlikely to be a cost-effective way to cut aggregate driving, and the assessments expect transport demand management incentives to be needed to support urban form changes rather than urban form acting alone.
