What Holds Up Issue three · 30 Aug 2026

There is a widely held sense that using AI is making us worse at thinking, and that there is now real evidence for it. The evidence usually offered is weak. The best evidence anyone has is real, and almost nobody outside medicine has met it with its qualifications intact.

Four endoscopy centres in Poland adopted computer-aided polyp detection at the end of 2021. Looking only at the colonoscopies performed without AI — 1,443 of them, 795 before and 648 after — the rate at which experienced doctors found precancerous growths fell from 28.4% to 22.4%. That is a drop of 6.0 percentage points, on a 95% confidence interval of −10.5 to −1.6. The nineteen endoscopists had each performed more than 2,000 colonoscopies and averaged 27.6 years since graduation.

It is a retrospective observational study, and that governs everything you can take from it. Its authors list eleven limitations of their own. The largest is that procedures in the second period nearly doubled, from 795 to 1,382, because Poland suspended its screening programme on 1 January 2022 and the centres drew patients off the waiting list. Withdrawal time was not recorded. There was no blinding. The authors write that AI exposure may reduce detection, and call for randomised crossover trials.

And the caveats were not hidden. On the day the study published, the Science Media Centre put three named academics on the record for journalists, one of whom identified the doubling problem precisely. We had expected to find that this evidence reached the public stripped of its qualifications. At the source, it did not.
When the AI’s suggestion was right, twenty-seven radiologists scored about 80%. When it was wrong, the same readers scored 19.8%, 24.8% and 45.5% by experience. The most experienced were wrong more often than right whenever the machine was.

The mechanism was measured in 2022, in the same procedure, by people who then said what worried them about it. Twenty-one participants watched 29 recorded colonoscopy sequences in eye-tracking glasses, each sequence once with the AI overlay and once without, three weeks apart. Median eye travel distance fell from 248.86 cm to 232.68 cm — about 6.5% across all participants, about 8% among the experienced and about 3% among novices. Their reading of it: effort decreased and gaze remained more focused, presumably waiting for a bounding box to appear, which might be efficient but risks missing a polyp that is not captured by the system.

Two findings in the same study cut against any simple story. The system detected polyps faster than people did, but the people using it were no faster than the people without it. And misreading got worse: participants falsely identified a polyp in a median of 4 cases without the AI and 6 cases with it.

Is it one study, or a pattern? A 2026 scoping review screened 373 records and included twelve studies, describing the evidence as scarce, limited and fragmented. A consistent direction, carried by very few measurements.

We had planned to set a null result beside all this — a training study the review reports as finding performance stable at three months. A check on this page requires every name to be verified against the paper’s own byline, so we opened it. It has eight residents, not 32. It measures scoring error and agreement between readers, not sensitivity or reading time. And it has no three-month follow-up at all.

There is a real null result, and we missed it. On 3 June 2026 — nearly three months before we drafted this — Pedersen and colleagues published a prospective, multicentre trial in Endoscopy: thirteen endoscopists, 5,013 colonoscopies, run in three phases, before the AI, during it, and after it was taken away. Among inexperienced endoscopists detection rose while the AI ran and returned to baseline after it went (odds ratio 1.03, 95% CI 0.79–1.34). Among experienced endoscopists there was no significant effect at any phase. Their conclusion: In non-CADe-assisted colonoscopy, no upskilling or deskilling was observed following a period of CADe exposure. And the two results do not cancel: the Norwegian authors point out that they measured performance after the AI was withdrawn, while the Polish study measured it during a period when endoscopists were still using AI on their other lists — so deskilling may be a temporal phenomenon that disappears over time after CADe is removed. They call their own endpoint their most important limitation, and they narrow their conclusion to less experienced endoscopists. Yuichi Mori is an author of both papers. An outside reviewer found this study after our own checks had passed over it twice; the assessment says so.

And the studies a general reader has actually been offered are the weak ones. The brain-scan preprint everyone cites had 54 participants, and 18 in the session carrying its claim about lasting effects; four researchers have published a comment noting that its brain measure was relative, so fewer significant connections does not mean less activity. The 2011 memory result has two failed replications behind it. The famous correlation of −0.68 measured all three of its variables by self-report in one instrument. And the most-cited meta-analysis on the reassuring side, with more than 500 citations, was retracted in April 2026.
