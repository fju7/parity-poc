WHAT HOLDS UP
Issue three - 30 Aug 2026

WHAT HAPPENS TO THE EXPERTS FIRST


There is a widely held sense that using AI is making us worse at
thinking, and that there is now real evidence for it. The evidence
usually offered is weak. The best evidence anyone has is real, and
almost nobody outside medicine has met it with its qualifications
intact.

Four endoscopy centres in Poland adopted computer-aided polyp detection
at the end of 2021. Looking only at the colonoscopies performed WITHOUT
AI - 1,443 of them, 795 before and 648 after - the rate at which
experienced doctors found precancerous growths fell from 28.4% to
22.4%. That is a drop of 6.0 percentage points, on a 95% confidence
interval of -10.5 to -1.6. The nineteen endoscopists had each performed
more than 2,000 colonoscopies and averaged 27.6 years since graduation.

IT IS A RETROSPECTIVE OBSERVATIONAL STUDY, and that governs everything
you can take from it. Its authors list eleven limitations of their own.
The largest is that procedures in the second period nearly doubled,
from 795 to 1,382, because Poland suspended its screening programme on
1 January 2022 and the centres drew patients off the waiting list.
Withdrawal time was not recorded. There was no blinding. The authors
write that AI exposure MAY reduce detection, and call for randomised
crossover trials.

And the caveats were not hidden. On the day the study published, the
Science Media Centre put three named academics on the record for
journalists, one of whom identified the doubling problem precisely. We
had expected to find that this evidence reached the public stripped of
its qualifications. At the source, it did not.

The mechanism was measured in 2022, in the same procedure, by people
who then said what worried them about it. Twenty-one participants
watched 29 recorded colonoscopy sequences in eye-tracking glasses, each
sequence once with the AI overlay and once without, three weeks apart.
Median eye travel distance fell from 248.86 cm to 232.68 cm - about
6.5% across all participants, about 8% among the experienced and about
3% among novices. Their reading of it: effort decreased
and gaze "remained more focused, presumably waiting for a bounding box
to appear", which "might be efficient but risks missing a polyp that is
not captured by the system".

Two findings in the same study cut against any simple story. The system
detected polyps faster than people did, but the people using it were no
faster than the people without it. And misreading got worse:
participants falsely identified a polyp in a median of 4 cases without
the AI and 6 cases with it.

IS IT ONE STUDY, OR A PATTERN? A 2026 scoping review screened 373
records and included twelve studies, describing the evidence as scarce,
limited and fragmented. A consistent direction, carried by very few
measurements.

We had planned to set a null result beside all this - a training study
the review reports as finding performance stable at three months. A
check on this page requires every name to be verified against the
paper's own byline, so we opened it. It has eight residents, not 32. It
measures scoring error and agreement between readers, not sensitivity
or reading time. AND IT HAS NO THREE-MONTH FOLLOW-UP AT ALL.

THERE IS A REAL NULL RESULT, AND WE MISSED IT. On 3 June 2026 -
nearly three months before we drafted this - Pedersen and colleagues
published a prospective, multicentre trial in Endoscopy: thirteen
endoscopists, 5,013 colonoscopies, run in three phases, before the AI,
during it, and after it was taken away. Among inexperienced
endoscopists detection rose while the AI ran and returned to baseline
after it went (odds ratio 1.03, 95% CI 0.79-1.34). Among experienced
endoscopists there was no significant effect at any phase. Their
conclusion: "In non-CADe-assisted colonoscopy, no upskilling or
deskilling was observed following a period of CADe exposure." It is
not randomised and its endpoint is polyp detection at 5 mm rather than
adenoma detection - and Yuichi Mori is an author of it and of the
Polish study both. An outside reviewer found it after our own checks
had passed over it twice. The assessment says so.

AND THE STUDIES A GENERAL READER HAS ACTUALLY BEEN OFFERED ARE THE WEAK
ONES. The brain-scan preprint everyone cites had 54 participants, and
18 in the session carrying its claim about lasting effects; four
researchers have published a comment noting that its brain
measure was relative, so fewer significant connections does not mean
less activity. The 2011 memory result has two failed replications
behind it. The famous correlation of -0.68 measured all three of its
variables by self-report in one instrument. And the most-cited
meta-analysis on the reassuring side, with more than 500 citations, was
retracted in April 2026.

This is a statement about evidence and not advice. Nothing here bears
on whether AI-assisted colonoscopy is good for patients: the randomised
evidence that AI raises detection during the procedure is not in
dispute and is not what the assessment examined.
