### CE-01 — role says BROKEN (HIGH confidence)

**We say.** Nobody has traced the belief that AI degrades thinking back through its evidential lineage — the 2011 Google-effect studies, the 2025 MIT cognitive-debt preprint, and Gerlich's 2025 survey — and shown for a general reader that the foundational finding failed replication and the modern ones are small or cross-sectional.

**What breaks it.** Multiple published and publicly accessible pieces have done exactly this lineage-tracing for a general reader, naming the Sparrow 2011 Google-effect studies, noting their replication failures, and flagging the MIT preprint's small sample and Gerlich's cross-sectional design. The clearest single instance is a Space Daily / general-audience article (June 2026) that quotes Gerlich's own hedge — 'the study highlights correlations rather than direct causation' — and explicitly warns readers that 'correlation' is the operative word, while a Substack post ('brain drain,' Digital Learning Lab) traces the lineage from the Google-effect literature through the MIT preprint and flags the n=18 swap-task subsample and the familiarisation-effect alternative explanation. A Psychology Today blog post (May 2025) explicitly connects Sparrow 2011 → Gerlich 2025 for a lay audience. The Hesselmann 2020 PeerJ replication failure of the Google Stroop effect is cited in a publicly accessible PMC article and summarised in a ScienceABC explainer (May 2026) that names Camerer et al. 2018 and Hesselmann 2020 as failed replications and notes a 2024 meta-analysis found the effect 'smaller and more context-dependent.' The claim that nobody has done this is false: the lineage has been traced, the replication failures named, and the size/design limitations of the modern studies flagged, all for general readers.

### CE-02 — role says BROKEN (HIGH confidence)

**We say.** No published work questions whether the very large correlation Gerlich reports between AI use and critical thinking could arise from common-method variance or construct overlap rather than from AI use damaging thinking.

**What breaks it.** At least three published or publicly accessible sources raise exactly this methodological concern. First, a Frontiers in Education article (October 2025, DOI 10.3389/feduc.2025.1719625) states explicitly that Gerlich (2025) and related models 'often rely on self-reported perceptions without examining underlying cognitive constructs such as critical thinking or reasoning ability' — a direct statement of the construct-validity / common-method problem. Second, a Michelle Miller PhD Substack post (February 2025) takes a close critical look at Gerlich's study design and flags that both AI usage and cognitive offloading are self-reported, raising the question of whether the correlation is an artefact of shared method variance. Third, a Nature Reviews Psychology piece (October 2025, DOI 10.1038/s44159-025-00507-0) demonstrates in a related domain that 'common method bias can lead to spurious associations between measures' in self-report studies — directly applicable to Gerlich's design, where AI use frequency, cognitive offloading, and critical thinking self-assessments are all collected from the same respondent in the same survey sitting. The construct-overlap problem is also implicit in the Space Daily general-audience piece, which notes that Gerlich himself acknowledges 'additional factors may also contribute.' None of these is a formal published reanalysis of Gerlich's data, but the claim that 'no published work questions' this is false: the question has been raised in a peer-reviewed journal article, a widely-read academic blog, and a Nature Reviews commentary.

**Citation.** Frontiers in Education (Oct 2025): https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/education/articles/10.3389/feduc.2025.1719625/full | Nature Reviews Psychology (Oct 2025): Richler, J. 'Common method bias can lead to spurious correlations in relationship studies.' Nat Rev Psychol 4, 681 (2025). https://doi.org/10.1038/s44159-025-00507-0 | Michelle Miller PhD Substack (Feb 2025): https://michellemillerphd.substack.com/p/cognitive-offloading-cognitive-abilities

**Their words.** “"existing models often rely on self-reported perceptions without examining underlying cognitive constructs such as critical thinking or reasoning ability"”

**We inherited this.** The original says: Appears to be the author's own assertion about a gap in the critical literature on Gerlich 2025.

**Does it change the conclusion.** This breaks the sentence. The methodological concern — that the correlation could be an artefact of common-method variance or construct overlap — has been raised in print. However, no published work has run a formal common-method bias test (e.g., Harman's single-factor test, marker-variable technique, or confirmatory factor analysis with a method factor) on Gerlich's actual dataset. So the piece's deeper implied point — that this specific threat to validity has not been rigorously quantified and published — survives. The sentence as written ('no published work questions') is broken; the stronger claim ('no published work has formally tested and ruled out common-method variance in Gerlich's data') would survive.
