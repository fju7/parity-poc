### CE-01 — role says BROKEN (HIGH confidence)

**We say.** Nobody has explained for a general reader that the evidence on AI and thinking splits by MODE OF USE — that experimental studies of AI as an instructional tool find improved learning while randomised trials of AI supplying answers find degraded unassisted performance — and that this distinction, rather than AI use as such, is what the evidence actually supports.

**What breaks it.** Multiple general-audience and semi-popular sources published before August 2026 explicitly articulate the mode-of-use split — tutoring/instructional AI improves learning, answer-giving AI degrades unassisted performance — using exactly the RCT evidence the claim says has never been explained to a general reader. The clearest single counterexample is a Substack post by Hana Lee Goldin (cardcatalogforlife.substack.com, May 26 2026) written explicitly for a general audience, which names the two modes, cites the relevant RCTs, and states the distinction as its central thesis. A second is The Conversation article (theconversation.com, ~August 2026) by the economists who built 'Macro Buddy', a general-audience outlet, whose headline is 'We designed an AI tutor that helps college students reason rather than give them answers' and whose conclusion is verbatim: 'The evidence from our experiment highlights a practical distinction: AI can be used to replace thinking, or it can be used to support it.' A third is the Stanford SCALE Initiative's public explainer (scale.stanford.edu, March 2026), written for educators and policymakers, which states that AI tools with pedagogical guardrails show better outcomes than 'general-purpose chatbots that provide answers directly.' A fourth is the aitoolsforkids.com guide (May 2026), written for parents, which labels the two modes 'tutor mode' and 'assistant mode,' cites three RCTs, and concludes 'assistant mode hurts, tutor mode helps.' A fifth is the Metaintro popular-science summary (May 2026) of the Liu et al. RCT series, which explains to a lay audience that 'how you prompt the model matters more than whether you use it at all.' The Bastani et al. PNAS paper (June 2025) itself received wide popular-science coverage (PsyPost, psypost.org, April 2026) that explained the mode split to a general audience in plain language.

**Citation.** https://cardcatalogforlife.substack.com/p/how-to-use-ai-to-make-us-smarter (Goldin, May 26 2026); https://theconversation.com/we-designed-an-ai-tutor-that-helps-college-students-reason-rather-than-give-them-answers-276584 (The Conversation, ~Aug 2026); https://scale.stanford.edu/research-in-action/understanding-evidence-base-ai-k12-education (Stanford SCALE, March 2026); https://www.aitoolsforkids.com/blog/ai-tutor-vs-assistant-for-kids (May 2026); https://www.metaintro.com/blog/ai-10-minutes-impairs-brain-cognition (May 2026); https://www.psypost.org/unrestricted-generative-ai-harms-high-school-math-learning-by-acting-as-a-crutch/ (PsyPost, April 2026); Bastani et al., PNAS 2025, doi:10.1073/pnas.2422633122; Liu et al., arXiv:2604.04721 (April 2026)

(The Conversation, theconversation.com); "AI tools designed with pedagogical guardrails … show more promising outcomes than general-purpose chatbots that provide answers directly." (Stanford SCALE, March 2026)”

**We inherited this.** The original says: The claim does not appear to restate a specific guideline or prior source verbatim; it reads as the author's own framing. However, the underlying empirical split it describes is accurately drawn from: (1) Bastani et al. (PNAS 2025) for the answer-giving/degraded-performance arm; (2) Kestin et al. (Scientific Reports, June 2025) and the Eedi/DeepMind RCT (Nov 2025) for the instructional-tool/improved-learning arm; and (3) Liu et al. (arXiv:2604.04721, April 2026) for the RCT series on persistence and unassisted performance. None of those sources contain the 'nobody has explained this' framing — that is the author's own addition and is the part that is broken.

**Does it change the conclusion.** This breaks the sentence but does NOT necessarily break the piece's broader conclusion. The underlying empirical claim — that the mode-of-use distinction is what the evidence actually supports — is correct and well-documented. What the sentence gets wrong is the sociological claim that nobody has explained this to a general reader: in fact, multiple general-audience outlets (The Conversation, Substack, PsyPost, parent-facing guides, Stanford's public explainer) had already made exactly this argument, some months before the piece was written. If the piece's purpose is to be the first to explain this distinction to a lay audience, that purpose is undermined. If the piece's purpose is to argue that the distinction matters and is underappreciated in mainstream discourse, the sentence needs to be narrowed: the distinction has been explained in specialist-adjacent and semi-popular venues, but may not yet have reached the highest-circulation general press (e.g. a New York Times front-page feature or a major magazine cover story). The sentence as written is a universal negative ('nobody') and is broken by any single counterexample.

VERDICT: broken
