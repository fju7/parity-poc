Do education programmes for children with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries work, and which kinds work best?

The short answer

Many programmes that were studied improved some of the skills they measured, but the studies are few, small and mostly of low confidence, and none of the three reviews on this page establishes which kind of programme works best. The earlier rapid evidence assessment gives examples of how its 'promising' evidence suggests interventions 'can be effective in improving the educational skills of primary-school-aged children with disabilities': 'computer-based interventions and modified teaching approaches' [2].

Nor does the evidence show that programmes in mainstream, inclusive settings work better than programmes in specialised ones: one review found no systematic differences between the two, though it notes this was likely because most interventions reported positive findings, and another included only studies in non-segregated settings, so could not make the comparison.

The most recent review

The most recent of the three reviews, a Campbell systematic review published in 2025, summarised the findings from 28 studies that evaluated interventions to improve the educational outcomes of people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries [1].

Its authors rated all but one of those studies as giving low confidence in their findings [1].

Its authors write that this lack of confidence was largely due to the use of low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1].

Most of the studies, 25, targeted children with disabilities; two targeted family members and three targeted service providers [1].

Interventions most often targeted people with intellectual or learning and developmental impairments, in 16 or 17 studies (the review reports both figures) [1].

The most common kind of intervention was educational attainment support, such as reading comprehension instruction for children with dyslexia, and the second most common was accessible learning environments, such as programmes to improve social skills or reduce victimisation in schools [1].

Most of the interventions targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1].

The review reports that none of its included studies looked at outcomes related to attendance, enrolment or completion [1].

Across 22 studies of skills for learning, the review's pooled effect was large, d = 2.10 (0.91 to 3.29), with high heterogeneity, I² = 99% [1]. The review also reports that, among the 18 studies included in its analysis of intervention effects on skills for learning, 12 interventions had a significant effect [1].

The review's abstract reports large effects on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy, each based on a low number of studies, and no significant effect on speech or school behaviour [1].

The review is not consistent on this point: a later passage calls the effects on cognitive skills, numeracy, and speech and communication insignificant [1].

Across studies, heterogeneity was high, and the review reports that the risk of publication bias varied but was frequently high [1].

Studies of the conditions for inclusion, such as teacher knowledge, showed a moderate pooled effect, d = 0.35 (0.12 to 0.58), across 4 studies [1].

The review did not find any systematic difference in effectiveness between interventions delivered in mainstream settings and those delivered in segregated or specialised settings, and its authors say this was likely because most interventions reported positive findings [1].

Its authors conclude that children with disabilities fall behind in educational outcomes because current school systems are not set up to teach children with different impairment types [1].

They also conclude that there is no one magic bullet intervention for this group [1].

They write that a twin-track approach is needed, which addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and also ensures that they are included in mainstream activities, for example through improving the skills of teachers and the accessibility of the classroom [1].

They call for evaluations of interventions that target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment [1].

They also write that more research is needed to understand which types of interventions are most efficacious, and how best to deliver them [1].

An earlier assessment

An earlier rapid evidence assessment, commissioned in advance of the Global Disability Summit in July 2018, included 24 primary studies and five systematic reviews with educational outcomes [2].

It judged that there is promising evidence that interventions are effective at improving the educational skills of primary-school-aged children with disabilities, but that better quality evidence is needed to make clearer judgements [2].

Of its primary-education studies, it judged 13 to be of low quality and the two remaining studies to be of moderate quality [2].

It also found promising evidence that school-level changes can be impactful [2]. The assessment notes, however, that only two studies assessed the effectiveness of school-level interventions [2].

It found the evidence insufficient for early intervention and for secondary education, and found none for lifelong and non-formal learning [2].

It concluded that more and better quality evidence is needed before specific approaches or programmes can be recommended [2].

Inclusive education programmes

A systematic review of inclusive education programmes in low- and lower-middle-income countries found only 31 studies that evaluated interventions and included 20 or more respondents [3].

Those studies covered 19 of the 84 low- and lower-middle-income countries the review searched for [3].

Of the 31 studies, only six had a pre and post design to evaluate the intervention, and four used a matched group [3].

Of six teacher training programmes, five improved what they targeted, such as teacher attitudes, self-efficacy, knowledge of disabilities or teaching strategies [3]. Of the four positive training studies that targeted teacher attitudes towards inclusion, the review's authors note that "these studies do not point to one instructional method or length of time" [3].

Two of those studies note that better attitudes did not translate into changed behaviour or intentions to adopt inclusive practices [3].

The review's authors conclude that adding small groups and individual instruction to inclusive large group instruction improved outcomes in low resource settings [3].
