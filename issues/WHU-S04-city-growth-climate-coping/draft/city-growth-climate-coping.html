What Holds Up — reliability-study issue WHU-S04, not for publication

Is the fast growth of cities a chance to cope better with a warming climate, or mainly added risk?

Study draft, 23 October 2026. Written from the documents listed in sources.json; every sentence is bound to a passage or declared a judgement.

The short answer

It is both, according to the main scientific assessment, but the measurements held here mostly show the risk. The IPCC's 2022 assessment of climate impacts and adaptation says that interactions between changing urban form, exposure and vulnerability can create climate change-induced risks and losses for cities and settlements, and that the global trend of urbanisation also offers a critical opportunity in the near-term to advance climate resilient development (high confidence). The studies held here that measure what has actually happened find that urban growth has added exposure: settlements in the most hazardous flood zones grew faster than settlements overall, and most of the rise in urban exposure to extreme heat came from population growth. Whether growth adds risk depends on where and how it happens: the same flood data show recent growth in high-income countries to have been relatively slow and safe.

What people have been told

Down To Earth, reporting a 2023 World Bank study, wrote that human settlement in the world's riskiest flood zones increased 122 per cent since 1985, making a lot more people vulnerable to water disasters from climate change. A 2016 World Bank blog put the opposite case: many countries are still in the early stages of the urbanization process, meaning they have a unique opportunity to develop sustainable cities right from the beginning. The two pieces take opposite sides, although the Down To Earth report also quotes a study author saying that smart planning can prevent some of those moving into the riskiest places.

Growth has been adding exposure

The IPCC's chapter on cities finds that urbanisation processes generate vulnerability and exposure which combine with climate change hazards to drive urban risk and impacts (high confidence). It finds that the most rapid growth in urban vulnerability and exposure has been in cities and settlements where adaptive capacity is limited, especially in unplanned and informal settlements in low- and middle-income nations and in smaller and medium-sized urban centres (high confidence). Between 2015 and 2020, urban populations globally grew by more than 397 million people, with more than 90% of this growth taking place in less developed regions. An additional 2.5 billion people are projected to be living in urban areas by 2050, with up to 90% of this increase concentrated in the regions of Asia and Africa.

A World Bank study of satellite data, in its authors' 2022 preprint, found that settlements across the world grew by 85 percent between 1985 and 2015, while settlements exposed to the highest flood hazard level increased by 122 percent. The published paper reports that in East Asia high-hazard settlements have expanded 60% faster than flood-safe settlements. In the preprint, 82 percent of the 36,500 square kilometers of settlements built in the world's highest-risk zones since 1985 are in low- and middle-income countries. Its authors explain that as safe spaces are increasingly occupied, the resulting land scarcity can drive new developments disproportionately into previously avoided areas such as riverbeds, flood plains, or wetlands.

A study of 13,115 urban settlements measured their population exposure to extreme heat from 1983 to 2016. Global exposure increased 199% over those 34 years. Population growth contributed 66% of the annual rate of increase in exposure, and total urban warming 34%. A 2015 global assessment estimated that the population of the low-elevation coastal zone would grow from 625 million in 2000 to between 879 million and 949 million people in 2030, with Africa expected to experience the highest rates of population growth and urbanisation in the coastal zone.

The opportunity, and whether it is being taken

The IPCC says rapid global urbanisation offers opportunities for climate resilient development in diverse contexts from rural and informal settlements to large metropolitan areas (high confidence). It also warns that dominant models of energy intensive and market-led urbanisation, insufficient and misaligned finance and a predominant focus on grey infrastructure in the absence of integration with ecological and social approaches risk missing opportunities for adaptation and locking in maladaptation (high confidence).

The flood study shows that growth need not add exposure at the same pace everywhere. Its authors find growth relatively safe in high-income countries and risky growth especially rapid in middle-income countries, and warn that low-income countries risk following their trajectory without risk-informed spatial planning.

The IPCC finds limited evidence of investment in the informal settlements hosting the most vulnerable urban residents (medium to high confidence). A review of informal settlements, home to approximately 1 billion urban dwellers, finds that some city governments have worked successfully with their inhabitants on upgrading programs to secure tenure, improve housing, install needed infrastructure, and provide public services. Its authors warn that in the absence of more effective policies, most of the world's growth in urban population will be accommodated in informal settlements. The IPCC's chapter on cities adds that limits to adaptation are often most pronounced in rapidly growing towns and cities and smaller settlements including those without dedicated local government.

What this means for a decision

For a planner or funder, the evidence held here supports treating how and where new urban growth is planned, rather than growth itself, as what decides whether it adds climate risk. The IPCC finds that the greatest gains in well-being can be achieved by prioritizing finance to reduce climate risk for low-income and marginalized residents including people living in informal settlements (high confidence). None of the sources held here measures how often planned growth has actually lowered a city's exposure; the contrast between countries in the flood study is the closest they come.
