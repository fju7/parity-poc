Study question WHU-S05 Evidence review

Does Going Hungry Make TB More Likely?

whatholdsup.org

For adults, yes: the best evidence says undernourished adults are about twice as likely to develop TB disease, and a randomised trial of food for household contacts cut TB cases. For children, the risk is uncertain.

The short answer

A 2024 Cochrane review asked whether undernutrition in adults, adolescents and children predicts later TB disease, and included retrospective and prospective cohort studies.

It included 51 cohort studies with over 27 million participants from the six WHO regions.

Pooled across 23 studies, undernutrition was associated with a hazard ratio for TB disease of 2.23 (95% CI 1.83 to 2.72), which the authors rated as low-certainty evidence because of risk of bias and inconsistency between studies.

For studies with less than ten years of follow-up, the estimate was 2.02 (95% CI 1.74 to 2.34) across 22 studies, rated as moderate certainty.

The review's conclusion is that undernutrition probably increases the risk of TB two-fold in the short term.

Adults: the thinner, the higher the risk

In a Korean cohort of 11,135,332 people who attended health screening in 2010, 52,615 developed active TB during follow-up.

Underweight adults (BMI below 18.5) had an adjusted hazard ratio for TB of 2.08 (95% CI 2.02 to 2.15) compared with adults of normal weight.

The risk rose with the degree of thinness: adjusted hazard ratios of 1.98 for mild, 2.50 for moderate and 2.83 for severe thinness.

Across the BMI range of 15 to 30, the authors found a log-linear inverse relationship between TB incidence and BMI.

The trial that gave people food

The RATIONS trial, in four districts of Jharkhand, India, enrolled the household contacts of 2800 patients with microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB and randomised 28 TB units to two groups.

Patients in both groups received food rations, but only household contacts in the intervention group received monthly food rations and micronutrients (750 kcal and 23 grams of protein per day).

Of the 10,345 household contacts, 34% (3543) had undernutrition at baseline.

Over follow-up, 122 contacts in the control group and 96 in the intervention group developed TB.

The adjusted incidence rate ratio for TB of all forms was 0.61 (95% CI 0.43 to 0.85), and for microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB it was 0.52 (95% CI 0.35 to 0.79).

The authors describe this as a relative reduction in TB incidence of 39% (all forms) to 48% (microbiologically confirmed pulmonary TB).

The trial was open-label: the investigators, the field staff and the participants were not masked, although incident TB was evaluated by government health providers who were not associated with the trial team.

The authors also say the effect size might not be the same in communities with lower prevalence of undernutrition and deprivation.

The Lancet has since published a notice that the article's appendix was corrected as of July 25, 2024.

Children: too few studies to say

Most studies in the Cochrane review were in adults: four were in children and three in children and adults combined.

The review's plain-language summary says its results apply to the general adult population, and that the risk for other groups, such as adolescents and children, is uncertain.

Its discussion adds that few studies assessed the risk of undernutrition in children, and that their results were inconsistent.

In a cohort of household contacts in Lima, Peru, 226 (1.8%) of the 12,648 contacts analysed were underweight.

Being underweight carried an adjusted hazard ratio for TB disease of 1.42 (95% CI 0.74 to 2.74; p = 0.29) compared with normal weight.

The same study found that being overweight lowered the risk of TB disease from age 12 upwards, but not in children under 12.

In the RATIONS trial, the effect of food among contacts who were underweight at baseline approached statistical significance overall, and was significant in adults who were underweight at baseline when the analysis was stratified by age group and nutritional status.

What holds up

For adults, the sources support the belief: undernutrition probably about doubles the risk of TB disease, the risk climbs with the degree of thinness, and a randomised trial found fewer TB cases among household contacts who were given food.

For children, these sources do not settle it either way: the review itself calls the risk for children uncertain, and in the household-contact cohort we read, 226 of 12,648 contacts were underweight, with a confidence interval for their hazard ratio running from 0.74 to 2.74.
