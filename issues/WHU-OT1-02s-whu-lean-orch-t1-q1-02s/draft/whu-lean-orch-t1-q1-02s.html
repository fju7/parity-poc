Do education programmes for children and young people with disabilities work in low- and middle-income countries?

The question

The question is: “In low- and middle-income countries, do programmes meant to improve education for children and young people with disabilities work, and which kinds work best?”

The short answer

A 2025 Campbell systematic review concludes that a range of individual-level interventions work to improve educational outcomes among people with disabilities in low- and middle-income countries (LMICs), but there is little research on systemic or school-level change [1]. The review's authors describe the evidence as providing "emerging support" for the efficacy and effectiveness of these interventions [1]. The effects they found on literacy, cognitive skills, handwriting and numeracy are significant and large, but they rest on a low number of studies [1]. The interventions and outcome measures differed across studies, which made direct comparison difficult, so the review does not establish a single best kind of programme [1]. The review covers people with disabilities generally rather than only children and young people [1].

What the reviews and evidence maps found

Why the question matters

People with disabilities fall behind their peers without disabilities in enrolment, retention and completion of education [1]. The review reports that evidence suggests the gap in educational attainment between people with and without disabilities is greatest in LMICs [1]. Before this work, the review notes, which interventions are actually effective had not been established, despite international directives on the right to education [1]. According to the source, all but one study received an overall rating of low confidence in study findings [1]. According to the source, a twin-track approach is needed, which both addresses the specific needs of children with disabilities and ensures that they are included in mainstream activities (e.g., through improving the skills of teachers and accessibility of the classroom) [1]. According to the systematic review, currently most interventions included in it targeted individual children with disabilities in an attempt to improve their functioning, skills, and competencies, but did not focus on mainstreaming these children into the school by system-level or school-level changes [1]. The source states: "Consequently, a focus on evaluation of interventions which target not just the individual with a disability but also their broader environment, are needed" [1]. The source states that the EGM it summarizes "provides a starting point" for researchers, decision makers and programme managers to access the available research evidence on the effectiveness of interventions for people with disabilities in LMICs, in order to guide policy and programme activity and encourage a more strategic, policy-oriented approach to setting the future research agenda [2]. According to the source, "This report illustrates the critical value of evaluating and publishing evidence from disability inclusive TPD interventions in LMICs, including any that are ongoing, or are components of highly resource intensive large‐scale education sector programs" [4]. The source, "Teacher professional development for disability inclusion in low‐ and middle‐income Asia‐Pacific countries: An evidence and gap map", identified fifty studies from 16 of the 41 LMICs in the Asia‐Pacific region, found that only three studies reported interventions supporting mental health among students with a disability, and concluded that more rigorous studies with larger samples and higher evidence quality are still required [4].

Effects by type of intervention

Of the 18 studies analysed for effects on 'Skills for learning', 12 interventions had a significant effect [1]. Studies concerned with 'Conditions for inclusion of people with disabilities in education' showed a moderately significant effect [1]. One study concerned with teacher knowledge showed a significant effect size [1]. The second most common category of intervention was 'Accessible learning environments', such as programmes aiming to improve social skills or to reduce victimisation of children with disabilities in schools [1]. The review asked which interventions appear most effective for different types of disability [1]. The excerpts used for this page do not report its answer to that question.

What kinds of programmes have been studied

Most interventions in the 2025 review tried to improve children's skills and did not focus on system-level or school-level changes [1]. An earlier evidence and gap map of interventions for people with disabilities in LMICs, which covers welfare broadly and not only education, likewise found that studies focus on 'fixing individuals', with fewer addressing infrastructure or institutions [2]. That map's authors call the absence of evidence on what works to promote disability inclusion 'a very striking gap' [2]. A 2022 evidence and gap map of teacher professional development for disability inclusion in LMICs of the Asia-Pacific region found 50 studies, with evidence from only 16 of 41 countries [4]. In that map, a significant number of interventions focus on changing teacher attitudes and understanding of disability, while only three studies address the mental health of students with a disability [4]. The same map found no studies on how teachers could support positive student behaviour [4]. Some studies in the map are qualitative; in one, from Vietnam, teachers reported that training seminars and workshops improved the general quality of teaching in their schools [4].

Limits of the evidence

None of the studies in the 2025 review were undertaken in humanitarian settings, and few disaggregated their findings by gender [1]. Its authors call for studies with larger samples, more rigorous designs and standardised measures [1]. The broader evidence and gap map reports a significant gap in study quality, especially for impact evaluations [2]. In that map, over half of the impact evaluations came from upper-middle income countries, with only nine from low-income countries [2]. The teacher-training map found no interventions supporting students with disabilities during emergency or crisis situations [4]. The studies concerned with speech and school behaviour show no significant effect of intervention [1]. Across the studies, heterogeneity is high, and the risk of publication bias varies but was frequently high [1]. All but one of the included studies were rated low confidence in their findings, so the evidence on whether these programmes work is weak [1]. The review found that the lack of confidence across studies was largely due to low-rigour study designs and was not always reflective of multiple points of weakness within a given study [1]. A twin-track approach is needed, one that meets children with disabilities' specific needs and also includes them in mainstream activities, for example by improving teachers' skills and classroom accessibility [1]. Most interventions in this systematic review targeted individual children with disabilities to improve their functioning, skills and competencies, rather than mainstreaming them through school-level or system-level changes [1]. Only three studies reported interventions aimed at supporting mental health among students with a disability, so evidence on this type of intervention is very thin [4].

What holds up

The clearest support is for individual-level programmes that build skills such as literacy, numeracy and cognitive skills, where the review found large, significant effects [1]. Even that support is described as emerging and rests on few studies [1]. Programmes that change schools, systems or institutions have been studied far less, and the few studies, on conditions for inclusion and on teacher knowledge, showed some positive effects, but the evidence remains thin [1, 2]. This is a gap in research, not evidence that such programmes fail, and the review's authors say more systemic approaches are needed [1]. Because interventions and outcome measures varied so much, the sources do not support ranking one kind of programme as the best [1].
